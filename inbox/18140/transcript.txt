Q1 Fleur Anderson: The Foreign Affairs Committee has been following very closely the situation in Cuba, and especially the impact on the citizens of Cuba, so we are really delighted that you could come to this sitting and that it is public so that more will be known—I hope—by the British people about what is happening in Cuba right now. I am Fleur, a Labour member of the Foreign Affairs Committee, and my questions are for Ruaridh and Katrin. What is the current situation? How stable is the current Cuban Government? Are there signs of internal political fracturing? What are the most plausible scenarios over the next 12 months—what is most likely to happen?

Ruaridh Nicoll: Your questions are straightforward, but do you want me to talk a little bit about the current situation on the streets for people in Cuba? Would that be helpful?

Fleur Anderson: Yes, very.

Chair: I believe you are living in Havana at the moment, so you have that insight. We are very interested to know what the short-term future is for Cuba, and what is happening on the streets is an important part of that, I imagine. We will be asking detailed questions about the humanitarian situation as well—don’t worry, we will have an opportunity to talk about it all—but I suppose we are really beginning with the politics.

Ruaridh Nicoll: Okay. It’s very difficult to talk without the politics, because about 40% of the Cuban population now have absolutely nothing. They are often elderly, they have no one abroad and they are trying to live on pensions or state salaries reduced by hyperinflation to the equivalent of £5 a month. I see them on the street all the time. Some of them are trying to maintain their dignity—they try to sell you knick-knacks from their houses—or they just straightforwardly beg or go through the huge piles of rubbish that have appeared. Politically, these are often the people who built the revolution, having listened to Che Guevara’s great plea—injunction—to give up their personal ambitions in return for cradle-to-grave healthcare, work, a roof over their heads and a ration of food. But the bodegas where they used to get those rations of rice, cooking oil, chicken and sugar have emptied. These people are being forgotten. They are relying on a very faltering family assistance system and church food banks.

Another 40% or so get money from family abroad, and 20% of the population either work for or own MIPYMEs—micro, small and medium-sized enterprises, which in 2021 were allowed by the Government, who were dealing with hyperinflation and trying to change the economy. Those people—the owners—are becoming incredibly rich. There are a lot of them. I have seen my first Range Rover and my first Porsche, and you see a lot of shiny Dodge Rams and BMWs. They support, with their workers, about 20% of the population. They import a lot of food, but they also do a lot of other things—for example, they now organise intercity transport, and companies are opening up that look after the elderly. But the 40% are in real trouble. The Government, who are struggling under this eight or nine-month-old oil blockade, struggle to do anything for them.

I have covered countries all across the world, and the Cuban Government remain the most opaque Government I have ever dealt with. After nine years of living here full time, if anyone tells me—whether it be a diplomat, a businessperson or a journalist—that they know what is happening within the Government, I would be very wary of believing them. However, the Government are clearly now quite factional. There have been huge attempts by the US Government to break them open, and other people support those super-rich new MIPYMEs—

Chair: Sorry to interrupt, but what does MIPYME mean?

Ruaridh Nicoll: MIPYMEs is the Spanish for small to medium-sized enterprises, small businesses that have grown very large—

Chair: So a bit of capitalism was injected in a few years ago.

Ruaridh Nicoll: No—as a mainland European diplomat said to me, at the moment we are in perestroika without glasnost. There is very little transparency in what is going on.

I should also mention that 2 million people have moved abroad since 2021. I have a four-year-old son, who is half Cuban, and the old people approach him on the street—they are like zombies—because they are aching for children. Their own are abroad and, to quote a friend, they are watching their grandchildren grow up on screen, often speaking another language.

Doctors are on state salaries, so if you go to a hospital, you have to take a present—a regalito—to get you through the door. Most people do not feel bad about that, because they understand the situation. The same goes for teachers—you need to start paying teachers. The education system is collapsing. As you know—I am sure many of you have visited Cuba—education used to be paramount, but many people now dismiss it. When you finish school, you face a choice: seven years at university—to train to be a doctor to earn only £5 a month—or go to work for a MIPYME and earn 20 times that immediately. It is a very easy decision. To quote another panellist—Katrin, whom I know well—the feeling is almost anti-intellectual now.

There are piles of rubbish everywhere, and communities are beginning to organise. They pay MIPYMEs to get rid of it. My wife’s 95-year-old grandfather has a pile outside his house so high that I can barely get to the door. The flies are terrible. Crime is burgeoning—Cuba used to be one of the safest places anywhere, and in 2022 The Telegraph listed it as the best Caribbean island to visit. That is only a few years ago. I do not want to overstate it—it is not Rio—but muggings and house and car break-ins are commonplace. Last week, the famous singer Ray Fernández was violently attacked outside his house.

There are, of course, the blackouts. Last week, a friend in Oriente—at the other end of the island; as you will know, Cuba is the length of Britain—said that people do not even talk about electricity any more, because there is so little of it. It is like any other very poor country, he said, but of course it didn’t used to be like that. The banging of pots and pans, which you may have heard about, has stopped in Havana.

I want to talk about one last thing. Water is now so irregular that a new breed of street hawker has appeared selling from big tanks that they push on trolleys. As you can imagine, that is potentially catastrophic. That brings me to the largest fear here, which is disease. The classics are all enjoying a resurgence—dengue, which I am currently recovering from for the second time, Zika, and chikungunya, which means “contorted” in Tanzanian Makonde and is as much fun as it sounds—but now we have waterborne nightmares. Hepatitis A is everywhere, diarrhoea is common, dysentery is about, and I think cholera is an increasing possibility. I wonder whether an outbreak of something that really kills people will bring this hellish year, and potentially this revolution, to an end. I will let Katrin take over on the politics—

Chair: Actually, Rory, given that you have begun with a very powerful testimony, with the agreement of the Committee we will move on to the humanitarian situation, and then come back to the politics afterwards, not least because Lena needs to leave in half an hour.

Q2 Fleur Anderson: Katrin, we will come back to you. Lena, what is the current humanitarian situation, what is your access, and what sectors are the most under strain? We heard about the health, education and water impacts. What are the numbers behind it for the World Food Programme? What are you doing and what is the future looking like for the humanitarian programme? Also, where are you getting most of your funding from? Are you getting enough funding from the international community?

Lena Savelli: The World Food Programme has been present in Cuba since 1963. We have an office in Havana and we have people placed in the provinces as well. We are working right now with a programme that has about $35 million a year, and we have so far been able to reach 1.5 million people in 2026. To echo what the previous speaker said, it is clear that the humanitarian situation is worsening. This, of course, is shaped both by long-standing structural challenges and by external pressures—the economic decline, the hyperinflation, which was mentioned, the very severe fuel shortages and the power cuts, which are of course impacting food security.

The World Food Programme focuses on all dimensions of food security. On food availability, Cuba normally imports about 80% to 85% of its food, and right now is challenged in doing so. The local production—the domestic production—is of course also constrained. You need fuel to run irrigation systems, to operate tractors and so on, so food is increasingly scarce and increasingly expensive. The social protection system is under pressure, obviously. The libreta system, which used to provide food commodities for the whole population under a universal social protection scheme, is now really moving to targeted assistance only for vulnerable people, as it is not able to provide sufficient food for everybody.

When I was there in June, I heard that only rice was being distributed through the libreta system at that time—no oil, no chicken, and none of the other commodities that were mentioned by the previous speaker. This is, of course, hitting those who are the most vulnerable—elderly people who rely on a very small pension of about 4,000 pesos, children, women who are pregnant and breastfeeding, and people with disabilities or chronic illnesses.

In July, the authorities had identified around 876,000 vulnerable people that they said were in particular need of support, and 94% of them reported food insecurity and the use of negative coping strategies, meaning that they start cutting down meals, are eating a poorer diet, or start selling off their assets to try to get money, because people are increasingly relying on private markets. Of course, a lot of the food there is out of reach economically for people who rely on state salaries or have pensions. For example, with about 6,000 pesos, which is an average salary, you could buy a carton of eggs and a bottle of oil—for the whole month. That is obviously insufficient for a family.

We are also seeing that supply chains are extremely concerned, with containers of food, medicines and other essential goods reportedly stranded in nearby ports and coming into the country on late schedules—

Q3 Chair: Sorry to interrupt you, but is the World Food Programme responsible for co-ordinating food coming into the country, or do you send food in directly yourself? What is your direct experience as an organisation of getting food to the country but not being able to distribute it within the country?

Lena Savelli: It is challenging to get the food into the country, even though humanitarian food is exempt from sanctions. Over-compliance with regulations sometimes means that shipping disruptions occur and there are severe delays getting the food in. Once it is in the country, of course you need to distribute it out to the provinces, which is more challenging these days given the fuel shortages.

We do continue to operate; like I said, the World Food Programme has already reach 1.5 million people with food assistance this year, and we are also providing fuel and logistic services for other UN agencies and humanitarian organisations in the country to ensure that we reach them. But it is slower, more difficult and more expensive to do so these days.

Q4 Chair: Which parts of Cuba are suffering the most from not being able to get food into their region or area?

Lena Savelli: All across the country, the challenges are great. Eastern Cuba is still recovering from Hurricane Melissa last year, which was a terrible blow to people and their food security. It destroyed 200,000-plus homes, schools and health institutions. From what we see in our monitoring, Camagüey and Villa Clara are other areas that we understand are increasingly in need. Like I said, we have people based in the provinces who continue to go out and monitor the work that we do there. Those are some areas we are particularly concerned about.

You asked about our support; as I mentioned, the World Food Programme’s programme is about $35 million a year. Right now, we are facing a gap of about $10 million for this year. We have a broad range of partners, including the Republic of Korea, Sweden and the United Kingdom, who supported us with the response to Hurricane Melissa last year—thank you for that—as well as Canada, Russia, Italy, Luxembourg, the European Union ECHO, Spain and many others. They are working with us to get support to the families in need. So we are continuing to operate, but it is more challenging these days given the constraints on both getting the food into the country and distributing it there.

For families, the power cuts are also impacting their ability to keep the food safe. Storing food in refrigerators when you have a 50-hour power cut is obviously a health concern. A comment was also made about water being increasingly scarce; that of course has many health implications and impacts food safety. We are seeing families increasingly trying to make food at home; the most recent thing I discussed with my colleagues in Cuba was how they are now starting to make home-made cooking oil. There are also food safety concerns when you are trying to produce food like that at home.

I think those were the main questions. In terms of the future, right now we do not see any particular change to the situation in the short term. Unless there is any change in the fuel access and the ability to import food, we expect to see a significant increase in humanitarian need and people needing support. We have been urging partners to use diplomatic channels to support humanitarian exemptions, facilitate humanitarian imports and encourage co-operation. That is aimed at protecting the most vulnerable people—the elderly, the women and the children, as I mentioned earlier.

The availability of resources and sufficient funding to cover the $10 million gap that I mentioned is incredibly important, particularly this year because, like many countries in the world, Cuba is already feeling the effects of El Niño, with increasing drought across the country, which of course will also impact domestic food production and hence the food security of the Cuban people. So there is a need to strengthen preparedness and contingency planning in anticipation of the impact of El Niño.

One positive thing this year is that the hurricane season has been calmer than last year. We have not had a major incident like Hurricane Melissa, which occurred almost a year ago to the day, and I think the El Niño conditions will keep it like that. But of course the drought and then potentially stronger storms next year is what we are really seeing on the horizon.

Q5 Chair: I have heard you say that what the World Food Programme would like from countries—I assume that includes Britain—is assistance to make it easier to get humanitarian aid into Cuba, and to stop the red tape and facilitate the easier movement of aid to the most vulnerable. Is Britain giving any assistance to the World Food Programme at the moment to assist Cuba? If not, should it?

Lena Savelli: The £1 million of support that we received after Hurricane Melissa last year has been implemented over the past few months. There is an open call for resources to continue to support the people in Cuba, but we received that generous contribution from the UK last year.

Like I said, we still have a $10 million gap, so more resources are of course urged from all our partners, not only for the provision of food but to support what the WFP is doing to facilitate logistics services, including the provision of fuel for other agencies, because obviously it is also challenging in the health sector, in water and in sanitation, as we discussed. It is important that we continue to be able to support all the needs of the Cuban population, not just in respect of food.

Chair: We have been joined by Dr Sabatini; thank you very much for coming in. A very powerful picture of the humanitarian situation is being painted for us at the moment. Lena Savelli has to go in 10 minutes or so. We have reordered the questions so that we can incorporate her evidence. Fleur, do you have another question?

Q6 Fleur Anderson: Is the World Food Programme able to bring in oil for humanitarian purposes? What change would enable more ability to distribute and use oil for humanitarian purposes? Can there be differentiation, or do you have that already?

Lena Savelli: We are bringing in food, oil, rice and other commodities—

Fleur Anderson: I mean fuel oil. Do you differentiate between fuel coming in for you to use for humanitarian purposes? Or is it all stopped at the moment by the blockade? Could that be changed?

Lena Savelli: We have been able to purchase fuel from private suppliers that have brought it into Cuba so that we are able to use it for humanitarian operations. Like the previous speaker said, in some cases the growing number of private sector actors in Cuba are able to get exemptions to fuel, and we have been able to purchase fuel from them, but it is not regularly available across the country. It is more pricey and more complicated to access this to keep the operations going.

Q7 Chair: There is so much to say on the humanitarian situation; may I ask Dr Hansing about it? Also, is it true that the infant mortality rate has doubled in the last few years?

Dr Hansing: Thank you so much for this opportunity; this is a very important conversation. Ruaridh and Lena gave a wonderful on-the-ground overview of the situation, which I would say is no longer a humanitarian crisis, but a disaster. The situation has been going on for a while, but since January this year, when the United States imposed an oil blockade, it has spiralled downwards. The lack of fuel really is what is making daily life so complicated for everybody.

We have heard about some vulnerable groups, and I would add that Afro-Cubans in particular—people of African descent in Cuba—are also very affected for historical and structural reasons. Because most of them do not have family abroad, they do not receive remittances, so we need to keep an eye on that group.

The crisis—the disaster—that we are facing is for most people an everyday survival situation. The lack of fuel means less or very little access to not just food, but water, public transportation, hospitals and the education system. All the infrastructure that the Cuban state used to provide, usually for free or very subsidised, has imploded—the social and the rest of the infrastructure. Most Cubans are now trying to survive on their own, which is psychologically a huge change. If you have been living your whole life in a country that has provided most services, and you are now in a survival situation and have to figure it out on your own, it is very difficult.

Cubans are dealing with what many people in my field call a polycrisis—economic, political and social, but also psychological. It is important to remember how people are doing in all this. Cubans feel abandoned by their own state. Many do not have family abroad, and the elderly population is in a very difficult situation. I will give you an anecdote—this is something that I hear almost every week. Many elderly people have been left by their children and grandchildren, who have emigrated in the last five years. Because of the power cuts, if they want to go to the bathroom at night, they fall, they break their hip and there is no help. That is an everyday situation. There is no public transport; there are no ambulances. It really is a crisis situation.

Of course, the central problem is that the United States and Cuba remain locked in a prolonged confrontation. It is really a stalemate situation. There has been talk of dialogue and talks, but we are really in a stalemate. This is contributing every single day to the worsening of the crisis on the ground. The Cuban people on the ground are paying an enormous price for this political deadlock. Both sides—the US and Cuban Governments—are entrenched in their positions, and every day that goes by is a day that more and more Cubans are not only suffering but dying.

I cannot emphasise enough how critical the situation is on the ground. We unfortunately do not hear enough about it in the media any more because there is so much else going on in the world, but the situation is really critical, and it can go in different directions. We are at a point now where it is very difficult to know what is going to happen. There are multiple scenarios, none of which is particularly good. That is why humanitarian assistance is critical. We need to bring in aid for most of these vulnerable populations quickly and in a co-ordinated way on an international level. What is happening now is that it is trickling in. Different countries—Mexico, the EU, Canada, Chile and so on—are sending aid in, but it is not co-ordinated. It is dripping in here and there.

We then have this massive problem, as Lena explained, around distribution because of the lack of fuel, but also because of the bureaucracy because of the US embargo. We are dealing with a very complicated logistical issue. We really need to address how to quickly organise an international co-ordinated humanitarian effort to get away from individual donors, countries or organisations helping and instead be more co-ordinated and work together, and put pressure on both the US and Cuban Governments to alleviate some of the logistical and bureaucratic obstacles.

Regarding your question about infant mortality, we have talked about some data and statistics, but it needs to be recognised that any data coming out of Cuba needs to be taken with a huge grain of salt. There is very little reliable data coming out of Cuba, because it is very difficult to do research in Cuba and the data that the Cuban Government give us is not the most trustworthy. We cannot really compare it to independent data. The data we have is probably underestimated. We are dealing with malnutrition on a large scale. You mentioned infant mortality, and there are numbers out there that suggest it has doubled. Maternal deaths have skyrocketed, particularly since the pandemic and over the last couple of years.

There is also extreme poverty. There are conservative estimates that more than 60% of the Cuban population is living in extreme poverty. We have these numbers, but some of the testimonies that we heard put an image to the numbers. I want to highlight that we really do not know how bad the situation is, but it is probably worse than the numbers that we have so far.

Chair: Thank you; that was very powerful. A couple of Members want to ask follow-up questions. I am going to Edward first and then to Sir John.

Q8 Edward Morello: Mr Nicoll, I want to follow up with you, on the ground, about some of the issues that have been highlighted in terms of access to fuel. Ms Savelli mentioned that the World Food Programme was able to purchase fuel from some of the SMEs that Mr Nicoll mentioned—the private company providers. There is an interesting report this week in a UK publication that alludes to the fact that the availability of fuel in Cuba is a result of American middleman companies effectively importing because they have exemptions to the sanctions. Is that what is happening on the ground? Is there a grain of truth to that? Is this a case of American exemptions to their own sanctions to allow American companies to make money importing fuel?

Ruaridh Nicoll: Yes, absolutely. There is a lot of fuel being imported from America now, albeit not on a scale that could affect the electricity blackouts, especially as it is designed so as to not get into the hands of the Government. But there is a lot of fuel. A lot of the MIPYMEs are importing container-sized pressurised fuel. There are stories of MIPYMEs taking over the state CUPET—a couple of state CUPET gas stations have now been taken over by MIPYMEs. This is where many people are buying their fuel. You can now get fuel for $2.50 a litre, which is expensive.

It is part of the American programme to allow a certain amount in so that the crisis bubbles but does not spin so far out of control that it is no longer handleable. There is also a huge amount of movement by the Cuban Government. You can import cars from the US, and they have far lesser import charges on electric cars, even though there are enormous blackouts. The famous old cars have disappeared, to be replaced by electric tricycles. It is shifting very fast. If you have the money, there is no real issue with fuel at the moment.

Q9 Edward Morello: To be clear, the exemptions from American sanctions are only for purchases from America or American companies.

Ruaridh Nicoll: We could talk more generally about this, but I will try not to because I know that we are on time. The American sanctions have pushed out every Canadian, Spanish and British company. They have pushed out Meliá and Iberostar, the big Spanish hotel companies, and Blue Diamond from Canada. They have pushed out Sherritt, the mining company. They have pushed out everybody.

Q10 Chair: The public watching this are probably also aware that there are no more flights. There used to be holiday flights to Cuba, and those stopped some time ago.

Ruaridh Nicoll: That is because of fuel. You have to refuel by then. Also, the situation is catastrophic: there are the reports of hepatitis in holiday zones and things like that. I went looking for tourists in Varadero, which is the famous resort. It took me a long time to find any, and they were pretty dodgy tourists.

Fuel is a very important thing. There is a fuel blockade, but it is related to the US trying to take control of all supplies into Cuba. However, there is, weirdly, a slackening going on for humanitarian aid. There are now ships: for a while, there were no ships from anywhere other than Miami, but there have been. There has been food. Lena might know more about this, but there is food and medicine coming from Jamaica and from Veracruz in Mexico, via Spain, and other ships have been allowed in. Two fuel ships have been arrested by the US on the way in, so clearly the Government here are trying to find black ships—if that is the right phrase—to bring in.

Q11 Edward Morello: Can I come back to the point you were making about foreign companies being forced out of Cuba? To finish your train of thought, are you suggesting that American companies are stepping in to fill the void left by those companies? Is that part of the design of the sanctions?

Ruaridh Nicoll: Yes, clearly. Donald Trump has made it very clear that he wants to open up Cuba for his Cuban American friends in Miami. He has literally said that several times. He has gone a bit quiet on Cuba, probably for a variety of reasons, but when I asked a senior American official why that was, he said, “Well, because it’s summer, Ruaridh.” I said, “Why summer?”, and he said, “He doesn’t go to Mar-a-Lago in summer, and that’s where he sees this.” We should start seeing more from him at the moment, but he wishes for Cuba to be opened up economically for Americans.

Sherritt is a very clear example. It is a big nickel miner. It left, and it looked like it had gone for good, but now there are signs that it is coming back, and it will come back with a deal agreed to by OFAC, the Washington Government organisation that allows licences. For a while, it looked like it was one of Trump’s former advisers who would be the business partner on that. That seems to have slipped away for a moment, but we are waiting to see who is allowed back. But yes, there has been a very clear shoving out of foreign countries and control taken by the US. That is very clear and is a stated policy ambition of the US.

Chair: That’s really shocking.

Q12 Sir John Whittingdale: All three of you have graphically described the appalling situation for ordinary people living in Cuba. My question is to you, Dr Hansing, and to Dr Sabatini, because it relates to Fleur’s original question. Who do the Cuban people blame, and therefore how secure are the Government of Cuba, given the appalling state ordinary people are finding themselves in?

Dr Hansing: Shall I take a shot at that? I think attitudes are changing as the situation changes. I would say that most people on the island are particularly angry at their own Government for not doing more for the citizens. There has been—this is interesting—a generational split in terms of the US pressure. You find that a lot of the young people who are still there—many have left, obviously—really hoped, particularly in the springtime and the early summer months, that the US would have some kind of intervention, military or otherwise. The logic was, “This reality we are living, this survival modus, will finally end.” People who grew up in the revolution in the ’60s, ’70s and ’80s, in a time with more economic stability, have a more nuanced view of the situation and are much more hopeful for a diplomatic solution, but they believe that both sides—the US Government and the Cuban Government—are equally responsible for this mess.

Of course, it is a long-term problem. We all know that Cuban-US relations have been quite dreadful for decades and that this is not coming out of nowhere; it has just now escalated downwards at a velocity like never before—maybe the only other comparison is in the 1960s—and that is really affecting Cubans on the ground. As I said, most Cubans feel abandoned by their own Government, particularly materially but also in terms of social services. That is really influencing those attitudes. There is a lot of anger from Cubans towards their Government. We saw a lot of people on the streets banging their pots and doing all that kind of stuff in their frustration and ire against their Government. But there is also, again, that generational situation. I think that is very important to take into consideration.

Dr Sabatini: To echo what Katrin said, most Cubans simply want change. They blame their Government for failing to respond to their needs, but however it comes, that is what they want: they are desperate. Most do not blame the United States necessarily; they would welcome some form of intervention for some sort of change; “Let it just happen now” is something you hear when you speak to people who have relatives inside the island. But that also speaks to one of the fundamental flaws of the entire strategy, which I will talk about later: it is intended to spark a popular rebellion against the regime like we saw in eastern Europe. Because of the mass exodus of youth—some of the most, if you will, entrepreneurial Cubans have left the island; Cubans are desperate—there is not going to be that popular rebellion.

Q13 Chair: Are people leaving now? If so, how?

Dr Sabatini: Mostly by boat, and they are now mostly going to Mexico. There are severe constraints on their ability to go to Florida, where they would normally go. Yes, they are leaving mostly by boats and flights overseas. There is a mass exodus. I heard a number the other day and I do not want to quote it. Probably Katrin and Lena know it better, but we are talking about several million Cubans who have left in the last couple of years.

Dr Hansing: Yes, 3 million have left in the last five years. The numbers have gone down because it is now more difficult to leave. For many years it was very easy to get to Nicaragua without a visa, and then people migrated through central America to the US border. All that has changed, obviously, in the last few years but people are still going wherever they can go. You have Cubans trying to go to Guyana, Brazil—wherever they can get to, they are leaving to—but the numbers have gone down because it is harder to get a visa for most countries.

Ruaridh Nicoll: Can I come in?

Chair: Lena has to go soon, so may I take her last contribution before she rushes off?

Lena Savelli: Thank you very much. Apologies for that. Since there was a comment about other food coming into the country, I want to mention that, apart from what the World Food Programme is doing within the framework of the UN response plan for Hurricane Melissa and the ongoing energy crisis, there are bilateral initiatives under way. Mexico has sent humanitarian assistance. Other countries of the region have done so too. The United States has also pledged $100 million and is working through civil society organisations and the Catholic Church in Cuba.

The Government have adopted a series of quite ambitious reform packages to open up the economy; in terms of food security, those include targeted social measures aimed at the most vulnerable groups and agricultural reforms such as proposed changes to land tenure and agricultural management. However, translating those policy measures into tangible economic and social outcomes will depend on resource availability and inputs and financing, so that will certainly take time. I wanted to mention those two things before, unfortunately, I have to disconnect.

Chair: Thank you very much for giving evidence to us, Lena. Ruaridh, I know you want to be involved in answering this question too.

Ruaridh Nicoll: To answer the previous question, the Miami Herald reported recently that $231.7 million-worth of fuel was imported from the US between January and August—I do not know how accurate that is, but that is what it says.
