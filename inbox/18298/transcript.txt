Examination of witness

Morgan McSweeney.

The Chair: Welcome back to this meeting of the House of Lords Constitution Committee. We are continuing to take evidence for the committee’s inquiry into the UK’s constitutional safeguards. We are now joined by Mr Morgan McSweeney, former chief of staff to Prime Minister Sir Keir Starmer. Mr McSweeney, you are very welcome. You are joining us remotely; I know that it is very early in the morning where you are, so we are particularly pleased that you have been able to join us. Thank you very much.

As you know, we are undertaking an inquiry into the constitutional safeguards in the United Kingdom. Obviously, your role as chief of staff was an important one in witnessing how this all worked in practice, and perhaps some of the vulnerabilities or strengths of how it all worked. So my initial question is: do you think that the UK’s constitutional democracy is under threat? If you do, then why? What is threatening to it? What did you witness at the heart of the operation in No. 10 Downing Street?

Morgan McSweeney: Constitutional democracies are always under pressure to demonstrate that they work for people. There are two ways that they can become under threat. The first is through abuse of power and the second is through a failure of democratically elected Governments to deliver on their lawful programmes. It is that second one that populists who want to undermine institutions exploit as a weakness. My view is that when Governments fail to deliver, it can undermine their legitimacy as much as it can when there have been abuses of power.

Lord Murphy of Torfaen: Are there any changes you would like to see to the United Kingdom constitution?

Morgan McSweeney: There are quite a lot of rules that govern the Prime Minister’s exercise of his or her power. Every single one of those individual rules, I am sure, has been put in place with the best intent, but they should be identified, there should be fewer of them and the ones that are there need to be justified. Ones that prevent the abuse of power should be kept and everything else needs to be looked at in the round—not just each individual one but the cumulative effect of all of them.

Then, there should be some codification of it, which could be a codification of the Cabinet Manual to make clear what the role of the Prime Minister is, what the role of the Cabinet is, what the role of Parliament is and what the role of the judiciary is. That is in the interest of citizens, of the media and certainly of those who exercise power. That is what I would like to see. There are a few other areas, such as how judicial reviews are applied and their impact on the effectiveness of government, but there should be a rounded look at things rather than picking each individual rule and seeing whether that one should remain, be reformed or change.

Lord Murphy of Torfaen: You make a very strong case, for example, for the Ministerial Code and the Cabinet code. Could the Ministerial Code be strengthened by making it statutory?

Morgan McSweeney: It could, because when the rules are just interpreted by individuals, there are limits to—I cannot remember exactly what phrase the civil servants use—“good chaps” and, essentially, a good faith way of interpreting the constitution. It leads to people having different interpretations. It leads to Ministers and Parliament being unsure exactly what the role of the Minister was and should be in an individual decision.

Before I would codify or make something statutory, I would have a wider review to clear out the thicket of rules that have been developed, often because a mistake happened and a Minister wanted to ensure that the mistake did not happen again, so a review body was set up or an independent process was added here or there, or a pause on decision-making was added. These things have accumulated over a long period. They are then applied through precedent rather than written-down rules, and they should be looked at in the round. Where there are rules that prevent Governments functioning at the speed they need to and delivering their lawful mandate or lawful programme of government then those rules should be removed.

Lord Beith: That poses the possibility of a Prime Minister saying, “Yes, I agree with you that there are two threats to our constitution. One is abuse of power, but the other is failure to deliver”, and then saying, “I prefer to deal with failure to deliver, because that’s a more urgent problem for us at the moment”. Is there anything you could do in your previous post to stop a Prime Minister taking that course?

Morgan McSweeney: Just to add to my previous point, the process of identifying and removing rules and codifying in whatever way should be decided by Parliament, not the Prime Minister. If a committee such as this made recommendations, it would be for Parliament to decide rather than the Prime Minister, because that is the appropriate place for it to be decided. Sorry, can you repeat your question, please?

Lord Beith: It was about a Prime Minister who says, “Yes, there are different threats to the constitution. I’m concerned about the threat to delivery, so I propose to imperil the constitution in some other respect for the more urgent need”.

Morgan McSweeney: There are constraints to prevent abuses of power, and it is important that those constraints remain in place. There are the courts and there is Parliament, which can reject and amend legislation. Cabinet can challenge or refuse to support and, ultimately, Cabinet members can resign. Civil servants can give strong written advice. Political parties can overthrow leaders and elections can remove Governments. So there are safeguards and it is important that those remain in place. Prime Ministers should not be able to usurp the powers of Parliament. They should not be able to usurp the powers of the judiciary, and they should not be able to abuse human rights. Those safeguards are important, and I am not arguing for the removal of any of those, but the safeguards that are in place need to be clear, need to be justified and need to enable both the prevention of abuse of power and the ability of Governments to deliver. I do not think they are in conflict.

Lord Beith: I will give you one simple example for which that does not work, and it is one that is currently about to be changed: the decision of a previous Government to require the Electoral Commission to work to a policy statement provided by the Government. That went through all those processes and survived them. In the end, of course, it is being changed by a succeeding Government.

Morgan McSweeney: I do not know much about the detail of that, but one thing that I saw happen was the way that quasi-judicial processes were applied. There would be legislation that said, “The Secretary of State shall decide”, and then I do not know whether it is because the legislation is too unclear about how a Secretary of State should make a decision or whether it is because of the interpretation of that legislation by the civil servants, but that meant that “The Secretary of State shall decide” removed the possibility of Ministers to decide or discuss it collectively, and for the Secretary of State to have an informed discussion or consultation, whether with the Prime Minister, at Cabinet level or at sub-Cabinet level. It prevents the Prime Minister from saying “It is for the Secretary of State to make a decision, but I’d like the decision to be quick and timely, because there’s a lot going on in the world”. There are some interpretations, either through its application or how the law was originally written, that slow the Government down on some pretty big issues that the country faces. I do not know about the Electoral Commission, I am afraid.

Lord Griffiths of Burry Port: Good morning. How far does our constitutional democracy depend on people in positions of power exercising self-restraint and choosing to abide by conventions? Does this make the constitution vulnerable?

There is a rider on the sheet of paper in front of me, which, in a sense, will put you on the spot. How do you ensure that those in positions of power possess the required character and good judgment? There. In Chicago, there must be an answer.

Morgan McSweeney: We cannot have a set of rules that are dependent on whether a Prime Minister is trustworthy or not. There are limits to how far, in any codification, a constitution could come in deciding that. The safeguards for the character of a leader and Prime Minister are to do with our parties. Our political parties have a constitutional role in that in the processes by which they choose their parliamentary candidates, the MPs themselves, and then, of course, the public decide through the ballot box who they want to form the Government. I do not think the character of the Prime Minister can be judged by codes. I would hope that the codes are strong enough to prevent a Prime Minister from abusing power and would enable a Prime Minister in delivering their lawful agenda.

Clarity helps enormously. Clarity helps the Prime Minister to understand, “This is the limit of my power”. They should know that to begin with; they should know what they can do to make government work as effectively as it can in the interests of the country, and not have to guess at that or ask a civil servant. Sometimes, civil servants will have slightly different views about what a Prime Minister can do and cannot do. I have certainly seen situations like that, which shows the limits of a lack of clarity, and I think that clarity will help.

If a Prime Minister wants to usurp the powers of Parliament or usurp the powers of Cabinet, it is not just that there are constitutional safeguards; there are political realities. A Cabinet that feels that a Prime Minister is abusing power will rebel against that Prime Minister. These are strong characters in Governments of all parties. So there are safeguards in place already. I think everyone would benefit from a clarity of exactly what those are, and I think that everyone would benefit from clearing out ones that are unnecessary.

Baroness Hamwee: Good morning—it seems to be getting light behind you now. You said before that rules will always be interpreted by individuals. When the interpretation goes one way, rather, perhaps, than the way that Parliament as a whole thinks it should, should we be looking to the House of Commons to act as a restraint?

Morgan McSweeney: Parliament has a big role in the constraint of prime ministerial powers, certainly. There are cases where, if legislation has been passed that is not as it was intended, that prevents Governments being able to make decisions at the right speed, Parliament has always got the ability to change and amend that legislation. If it feels that a Prime Minister is overstepping, it has a number of different ways, whether that is passing legislation, PMQs or committees. Then there is the role of political parties. So there are a number of different avenues where Parliament is able to challenge and prevent a Prime Minister from abusing power. It absolutely has an essential role, to answer your question.

Baroness Hamwee: In reality, one knows that one has to pick one’s battles. Sorry, there should have been a question mark at the end of that.

Morgan McSweeney: Sure, but Parliament has a lot of MPs, and different MPs will focus on different things. If a big enough group of MPs feel the Prime Minister is overstepping the mark, usurping Parliament’s proper place, then there is a place for a debate; there is a place to challenge that. That is appropriate. Again, I think that Parliament would probably benefit from understanding what exactly those rules, constraints and limits are, and what are the rules that govern how the Prime Minister exercises power.

The Chair: When you said that there may be some guidelines that should be cleared out, what sort of thing did you have in mind?

Morgan McSweeney: Rather than pick individual issues, I think it is about the accumulation of all of them. I think what happens from time to time in government is that the Government take a decision and it does not go quite the way it should do, or there is a problem with the decision, and then Ministers or Prime Ministers will announce a review, or an extra safeguard is put in place, without looking at the cumulative effect of all these safeguards. You could look at each individual rule and say, “That one is of itself justified”, but I think, actually, this is better looked at in the round, and I do not feel that I am qualified to give advice as to what are conventions, what is law, what is precedent and what is overinterpretation. I think it requires civil servants, or ex-civil servants, to help the committee with that.

One area I think ought to be looked at is this idea of quasi-judicial processes that prevent Secretaries of State from having conversations with their colleagues, other Cabinet Members, at Cabinet level, where it prevents the Prime Minister from looking for quicker decisions or from being involved in a conversation. I think that is an area that needs to be addressed, but I do not want to pick individual rules, because when you look at those individual rules, you can justify them. It is the cumulative total of a Prime Minister being unclear about which powers they can exercise at speed.

This country is facing multiple challenges and a very volatile world, and the public are looking to the Government to act in their interest, and act in its interest at speed. The public need to have confidence that the Government can meet these challenges, and that means not just meeting them, but meeting them at speed. Rules that prevent that and that are unnecessary should be cleared out. Like I said, I do not think any rule has been put in place for bad reasons; they have been put in place for good reasons. It is the cumulative effect of all of these rules that are not being added to some kind of code, they have just been added to precedent and what previous Prime Ministers did.

The Chair: That is very helpful. Lady Hamwee, I think I may have cut you off slightly too soon.

Baroness Hamwee: This is actually a slightly different question. Mr McSweeney, you mentioned judicial review at the start of this session in a way which suggested that you think that the possibility for the courts to come in is too extensive. We are being told, it seems to me, increasingly in Parliament, when we talk about reviews of things and appeals, “Well, there’s always judicial review”, but in fact judicial review is really very limited. What are your ideas about that?

Morgan McSweeney: I beg your pardon: I misspoke. I meant quasi-judicial processes. I meant processes that the Secretary of State should decide that then seem to prevent a wider conversation happening at Cabinet level—that is, a proper consultation with Cabinet colleagues. I beg your pardon; it is very early here, and I did misspeak.

Lord Waldegrave of North Hill: I want to bring you back to your subject of failure to deliver and to explain your ideas. We read the interview that you gave in September in which you said that the centre of government is “too weak”. And the Prime Minister was right to “make the cabinet secretary stronger in the centre”. I am interested in that. The Cabinet Office has expanded enormously in recent years, and in some sense that presumably means that more has gone to the centre. Anyway, I do not want to pre-empt you. How would you make the centre work better? You say it cannot think strategically and so on. What would you do?

Morgan McSweeney: Size and strength are different things. The state is misshapen in lots of ways. In some ways, Whitehall is too centralised. The state is big, but in places it is out of shape. It is a good idea for there to be more central controls around some central issues.

I will try to give you an example. The Government have a lot of communication teams and there are a lot of communication staff around government, but they are not centrally co-ordinated in any way. Therefore, important issues that the Government need the communications teams to work on are not worked on because each individual department is just addressing media queries about its own department. Some of that has to do with culture. It is not all to do with just adding more staff; in fact, you could have fewer staff. Some of it is to do with a sense that departments are almost independent of each other. That does not make sense. They need more co-ordination. Government needs coherence; it needs consistency of approach. It cannot be developing different ways of thinking that come into conflict with each other.

In terms of strategic thinking, my sense was that No. 10 is addressing multiple crises at any given time, and that developing more strategic functionality that thinks through some of the more longer-term, or certainly medium-term, challenges that are facing the country that can be inputted into government decisions like King’s Speeches and Budgets, would enhance the ability of Governments to plan further ahead on some of these challenges the country has.

Lord Waldegrave of North Hill: I agree with you on that, but that is partly because in 1971 I was working in a unit in the Cabinet Office that was supposed to do exactly that, but it got abolished later on—for better or worse.

Lord Bichard: I just want to explore, briefly, the role of the Civil Service. Sorry to be predictable as a former civil servant, but I just wonder whether you thought the Civil Service has a role or should have a role in protecting the Constitution, independent of the advice it gives to Ministers?

Morgan McSweeney: In what way are you thinking?

Lord Bichard: If there is a threat to a particular part of the constitution, should the Civil Service see itself as having a role to step in and seek to protect that? There may be all sorts of ways in which they could do that. I do not think I want to go and explore those at the moment. But the relationship between Ministers and the Civil Service is at the core of this, along with whether, sometimes, the Civil Service should see itself as a guardian of our constitution.

Morgan McSweeney: It should, and by large I think it does. It will give very clear advice if it thinks a Minister is doing something wrong. But I think it is important to distinguish between what happens if a civil servant thinks that a Minister is acting unlawfully, and what happens if a civil servant believes that a Prime Minister is unwisely breaking a convention. I think these are two very different things, and how a civil servant should act in each one of those situations is, I believe, different.

On the first one, there are a number of different routes they could go down. My experience of working in No.10 was that the two civil servants I most worked with were the Cabinet Secretary and the Prime Minister’s PPS, and they obviously talked to each other and had a very good relationship. They also had what they called “the golden triangle”, where they worked with the King’s PPS as well. So there are a number of different routes there where they can exercise their position.

But it is important to distinguish the difference between civil servants thinking a Prime Minister or Minister is breaking a law and thinking that they are interpreting precedent incorrectly. Ministers ought to be able to, and should, question the advice that is coming their way; if they do not agree with a civil servant’s interpretation of the rules or safeguards, they ought to be able to question those as well. That is important.

Lord Bichard: There is often tension where a senior civil servant appears not to be implementing the wishes of a politician. I am trying to get at that. Do you think that, where what you might call the entrenched constitutional principles are concerned, the Civil Service ought to be prepared to do that?

Morgan McSweeney: It depends on the nature of where they feel a Minister is getting it wrong. When it works well, it should not come to that level of conflict. When I was chief of staff, I would meet pretty regularly with the Cabinet Secretary to surface issues where I thought these conflicts might be happening or where the Prime Minister was feeling frustration within the system. The Cabinet Secretary could help to resolve this, or we could resolve this, and I would not need to wait for the weekly meeting—if I felt this was becoming more urgent, I would pick up the phone. Or vice versa, if a Cabinet Secretary felt a Minister or the Prime Minister was asking to do the wrong thing in a department, they would flag it with me, and we would try to resolve it. There are a number of informal routes before you get to that level of conflict, and it is important that those relationships work well and that those conversations are ongoing.

It is a job of a chief of staff to understand what their Prime Minister wants. Chiefs of staff should not be really surprised if a Prime Minister is frustrated with how a decision has been implemented. But before you get to that sort of standoff, there is scope for conversations and for ways of trying to resolve it. But in my view, there is a big difference between where a civil servant believes a Minister is breaking rules or breaking the law, and where a Minister thinks, “I do not agree with this convention. I do not agree with this precedent. I would like to change it, and if it is not unlawful, I am going to do it”. I think that civil servants have to sometimes be able to put things into place that they do not agree with, as long as it is lawful and within rules. Sorry, I know it is a bit of a vague answer, but there are distinct different challenges.

Lord Bichard: It was probably too vague a question.

Lord Jones of Penybont: Good morning. We are in a formal scenario here, Mr McSweeney, although you and I have known each other for a few years. When you were in Downing Street, did you ever think about what the constitutional implications of a decision might be? If so, what was the process that was followed in order to get to the point when it felt comfortable to take that decision?

Morgan McSweeney: The constitution and the rules play a big and constant role in the life of No. 10. When Ministers or Prime Ministers were taking decisions that could be constrained in any way by the constitution, you would talk to civil servants about what precisely the barrier was and whether there was a better or more effective way of taking the decision. Quasi-judicial processes, like I said, had a way of preventing necessary conversations between Cabinet members or Prime Ministers and Ministers, and they should be looked at again.

Lord Jones of Penybont: You mentioned quasi-judicial processes. What do you mean by that? You do not mean judicial review; you made that clear. What else do you mean?

Morgan McSweeney: There will be a piece of legislation that will say, “The Secretary of State shall decide” X or Y or Z. That is quite often interpreted as that the Secretary of State should make that decision but should make that decision without a conversation in Cabinet—without a conversation with their Cabinet colleagues. The advice would be that a Prime Minister cannot say to the Secretary of State, “I would like that decision to be taken at speed”. It is an important decision, and it is all left to one individual member, and I do not know if that is law or if that is application. There should be clarity about what exactly that means, so that it enables a decision, especially one that affects the economy at large or national security or a number of different departments. The other Cabinet members ought to be able to make their views known on something like that. It is still the decision of the individual Secretary of State. I do not think that is a problem. I think there is a problem when there are not conversations at Cabinet level or there is not the ability of a Prime Minister to chase.

I will give you another example. When Keir first became Prime Minister, he wanted to professionalise the management of Cabinet more. At the beginning of the year, he wanted to send out letters saying, “Here is what the priorities of your department should be in order for us to achieve what we want to achieve in government”, and he would then talk to individual Cabinet members to chase progress on those priorities. The advice at the start was that that was not convention. While individual Cabinet members need to run their departments, Prime Ministers need to co-ordinate and give their Cabinet direction, and that is good for government. That is an appropriate thing for a Prime Minister to do. It is not usurping the role of Cabinet; it is strengthening it. But that is another area where I think people would benefit from clarity. There is a role for a Prime Minister. It is not just a collection of individuals; it is a collective.

Lord Beith: Is it your view that the autonomy of Secretaries of State in the decision-making process is actually part of the constitution which should be changed, or is it simply a practice or interpretation which is not part of the constitution at all?

Morgan McSweeney: There are decisions where the law says the Secretary of State should take that decision. I do not think that should change. What I think should change is that, where those decisions impact on a number of different departments, and where there are bigger national interests, there ought to be the possibility of Cabinet conversations or conversations between the Prime Minister and that Secretary of State before the Secretary of State takes the decision. But I do not think that that element should change. The Secretary of State should still take the decision where the legislation says that.

Lord Beith: Most people believe that is what actually happens and is supposed to happen, but as you have vividly described, it is not like that.

Morgan McSweeney: Not for what are called quasi-judicial processes, no.

Lord Cryer: Good morning, Morgan. You said early on in your evidence that there are checks and balances. I think the two that you mentioned were the Cabinet and Parliament, which is true. The problem is that, in many ways, both have become weakened over quite a few decades. If you take incoming Labour Governments, until about 12 to 13 years ago, the shadow Cabinet or much of it was elected, so an incoming Labour Prime Minister had to appoint certain members of his Cabinet, because they had a power base in the PLP, and so that is why they were in the Cabinet. At the same time in Parliament, with all due respect to everybody at the other end of the Corridor, I think there has been a shift towards MPs becoming super-councillors. To use Burke’s phraseology, MPs are becoming more like delegates and less like representatives. So, when they are lobbied about an issue, they will think “Well, that is an important issue in my constituency. Therefore, I have to ask awkward questions or perhaps rebel. But if it is not an issue in my constituency, I might not necessarily be that bothered about it”. On those two fronts, it seems to me that the House of Commons is weaker than it was maybe 30 or 40 years ago. There might be nothing that can be done about it, and we are going to have to live with it, but do you think there is anything that we could do to strengthen those checks and balances?

Morgan McSweeney: I cannot say what the nature of it was 30 years ago, but my experience of the MPs I worked with is that they have a wide range of interests. On pretty much every single national issue, there were groups of MPs who had an interest and who were well informed, well read and who wanted to challenge or discuss. Conversations might happen well in advance of legislation and they should. A Minister who is bringing a decision to Parliament that is contentious will want to build parliamentary support for that, and they will find, in most cases, there are groups of MPs that are interested in it. We have an exceptionally high calibre of Members of Parliament with a wide range of skills and experience, and, yes, of course they have to raise issues about their own constituency, but quite a lot of them have bigger national interests too.

On the Cabinet, I think the political reality is still that a Prime Minister will need to include, in their Cabinet, Ministers who have got big support in the PLP, because the Prime Minister will want to pull together a Cabinet that commands the respect and the authority of the full PLP. The political realities of it are that we have got serious, smart people—the same is true in Cabinet—who will challenge and have the argument, sometimes behind closed doors or at Cabinet level.

When I think about the constraints on a Prime Minister, each one of them has a particular role—courts, Parliament, Cabinet, civil servants, political parties, and, ultimately, the public. No one of them can provide all the constraints on the Prime Minister themselves; each one of these parts matter and the collective matters too. But my experience of MPs is that we have got a very high calibre of well-informed, smart Members of Parliament who want to strengthen legislation, make government better and are interested in a wider range of issues. I cannot speak for other parties.

The Chair: We have come to the end of our session, and so I thank you very much indeed, Mr McSweeney. It has been a pleasure listening to you. It has been even more of a pleasure watching the dawn rising behind you, so I hope that means you will have a good day in Chicago. Thank you very much indeed for taking the trouble to talk to us.
