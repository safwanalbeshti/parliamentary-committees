Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Wes Streeting, Jeremy Pocklington and Air Marshal Tim Jones.

Q1 Chair: I call to order today’s House of Commons Defence Committee evidence session with the new Secretary of State. It is an absolute delight to have the right hon. Wes Streeting MP, the Secretary of State for Defence, for his first outing at the Defence Committee. It is a pleasure to have back with us Jeremy Pocklington, the permanent secretary at the Ministry of Defence. I also welcome back Air Marshal Tim Jones, who is the Deputy Chief of the Defence Staff (Force Development).

Secretary of State, thank you very much for appearing before the Defence Committee. Formally, on behalf of the Committee, congratulations on your new post—it is not quite as new now as when you took charge.

Wes Streeting: It feels like a long time ago now.

Chair: A long time ago, back in July. You have now been in post for nearly three months. What is your initial assessment of the main challenges facing UK defence?

Wes Streeting: Fundamentally, this country and our allies in NATO face a threat of a kind we have not seen since the end of the cold war. I come into a Ministry of Defence that is well positioned to meet that challenge, because of the work that John Healey did on defence reform to modernise the shape of the Ministry of Defence and because of the way the national security strategy spells out very clearly for us what the threat environment is. The strategic defence review sets out how we need to respond to it and the defence investment plan sets out how we then make that happen. I have come in and inherited a Department with the right focus, moving in the right direction.

Where is my focus? What am I most worried about? First and foremost, spelling out my priorities is simple because, fundamentally, they are to make sure that this country can deter our enemies and, should deterrence fail, be able to fight and win. That means that to meet the challenge set out for us by NATO we need to strengthen our capability; strengthen our defence industrial base, effectively making sure that we can out-produce and out-innovate our adversaries; and strengthen our alliances.

In that context, we have the right plan and the right strategy, and we’ve got very capable people. One of the great privileges of being in this post is that I get to peer behind the classification curtain and see just how extraordinary those people and much of our capability really are. It is important to say that against a backdrop in which talking our country down is something of a national pastime, and that we can be self-confident enough in what we can do but also self-aware enough to know we need to step up several gears.

That brings me to my final point, which is about how the country—the Ministry of Defence, Government, industry and the public—need to be thinking. NATO says we need to be able to fight and win a war against our adversaries by 2030. That is not because NATO wants a war—quite the opposite. We do not seek conflict with Russia. We do not seek escalation. We do not seek to provoke Russia. But we know that every single day Russia is testing us and testing our allies.

We know—I feel very strongly about this personally—that the term “hybrid threats” offers a sanitised description of what are acts of sabotage, targeted assassinations and, effectively, state-backed terrorism. That is the world in which we live today: it is extremely uncertain. If we are serious, and if we set as our organising principle warfighting readiness, that means making some whole-of-Government and whole-of-society choices to make sure that we invest in our defence sufficiently, modernise our capability and are ready to fight the next war and not the last one. It also means thinking not just about broadening and deepening our defence industrial base but about strengthening resilience across British industry, so that we are in a place where we could sustain and win a war.

Finally, on alliances, we are extremely fortunate that we sit at the centre of one of the most successful alliances in the history of the world: the NATO alliance. That is important at a time when multilateralism has somewhat gone out of fashion, but one of our challenges as a country is to make sure that the same unity of purpose that we see driven through NATO on defence is read across to other areas, like economic co-operation.

These are interesting times, and it is a great privilege to be asked to take on this job. For all the challenges, I think there has never been a better time to join Defence, in terms of the investment, opportunity, innovation and opportunities for careers in defence in the broadest sense, but there are significant challenges and we have to be honest about them.

Q2 Chair: Secretary of State, I wanted to give you a couple of minutes to set the scene and explain the importance of your role to the wider public, because you are our Defence Secretary at a critical juncture in history, so how you communicate to the wider public, particularly via the national conversation that we have talked about, will be very important. On behalf of the Committee, I also place on the record my thanks to you for the secret and classified briefings, and to the wider Ministry of Defence team for their co-operation.

You talked about modernisation; can you explain any immediate changes that you have managed to implement since taking charge at the MOD?

Wes Streeting: When you come in as someone new, you want to make your mark, and there is always the temptation to come in and upend everything, but actually, I have inherited defence reform at an advanced stage, in terms of how the Ministry of Defence is modernising.

But there are things that we need to do, and that we are doing, to cohere the four strands of the Ministry of Defence and to make sure that we have joined-up leadership at the top of the organisation. The NAD group is absolutely central to dealing with the issues that have historically plagued the Ministry of Defence and its reputation: value for money, productivity, procurement, buying the right things, and distinguishing between the exquisite capabilities that we need to design and procure and the stuff that we can get off the shelf. That is one of the most important parts of defence reform, so it is about seeing that through.

If I may say, the one thing I have personally brought and will continue to bring to the Ministry of Defence is the focus on value for money, productivity and making sure that we spend taxpayers’ money wisely. There are lots of similarities and lots of differences between being Defence Secretary and being Health Secretary, but there is one fundamental difference. When I was Health Secretary, I was constantly asked why we were “just throwing more money at the health service,” and I constantly had to prove that we were modernising, innovating, balancing the books—which we did in the NHS for the first time in a decade—and focusing on productivity, where we smashed our targets.

In defence, the entire media conversation, and a lot of the conversation we have on the Floor of the House of Commons, is—not to put too fine a point on it—about asking for more money. That is really important, and long may it continue, I might say, ahead of the Budget and the spending review—I’m never going to complain about people batting for the Ministry of Defence—but I have to bring the focus on to making sure that the money going in is well spent, because I am acutely aware of the choices and trade-offs that imposes on other Government Departments and on the taxpayer.

I am also really convinced that, when you look at the Prime Minister’s overall vision for the country and some of his key priorities around reindustrialisation and opportunities for the next generation, the Ministry of Defence, led correctly, can also be a Ministry for growth and opportunity that can serve the economic and social benefits and impacts that the country needs, so that we are not just able to fight for our county but can build a country worth fighting for.

Q3 Chair: You touched on a range of issues in relation to defence spending. You will find Defence Committee members calling for not just more defence spending but making sure we have value for money. As you rightly pointed out, we want to ensure that defence reform addresses the capability gaps. In due course, Committee members will be drilling into the various facets, but I want to come in on value for money. As you rightly said when you took office, when you were Health Secretary you embarked on a bold programme of reform and modernisation, which is why you want to tackle wasteful procurement practices. In defence, we have unfortunately had perennial procurement problems. Can you give me any specific examples of where you have cut waste or are looking to do so to ensure value for money for the British taxpayer?

Wes Streeting: Some of it is held at a higher classification. Obviously, I have been in for a few months, so the first thing I will say is that I am not going to pretend that I have completely overhauled the way the Ministry of Defence approaches these things in the first few months; I will not claim credit for other people’s work. What I definitely want to do this afternoon is a bit of naming and praising, but I also want to take some responsibility for some decisions I have made since coming in.

On naming and praising—obviously, some of this is held at a higher classification—the way in which the Defence Nuclear Enterprise has organised itself to drive real productivity gains, working with industry as a good partner, but also challenging industry to make sure it delivers on time and with good value, has shown what is possible when you have the right leadership and the right focus on those things. What we are seeing in DNE is the most important part of what we are doing, in terms of our capability and industry partnership, and I think we will see more of that come through with the NAD group as well.

In terms of decisions that I have taken, and have taken some flack for, when I came in the finances for this fiscal year were off track, and we needed to make some savings to bring them into line and balance the books. I have taken decisions to stop doing some things this year. In some cases, they have been higher profile, and we have taken some criticism. I have taken those decisions unapologetically, mindful of the impact—I am not indifferent to it, by the way—because it was important for me to signal very early on that we cannot have a culture of overspending in the Ministry of Defence.

We are receiving more money. It has gone up significantly since Labour came into office. We are delivering the biggest sustained increase in defence spending since the end of the cold war, with more to come at the Budget and in the spending review, but that is not an excuse for overspending and overcommitting. I hope that some of the decisions I have taken, although they are not entirely popular—stopping doing things is never popular—have sent the right signal throughout the organisation and the armed forces that we will get greater resource and support, but that we also have a responsibility to live within our means.

Q4 Chair: You mentioned the Defence Nuclear Enterprise; I put it on the record on the Floor of the House that the Defence Committee unfortunately does not have sight of it, which is a major gap in parliamentary scrutiny. Your predecessors were talking about establishing a Committee to look at that, with linkages to the Defence Committee, and I hope you will take some steps on that very soon.

Wes Streeting: Yes, I can absolutely give you the assurance that I have been engaging with the Prime Minister on making sure we have appropriate parliamentary scrutiny of the DNE, given how important it is to the country. We can get this right. The Intelligence and Security Committee shows that there are ways of having effective democratic oversight of highly secretive and sensitive work.

Q5 Chair: Good. We look forward to that taking shape very soon, because, as you rightly said, value for money is key.

Q6 Mike Martin: Thank you all for coming. Secretary of State, what personally would you like to achieve over the next year?

Wes Streeting: To gear-shift from strategising to delivery mode. For lots of reasons we are all familiar with, because it was highly visible, the necessary work around developing both the SDR and, particularly, the defence investment plan that followed took far longer than anyone involved in that process would have liked. It has meant that for some time—although, to be fair to my predecessors, there has been lots of really positive work going on, lots more money going into defence and lots more activity as a result—it has inevitably been the case that, when you are focusing on something as fundamental as a strategic defence review and then wrangling over the defence investment plan, that has consumed a lot of oxygen and focus.

For me, shifting into the implementation of the DIP is the most important thing. I pay particular tribute to my immediate predecessor Dan Jarvis for the heavy lifting he did to get the DIP over the line. But we need to shift from saying to doing.

Q7 Mike Martin: So it is about the implementation of the DIP. Are there any metrics you would like to put on that to judge yourself by over the next year?

Wes Streeting: The DIP sets it out. We have got just shy of £300 billion over the next four years. We need to show that that money is being well spent, that programmes are running to time and that, at the same time as we are doing all of that work domestically, we are also strengthening international alliances.

A personal priority for me, as well as for the Government, is supporting Ukraine. In the short time I have been in office, I have done a lot through the Ukraine Defence Contact Group, which I co-chair with Boris Pistorius of Germany, to mobilise support for Ukraine ahead of this winter. We have another UDCG shortly, in November. It is of critical importance to this country, as well as to Ukraine and our wider allies in Europe, that we see Ukraine through what will be a very difficult winter and make sure they can build on the extraordinary progress they have made this year.

Q8 Mike Martin: As you know, the DIP is in two parts: there is the bit covered by the current spending review, and then another spending review is slated for next autumn, which will do the bit from 2030. You will also know that two thirds of the money in the DIP is in that bit beyond 2030. Can you speak a bit about your personal ambitions for that second bit of the DIP, which is currently unfunded?

Wes Streeting: This is one of the tensions, to go back to what I was just saying about strategising and planning versus delivery and doing. Obviously, it is my responsibility—

Mike Martin: It is a huge bit of strategy.

Wes Streeting: It is, yes. It is my responsibility. Without walking straight into the set of inevitable questions I am about to get around the spending review, it is my responsibility to put the best possible case forward to the Treasury for the spending review, to make sure that we make it as easy as possible for the Chancellor to say yes.

This goes back to the point I was making with you, Chair, about sending the right signals internally very early on. It would be very easy—and, indeed, extremely complacent and irresponsible—to assume that we can just send John Healey’s resignation letter into the spending review process as our submission and say, “Well, this is what you said and this is what we expect you to fund.” We know the Chancellor wants to spend more on defence. We know that is one of the big reasons why the Prime Minister appointed him. We none the less have to put forward the best possible case to justify that investment and to make sure we are well on our way on the path to 3.5%, which the Chancellor will set out.

Chair: Derek Twigg is going to come on to all these issues in terms of defence spending.

Q9 Mike Martin: Sorry to bring this up, because you are only just in post, but you mentioned resignations. If the second bit of the DIP is not fully funded as per the document in the DIP, will you be offering your resignation?

Wes Streeting: I am not sure if there is a precedent for this in British politics, but I managed to achieve front pages in my first week setting out the conditions in which I would resign from the job I had just been given. I would rather not repeat those front pages today, but suffice it to say that I stand by everything I have said previously. One of the reasons I said what I said in my first week so confidently is that I have absolutely no doubt about this Prime Minister and this Chancellor’s commitment to funding defence and to making sure we get what we need when we need it, but I also have a responsibility to make sure that I make it as easy as possible for them to say yes.

Q10 Derek Twigg: To follow on from that and clarify your position exactly, John Healey’s resignation letter said that the DIP funding settlement was insufficient. You posted: “Every word of warning here needs to be heeded.” Do you still think it is insufficient?

Wes Streeting: To be fair—this goes back to the point I made about Dan Jarvis, who did a lot of heavy lifting to get the DIP over the line and unlocked extra resource—

Derek Twigg: You can tell us it is going to take us to 2.7% by 2030. We have not committed to anything above that at the moment.

Wes Streeting: It takes us to 2.7% next year.

Q11 Derek Twigg: Where is the commitment after 2.7%?

Wes Streeting: That will be set out at the spending review.

Derek Twigg: That is what I am saying—there is no commitment currently.

Wes Streeting: But to be fair, Derek, if we go back through, Dan got the DIP over the line and got it out the door. There was a £4.7 billion gap, which needs to be plugged at this Budget for the immediate term. John—sorry, I am being far too familiar—the Chancellor has already said that he will plug that gap at the Budget and he has indicated that there will be more to come at the spending review. I understand what you are saying, which is that, so far, we can only see a line of sight to 2.7% next year, but given that it was 2.3% when Labour came to office and by next year it will be 2.7%, we can see a really clear trajectory that puts us well on the way to 3% and on the way to 3.5%.

Q12 Derek Twigg: Well, we don’t, because we have not got any commitment or confirmation yet. If you can point me to a commitment or confirmation that it will be at 3% by 2030, please share it with the Committee now.

Wes Streeting: But we would not normally, and we do not normally, set out ahead of spending reviews what the control totals will be.

Derek Twigg: That is my point. There is not any commitment as of today of getting to 3% by 2030. Unless you can share something we have not seen with us today, I fail to see where that is.

Wes Streeting: My argument would be that, from the choices that Labour has already made in Government through to how strongly the Chancellor felt about this as a matter of principle and through to the public statements of commitment that there will be more to come at the Budget and more to come at the spending review, I feel confident that we will get there, but I am not complacent and we need to put our best foot forward.

Q13 Derek Twigg: So we are relying on your confidence at the moment. You are on record that you are confident we will be at minimum 3% by 2030. Is that what you are saying?

Wes Streeting: I am looking for a mutual defence pact with this Committee. You can rely on my confidence, but I also rely on your advocacy, and I rely on colleagues across Parliament and the public. Honestly, Derek, I genuinely think that we will get to where we need to be, but we have to put our best foot forward as well. It is not reasonable to expect the Chancellor to make big spending commitments outside a fiscal event given where we have been as a country in the recent past, where the economy has tanked and the borrowing costs have gone up as a result.

Q14 Derek Twigg: We have heard similar commitments previously and we have not got there. I am pleased that you feel you are confident. Let’s hope that proves right. I am not going to ask about the spending review because you will just say, “It’s not for me, it’s for the Chancellor”, but I am going to ask this question. The Financial Times reported in August that the Chancellor would not use the October Budget to commit to the 3%, but that the decisions about the future of defence spending will be deferred until next year’s spending review. Is that your understanding?

Wes Streeting: I don’t think the Chancellor was ever going to set out 3% in the Budget; it was always the spending review.

Q15 Derek Twigg: That’s fine; it was just to be clear. Again, you will come back to me and say, “We haven’t actually set a date for the spending review,” but the report is that it was going to be in the spring, but it will now be delayed to the autumn, which is around about a year’s time. Based on our and NATO’s intelligence, there will possibly be a Russian attack on NATO or a NATO country by 2030. Do you think that year’s gap is showing sufficient speed to up defence spending to meet that threatened potential attack by Russia in 2030?

Wes Streeting: Given that we are already experiencing increased investment in defence, and we are on the upward trajectory, it does not stop us from doing anything now. This in some ways comes back to the line of questioning or the tension I identified in response to Mike, which is that we absolutely need to plan for the medium term. We have to make the case at the spending review and win the resources that we need, but we also have to crack on with delivering now. This is part of my anxiety in a way about the debate always being about what is going to happen at the spending review. We also have to focus on making sure that we spend what we already have.

Q16 Derek Twigg: John Healey said in his resignation letter: “The extra support is backloaded when the pressure of operations and imperative to speed up readiness to fight is in the first two years and it rises to just 2.68% of GDP”—it is obviously 2.7%—“in 2030.” What do you make of that comment, given what you have just said?

Wes Streeting: Extra funding was won by Dan Jarvis during the DIP process, and we will be getting—

Derek Twigg: But it is still backloaded.

Wes Streeting: We will get the £4.7 billion at the Budget. I will bring in the permanent secretary, because he was party to those discussions.

Q17 Derek Twigg: So are you saying that it is no longer backloaded? I missed that, if that is the case.

Jeremy Pocklington: The specific point about—

Derek Twigg: Is it backloaded or not?

Jeremy Pocklington: The additional resource—

Derek Twigg: Sorry, can I interrupt, because I want to get a straight answer for the Committee. Is it backloaded or not?

Q18 Derek Twigg: Can you remind the Committee how much that was?

Jeremy Pocklington: That included a £500 million modernisation fund and additional changes to our spending power that gave the Ministry of Defence more RDEL than previously. I am in an unusual—the first position in time—

Chair: Before we get into RDEL and CDEL, Mr Twigg is asking a question about backloading. It would be good to get an answer to that, please.

Jeremy Pocklington: The overall departmental spending is on a rising profile, so by the end of this Parliament it will be 27% higher in real terms than at the start—

Chair: We have very limited time—we have only two hours to drill into these facts. Can we get the answer to Mr Twigg’s question?

Q19 Derek Twigg: Is it backloaded? Can you tell the Committee?

Jeremy Pocklington: I am keen to help the Committee. I can read out what our spending totals are for each year—

Q20 Derek Twigg: I am asking a specific question: is it backloaded?

Jeremy Pocklington: I am not sure I understand the definition—

Derek Twigg: Well, your previous Secretary of State said it was.

Jeremy Pocklington: What I am saying is—

Derek Twigg: It is in his letter.

Jeremy Pocklington: We received additional support between the time between the time of John Healey’s resignation and Dan Jarvis that meant that the spending power of the Department has actually increased by £15 billion. It was a lower amount at the time that John Healey resigned. We do have additional support, including, in particular, additional support for RDEL for readiness—

Q21 Derek Twigg: I will ask you one final time, Mr Pocklington. Do you agree with your previous Secretary of State that it is backloaded?

Jeremy Pocklington: I think the settlement had changed. There is not more that I can give to the Committee. The settlement had changed after John Healey resigned and Dan Jarvis—

Q22 Derek Twigg: Time is running out, so can I go to another question for the Secretary of State? Do you think that it is acceptable that we have to wait until possibly next autumn, assuming your confidence is borne out, for the decision on reaching the 3% to be taken? Wes Streeting: First, it doesn’t stop us doing things now, it does not stop us doing things next year and it does not stop us planning, with some assumptions that defence spending will continue to rise. I do not think it needs to slow us down or stop us from doing anything. I do not know when the spending review is going to be.

Q23 Derek Twigg: Are you going to be spending money you have not got, then?

Wes Streeting: No, because we know what we have got for this year. We know what we will get for the next year—

Wes Streeting: The Budget will set out—

Wes Streeting: I think we can live with that. We can absolutely live with that. We have got the resources we need for this year. Q25 Chair: But Secretary of State, I think what Mr Twigg is saying is that the threats are here and now. It seems palpable to many of us that we are not meeting the moment, and that is why there is a sense of exasperation. Wes Streeting: No, I don’t, because we have already got the biggest sustained increase in defence spending since the end of the cold war—in the space of not even two-and-a-half years, from 2.3% when we came in, to 2.7% next year. We have a stated commitment from the Chancellor to give us—

Q26 Chair: It is 2.7% by the end of the Parliament. I don’t think it is 2.7% by next year.

Wes Streeting: No, in 2027-28, we will be spending 2.7%.

Chair: By the end of 2028.

Q27 Chair: But Secretary of State, how can we can go about taking reasonable steps when the funding is not there? You also intimated that we have to wait until the spending review. The spending review has now been kicked into the long grass again, until next autumn.

Do you not think, given the urgency, that we can make a statement and that that is exactly what the Prime Minister and Chancellor should be doing?

Wes Streeting: But crucially, Tan, what Prime Minister Keir Starmer did when he made that choice was to set out the increase in defence funding and how it was going to be paid for simultaneously—

Chair: Without a spending review.

Wes Streeting: Outside a major fiscal event, but still explaining how it would be paid for. We have had very bitter recent experience of a former Prime Minister making unfunded commitments to spend more, tax less and borrow more, all simultaneously, and it led to enormous loss of confidence and people’s mortgages going up, and a level of fundamental loss of confidence in Britain among investors that this country is still recovering from.

Chair: That was done at a fiscal event, in a Budget.

Wes Streeting: Where I would absolutely defend the Chancellor is that he wants to spend more on defence—we know this. He says he will do more at the Budget. He says he will do more at the spending review. He also has to show how he pays for it.

Our debt-to-GDP ratio is somewhere around 100%. The amount we are spending as a state just on servicing debt interest is about one in every 10 taxpayer pounds. If we go out making unfunded commitments, and the markets choose to price that risk in, the cost of borrowing goes up and we have less to spend on our priorities, before we have even done anything.

Q28 Chair: I don’t think the Defence Committee is advocating for unfunded spending commitments, but the Committee was recently in Denmark which has gone, amazingly, from a 1.3% spend of GDP on defence to 4.99% within four years. I wish we were, but that is one of the reasons why they have got a bit more headroom than us to make those choices. Given the constraints we are operating under, I would just say—and believe me I am not remotely complacent about this; I cheered John Healey on when he made his principled resignation, I felt just as uncomfortable as he did about where we were on defence spending, and I would not have accepted the Prime Minister’s offer to take on this role if I did not believe he was also committed to funding—that we have got to make sure we understand the economic and fiscal context we are in and give the Chancellor the time and space he needs to give us what we need while showing how to pay for it.

Today’s IMF numbers are particularly encouraging—relatively as well—but they do not show the benign conditions we want to be in. We have a responsibility collectively, Parliament and Government, to think about that context, otherwise our taxpayers, our citizens, our constituents and the state pay a heavy price if we are not disciplined with the finances.

Chair: We look forward to your confidence being rewarded.

Q29 Lincoln Jopp: Secretary of State, you said you wanted a defence pact with this Committee—that we would advocate for higher defence expenditure. No doubt you will be aware that this Committee has been calling for 3% by 2030 for quite some time. You also said that you wanted the public to be calling for that.

In the strategic defence review, there was a call for the beginning of a national conversation. When the Committee was in Norway, we heard that their Prime Minister, regardless of what speech he is making, will always refer to the threat that his country is under. We here are not having that conversation nationally and the Prime Minister rather foolishly said that national security cannot come at the expense of social security, a position I was happy to hear him row back on yesterday. Do you see part of your role as an advocate for defence to be louder in that conversation at a national level, so that the public come to realise just how much money we need to spend on defence and potentially some of the difficult trade-offs that we are going to have to make?

Wes Streeting: Yes. One of the reasons why the Prime Minister asked me to take on this responsibility is to make sure that we communicate effectively the kind of threat environment we are in, and to ensure that we have a country that is alert but not alarmed. In terms of the Prime Minister’s position, he has, on multiple occasions since he took office, talked about the kind of threat environment we are operating in, and in a number of ways—from hosting President Zelensky down in Portsmouth through to the two-and-a-half hours he spent on the Floor of the House of Commons yesterday talking about some of the hybrid threats that we are experiencing as a country. At the moment, it does not carry the support of the President of the United States of America. Therefore, the deal is not good enough. We have to work through it to get to a settled position. That is why every Prime Minister since Boris Johnson has been trying to sort this, because the risk we are carrying is not worth it, set against the capability that is put at risk.

Q77 Lincoln Jopp: You missed out one person in your list, of course; Lord Cameron killed it as a negotiation when he was Foreign Secretary.

Wes Streeting: I am always happy to speak to former Prime Ministers, and I would be very happy to talk to David Cameron about it. I am not sure that is an entirely fair characterisation of where the Chagos process got to.

Q78 Jesse Norman: We obviously have significant interests across the Gulf. We have had a long historical footprint there. It is under an enormous amount of strain. What is our current strategy and objective in relation to defence of UK interests in the Gulf?

Wes Streeting: First, being a reliable partner and ally. Whether it is on defence exports or mutual defence assistance, we are actively engaged in the Gulf. We have seen that particularly brought to light by the appalling events in Saudi Arabia in recent days. Houthi escalation prior to the attack on airports was completely unacceptable, but to attack civilian infrastructure in this way, with the numbers of casualties we are seeing, is contemptible. I am proud of the fact that this country has stepped up when our ally has called, with air-to-air refuelling support and with ground-based air defences, and we are looking at what more we can do to assist Saudi Arabia.

Ultimately, we know that, whether it is the Strait of Hormuz or the risk surrounding Bab al-Mandab, and the impact that both those things are having on the global economy and on maritime freedom, there are not military solutions to those conflicts. There does need to be a negotiated end. We are supporting the diplomatic track, but we are also doing everything we can to stand with our allies who are under attack.

Q79 Jesse Norman: How do you trade that off with the escalating demands being made on us in relation to Ukraine and to NATO?

Wes Streeting: In some ways, it has not been too much of a wrestle in terms of the capability that we have provided to Saudi Arabia, for example, and in some of the work that we are planning with the multinational military mission in the event we have an environment in which the MMM can operate in the Strait of Hormuz. However, there are some trade-offs and things that we would like to do that sometimes we cannot agree to, because we might put our national interests at risk or stretch ourselves too thinly in terms of our own continent and interests, and our partners understand that. You are right to say that there are those choices and trade-offs from time to time, depending on the sorts of requirements and capability we are asked to provide.

Q80 Derek Twigg: Turning to Ukraine for a moment, obviously her defence ability has been severely and detrimentally impacted by the lack of Patriot missiles. We have seen the consequences just in recent days and weeks of the dwindling air defence capacity. What are we doing as a country to try to source more Patriot missiles but also, in particular, to find alternatives and develop new interceptors, to give Ukraine that greater assurance and ability to defend itself, not least in the coming winter onslaught that we will see?

Wes Streeting: In the last meeting of the UDCG that I chaired, we announced a £100 million contribution to PURL to support delivery of Patriot missiles and other air defence equipment before winter. We are also working with allies across the UDCG to mobilise support. I will not pretend we are yet where we want to be, and you will have seen that President Zelensky is always at pains to thank allies for support while also bluntly pointing out that they need more assistance, so we are doing our best to accelerate. Obviously, you will have seen the very early announcement the Prime Minister made around sharing our IP and permissions on components for the French-made SCALP missile that will support plans for local assembly and production in Ukraine.

We are also working with France and Italy on Stratus, which is the Storm Shadow successor. Ultimately, we are all in the same space at the moment: looking at what we have across Ukraine’s allies and at what level of risk those countries are willing to carry when thinking about what more they can provide to Ukraine; how we can up production, but also what more we can do to accelerate the pace of development of low-cost, high effectors.

Q81 Derek Twigg: I will put to you a difficult question, because it is a matter of what you believe to be the case, and what we should know: are we really saying that we cannot make a significant difference to Ukraine’s defence in the next few months, and it is going to have to basically take it?

Wes Streeting: I would not go that far. I would just say there is a lot of work to do ahead of the next UDCG. I was keen for that meeting to be in person because one reason the UDCG has been effective is that it is a forcing mechanism. If we have something coming up in the calendar, whether swotting up for a Select Committee or not wanting to look embarrassed in front of our counterparts at a meeting about support for Ukraine, at an in-person meeting you can literally eyeball people. We are having an in-person meeting precisely to do that.

Between me, Boris Pistorius and Mark Rutte and those allies who are really stepping up to support Ukraine, we are once again doing the loop around the UDCG. We all have fiscal constraints. We are all operating with a level of risk in the current threat environment that we would rather not be facing, so it is requiring people to compromise and to go a bit further than they might like, but it is absolutely worth it and our support for Ukraine is resolute.

The other argument I am at pains to make publicly as often as possible domestically, given that support for Ukraine remains high here when it is not so high in some of Ukraine’s other ally countries, is that, yes, we are supporting Ukraine and a set of principles that we subscribe to—freedom, democracy, self-determination—but no one in Britain should be in any doubt that the support we are providing Ukraine is also in our national self-interest, so we will do whatever we can as fast as we can for as long as it takes.
