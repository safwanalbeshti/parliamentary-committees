Witnesses

I: Sir Declan Morgan, Chief Commissioner, ICRIR; Ronnie Armour, Interim Chief Executive Officer, ICRIR; and Amanda Logan, Assistant Commissioner for Investigations, ICRIR.

Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Sir Declan Morgan, Ronnie Armour and Amanda Logan.

Chair: Welcome to this Northern Ireland Affairs Committee session on the troubles Bill. I thank Sir Declan Morgan, the chief commissioner, Ronnie Armour, the interim chief executive officer, and Amanda Logan, the assistant commissioner for investigations, for joining us.

Sir Declan, thanks for the information you provided to our session in June, and for your recent correspondence on information sharing by the Irish Government. Have those arrangements improved since you were last before the Committee?

Sir Declan Morgan: No, not particularly. We have, I think, sent a total of 12 letters in relation to these matters, and we have had an acknowledgment, but we have not had a substantive reply.

It is important to recognise that this issue is necessarily complex. The arrangement we have in place in relation to the United Kingdom is that the Secretary of State acts as the guarantor of matters of national security. There are strict provisions within the Act in terms of what the commission can do in relation to that material, and there are criminal offences in place to ensure the protection and operation of those procedures.

We have always aspired to have information disclosure protocols with Ireland, in exactly the same way as we have with agencies within the United Kingdom. But if we were to try to replicate the protections within the UK space for Ireland’s national security interests, it becomes extremely difficult to see how that works. That means, therefore, that there is a need for a political solution to that issue. That political solution may come as we go through the legislative process, and we would hope that that will be the case.

Although there was good will from the Garda Commissioner, for instance, when we met him to put matters in place, and although the authorities in the Republic of Ireland are putting together procedures, one thing that occurred to me is what happens if there is an investigation in the Republic of Ireland in relation to a legacy matter. Will there be reciprocation from our Security Service and others in relation to providing full and unredacted information to the Irish authorities, on the basis that they will have some method of protecting all that? That is really what we are talking about if we are talking about exchange.

I think that that indicates that this is a complex, difficult, sensitive issue, which the Governments are going to have to work out.

Chair: We have heard, and the Secretary of State has said, that as the troubles bill is passing through Parliament, the Irish Government is in lockstep. We will then see legislation in Ireland, and it will then take time for you to reform the ICRIR into the Legacy Commission. It could take a very long time until you have that co-operation with the Irish Government for them to provide information.

Sir Declan Morgan: Unless there is some political solution of which I am not aware, it does not seem likely that this is going to be resolved until the legislation is passed in both parts of the island.

Chair: Do you think the Secretary of State needs to agree some kind of memorandum of understanding with the Irish Government for them to co-operate with the ICRIR?

Sir Declan Morgan: It would be helpful if they were to do that, but the memorandum of understanding would have to look at the extent to which that would include sensitive information. That is the issue that might turn out to be troublesome. There would be a difference in terms of providing non-sensitive information, which would not require the same protections. But we also have access to sensitive information from the authorities in the United Kingdom, which in many cases can be very important in forming a view as to where the case should go.

Chair: Your letter referred to 11 requests for assistance to the Garda in Ireland, covering 31 deaths. Have any of those requests resulted in substantive disclosure, or have you had nothing?

Sir Declan Morgan: No.

Chair: So you have had nothing.

Sir Declan Morgan: We have had no material. As a result, I think, of the issue being raised before your Committee, Chair, we have had acknowledgments of receipt of our letters and an indication that this was part of the ongoing process. But the position, as we understand it, remains as explained by the Garda Commissioner to us in January when we met him, when the political direction was that they were not in a position to provide information at this stage. That is why it seems that the political resolution that will come with the passing of the legislation in this jurisdiction and in the Republic of Ireland is the key to a serious discussion about how exactly the proper national security interests of the United Kingdom and Ireland are respected.

Chair: How many cases is this holding up for you now?

Sir Declan Morgan: As we have said before, there are approximately 500 deaths along the border, in respect of which a number of support groups are intimately involved. Their indication to us is that many of the people who lost loved ones in those events are not going to take part in this process unless they feel that information from Ireland can be secured. For instance, the bomb in the Warrenpoint case was detonated from the Republic of Ireland. That seems clear, but we have no information. At this stage, we would not be able to ensure that we could get information in relation to what happened there, and it does not seem likely that that will be provided to us before the political issues are resolved.

Adam Jogee: Sir Declan and colleagues, thank you for being with us this morning. Sir Declan, thank you for your years of public service. Before I ask my questions, I want to say that I have constituents down from Newcastle-under-Lyme for Armistice Day, so I will have to go after my questions, but that is no reflection on or discourtesy to you.

I join the Chair in thanking you for your very helpful letter. We went through it again before your questions, and it was very useful indeed in helping to paint the picture. In that letter, you say that the Irish Government has taken the position that it cannot share information with the commission because it is not a law enforcement agency. However, with Operation Kenova there was significant engagement with An Garda Síochána. Why do you think the situation is different with the ICRIR?

Sir Declan Morgan: One has to understand that the ICRIR is not a law enforcement agency. Kenova was set up under the auspices of the PSNI to carry out a criminal investigation. It was not actually there to provide reports, although it did so as an addendum to its criminal work. It is important to understand that we have sent 12 letters but, as far as I know, no ILRs—international letters of request—have been sent. Because we are not a law enforcement agency, we would not be able to send them. If they are to be sent, they will be sent by the Public Prosecution Service. They will be sent only in relation to cases where we are in a position to proceed, and are proceeding, with a criminal investigation. ILRs would not be applicable in cases where we had concluded that there is no real prospect of a criminal case, but we still want information in order to explain to loved ones what had happened to their family members.

Adam Jogee: On that point about criminal investigations and information, do you think the powers that will be given to the Legacy Commission to conduct criminal investigations will help to address the challenges around the sharing of information?

Sir Declan Morgan: We start every case on the basis that it is a criminal investigation, but we have to be clear about the fact that, in the vast majority of cases, it is unlikely that there will be prosecutions. Where there are not prosecutions, we will be seeking to provide answers for the family members. I am on the record as saying that I believe that when we stand back and look at our work in 10 years’ time, we will be seen as a truth recovery body, rather than as a law enforcement organisation. At the moment, something around a third of the cases that we have through the investigation space have already been identified as cases that will proceed as non-criminal cases. I would expect that number to increase significantly.

Adam Jogee: This question is linked but slightly different: what assessment do you make of the feeling the Committee has picked up that there are perceived delays when it comes to legislation being taken through the Oireachtas in Dublin, compared with here? Are you concerned that there is a feeling that Ireland is waiting for the United Kingdom to finish its job before starting its own?

Sir Declan Morgan: There is absolutely no question about the fact that Ireland is waiting to see the Bill that the United Kingdom passes. I do not think that there is anything hidden about that, or that it is anything that they have tried to hide. All their political pronouncements that I have noted have tended to indicate that their commitment is to pass their legislation, which they claim they can do expeditiously. Our desire is to see the implementation of the legislation on both sides of the border at the same time, so that it gives the new commission the opportunity to have a clean slate as to how they can proceed.

Adam Jogee: Are you concerned that they are waiting?

Sir Declan Morgan: I was concerned until 19 October about the position in this jurisdiction. I was delighted to see that things went as they did, and I hope that in the other place the position will be the same. There is always a touch of nervousness when somebody has given you a promise but you have not actually seen the fulfilment of it. There is always the worry that somebody could decide that some aspect of the legislation that was passed did not suit them and that that would be a way out. From that point of view, there are those who would suggest that Ireland has reserved its position as to whether it plays ball. I can understand why people might think that; I am not saying that I have any reason at all to think that that is the case, because all the pronouncements are the other way. But I can understand that people are concerned until they see the thing done, and that there could be difficulties.

Claire Hanna: It is good to see you. You touched on this in your answers to my colleagues, but to what extent do you think the Government took on board your feedback about the operation of the Bill and the body, and were you satisfied with that? What engagement have you had with them since the package of amendments they brought forward last month?

Sir Declan Morgan: We have engaged with them in a number of areas. First, I will say that there are aspects of the Bill that are very positive in terms of securing public confidence: the victims and survivors advisory group, which is important; the extent of the oversight arrangements that are in place through the oversight board and the regular two, three, five and seven-year reviews; and the involvement of HMIC in the way in which we conduct these things. All those are designed to achieve public confidence, and I welcome anything that is going to do that. It can only be of assistance to the new commission to have the advice of all these extremely efficient, well-run organisations, and the people who are involved as victims and survivors, in looking at the way forward.

Some aspects of the Bill are necessarily going to require us to think about what skills are necessary in relation to some of the roles. On the indication of the two directors, for instance, we wonder exactly how that relationship is going to evolve and what the role of the chief executive officer is in relation to it. For what it is worth, I think that the chief executive officer, on one view, seems to have enormous responsibilities within the Bill, but if you think it through, the chief executive is really there to make sure that conflict of interest issues are properly addressed and to resolve any difficulties that may arise between the two directors. Hopefully the two directors will be grown up and the chances of those issues arising will be minimised.

When you read the Bill, you have to start thinking through how you will operationalise this, and we have been talking to the NIO about that. There is still a way to go with inquisitorial proceedings, which are also an important part of providing confidence to people. We need to make sure that they are effective. I have had a number of meetings with people in various areas of Government or supporting Government, and the unanimous voice coming back to me is that public inquiries are very expensive, take a long time to put in place and are not necessarily as focused as they should be.

There are a lot of lessons in there for us in terms of the way we should approach inquisitorial proceedings. My own aspiration is that, if we do this properly, we might shine a light on how inquiries of this kind might be put in place in a more focused and efficient way, so that we ensure that people feel more comfortable about having further public inquiries.

Mr Kohler: Good to see you, Sir Declan and colleagues. As you are aware, the Government inserted a clause into the Bill requiring that the Attorney General give consent to any serious troubles-related prosecutions going ahead. What is your view of that provision?

Sir Declan Morgan: Two things, I suppose. First, there is no reason at all why it should lead to a different result from the result that would be generated by the Public Prosecution Service. Secondly, there is no doubt that some people will think that it might lead to a different result.

You have to take both those things on board and recognise them as realities. Whatever way this works, there is a long history of litigation in Northern Ireland in relation to judicial review of decisions to prosecute or not prosecute. The Bloody Sunday case is an example where there were two or three court hearings before the case was eventually prosecuted, and the soldier was then acquitted. There is judicial oversight no matter which way you go.

The choice as to how one approaches that particular issue is one that needs to be looked at. I am a mere judge—or I was a mere judge until they told me that I was over five years retired, and therefore was not capable of being one any more—but this is a classically political issue. I think I am going to dance my way off that particular podium, thank you very much.

Mr Kohler: The Irish Government have stopped short of criticising the provision, but the Committee on Justice of the Oireachtas has recommended that the Irish Government formally oppose the provision. If that happens, what will it do for the hoped-for co-operation between the Legacy Commission and the Irish Government?

Sir Declan Morgan: In the end, with all these things, it depends. There may be issues on which each side takes a different view, but as I have said before, if they are grown up about it, they will understand the bigger issue here, which is what we can do for victims and families. There may be some element of give and take—so one side gives some things, and the other side gives others—but I am afraid that that is well beyond my pay grade, Paul.

Mr Kohler: Okay, but in your estimation, is the Attorney General clause a make-or-break provision, or is it just one of the many issues on which there will be give and take?

Sir Declan Morgan: I do not know. I do know that it should not make a difference, but that it will be perceived by some people as potentially making a difference. Those are the factors that people will need to take into account in deciding whether or not it should be retained, and it will no doubt be the subject of discussion in another place.

Sir Alec Shelbrooke: The Bill includes new duties to avoid the duplication of previous investigations. How will you determine when a fresh investigation is justified, and when previous work should be relied upon?

Sir Declan Morgan: One has to remember that, in a lot of these cases, there are materials that are available from previous investigations, and in some cases, there are even materials from previous court hearings on what has happened. If you have cases in which there has already been some form of inquiry, statement taking or setting out of position, you need to think very hard about whether and to what extent there is value in going back 30, 40 or 50 years later to those who may have been involved in making those statements or contributions. It seems to us that the duplication provisions are there to make sure that happens only if it is necessary. You have the material from the earlier investigations, which you are able to make judgments about. If you have detailed statements from a previous investigation, those are the starting point from which you proceed. You would have to have a reason for going back to any of the people involved if you were conducting either a criminal or another investigation.

Sir Alec Shelbrooke: That is a very interesting answer because there is a lot of controversy around people being newly questioned. Many people would say that, even at the time, recollections may vary, which is why debriefing takes place almost immediately. Is there any value now, 50 years later, in interviewing people about what happened at the time? You will be well aware what I am driving at.

Sir Declan Morgan: The point I have made is that I cannot see that there is value if you already have an account of what people say happened at the time. Where there may be value is if you find that somebody who is identified as a central player has not made a statement or for some reason or other was not interviewed, or something of that kind. You might want to go back at that stage.

As is clear from the umpteen court cases that have occurred in this area, every account that somebody gives, particularly if the period they are speaking about was a long time ago, needs to be seen as vulnerable in terms of its reliability and accuracy. In this sort of territory, where you are looking back over many years at events—some of which may be memorable; some of which may have passed in a cloud of other experiences people had during that time—you have to be quite careful about thinking that there is a need to engage further.

However, as I say, there will be cases where new material has potentially emerged and it may be relevant to follow up with somebody who was not questioned at the time to see if they have anything to contribute. But all of that seems to me perfectly consistent with the need to ensure that you produce an efficient and expeditious report that would comply with any legal obligation nationally or internationally.

Sir Alec Shelbrooke: Thank you for that answer, because there are some very important aspects to what you have just said that are worth taking into account. The Secretary of State retains significant powers to prevent the disclosure of sensitive information. Are there any safeguards that you would like to see included in the Bill to maximise public confidence that national security considerations are not applied more broadly than necessary?

Sir Declan Morgan: I welcome the fact that the commission has been given the power to appeal against the Secretary of State’s determination in relation to national security. That is an important power and it is important that the new commission should be seen to be exercising that role. For me, that is the biggest plus for the commission because it will be in a good place to make a judgment. It will have seen the material. It will form the view. It will understand, in terms of the dialogue it will have with the Secretary of State, what the national security issues are and, on the other side, what the public interest issues are. It is helpful from everybody’s point of view that the commission should be a party to those proceedings and in a position to initiate them if it considers it appropriate.

Sir Alec Shelbrooke: Leading on from that, the obvious question is: do you foresee a circumstance in which you may disagree with the Secretary of State’s decision on the disclosure of sensitive information?

Sir Declan Morgan: Yes, I imagine that is entirely possible. I would not like to speculate on exactly what the circumstances might be that would lead to that, but we welcomed the power for a reason, and the reason was that we thought there may be circumstances in which we would take a different view. It was important for us to be in a position to demonstrate that, rather than coming in through the back door, as it were, behind a requesting individual who would have had to initiate things. This way, we can demonstrate our view of what the public interest requires in a particular circumstance.

Chair: I introduced an amendment to the troubles Bill on behalf of the Committee to remove from organisations such as the PSNI the costly administrative burden of categorising the sensitivity of evidence it passes to the commission. What impact would that have on your organisation’s work? I will give you a break, Sir Declan, and ask Amanda.

Sir Declan Morgan: Thank you.

Amanda Logan: At the minute, we have processes in place whereby we receive information from not just the PSNI but other holders of information. That is marked “Legacy Act sensitive” on our systems. I think putting the onus on the commission is obviously an additional burden that we do not currently have. Sometimes it is very obvious if a piece of information is sensitive, but other times there is the collective piece that only the originators of that material would know. We would have to work in partnership with those agencies to make sure we were all satisfied that the information was tagged appropriately.

Chair: You have your findings team. Do you need more investigators and investigatory powers people in there actually getting a grip of this material?

Amanda Logan: Absolutely. We did a piece of work towards the end of last year in terms of identifying the investigative model that would suffice for the commission to deliver on its responsibilities. That went through a rigorous independent scrutiny panel that was brought forward by the NIO in January of this year.

That panel concluded that the commission needed around nine syndicates. We talk about syndicates; there are 50 investigators and support staff in a syndicate. However, they also concluded that, if we were able to utilise artificial intelligence, we could reduce that to seven. That is about just over 400 investigators and investigative support staff. When we talk about support staff, we are talking about intelligence officers, analysts, the MIR that are taking in that information and our case support—the front door, if you like.

At the minute, we have 189 operational staff in those roles, so we have less than half of what the commission needs in order to fulfil its functions, which means we absolutely need the resources to be able to do it. If it is helpful to resource other parts of the system, if we reduce the impact on us in order to get the information and archive it in the first instance, that would be helpful.

Chair: Going back to my amendment, would you like to see it reintroduced in the Lords, or would you see it more as a problem?

Amanda Logan: I see it as something we could work—it is a difficult one.

Chair: I don’t mind; I’m not precious.

Amanda Logan: It is always useful for the originator of the information to mark that information as sensitive or non-sensitive, because they know where that information has come from. We have the keys to the castle, as Jon Boutcher put it, but we do not necessarily have a blueprint or know where that information is held or where it has come from. It has been very challenging to work in that arena, so I would welcome as much assistance that the commission can get at the front end as possible.

Sir Declan Morgan: One thing that we hope might get a bit better is that we had one case, as you know, which is reported, and it spent two months in the Secretary of State’s office for security checking.

Chair: Two months?

Sir Declan Morgan: The second case just came out last Friday, and that spent two months in the Secretary of State’s office for security checking. My understanding is that part of that is finding out from the various agencies what the national security issues may be in relation to these cases and, if the agencies do not respond as quickly as they might do, that leaves the Secretary of State having to utilise the full 60-day period that the provision provides. I suppose my qualification to all this is that whatever we do, I would like to see something that ensures that the people who need to understand and explain what the national security issues are do so expeditiously, because that is what is keeping us from publishing—

Chair: Sir Declan, I would have thought that would be all sorted before it even got to his desk—surely.

Sir Declan Morgan: You might well think that.

Chair: I am just trying to apply my normal logic to these situations.

Sir Declan Morgan: I have some experience of the way in which national security works. I do not think expedition is one of its big attributes, as far as I can see. I think you may have an opportunity to take that up with the Secretary of State at another stage.

Chair: Don’t worry, I will. Ronnie, do you have anything to add to that?

Ronnie Armour: No, I do not think so.

Simon Hoare: Chair, my apologies for being late. Sir Declan, in answer to Sir Alec’s question, you referenced national security and the powers given to you to appeal a decision by the Secretary of State. I think we all realise that praying in aid of national security with that sort of knowing nod of the head and sagacious look on the face has been used quite often to effectively say, “We don’t really want this in the public domain because it may cause embarrassment, discomfort and so on,” is nothing to do with national security; it is more to do with personal embarrassment. Given the fact that you now have a right to appeal a determination by the Secretary of State, do you hope that might encourage the Secretary of State and those who advise him or her to be somewhat judicious in praying in aid of national security knowing full well that it could be appealed? Do you hope that the spotlight will be placed more forensically on the reason?

Sir Declan Morgan: I agree with that, because the advantage that we have is that we see the full picture against which the national security issue is sought. Therefore, if it appears to us that there is any kind of misuse for the type of purpose that you have suggested, at the very least, that is something that we would have to take to a judicial tribunal to examine. As with all these things, you do not have to actually use the statute as long as you have the backing of it behind you.

Simon Hoare: Do you think that one by-product of that is that those who have been suspicious of the process—who think the state will continue just to find an updated way of, as they would see it, covering up certain things—may gain greater confidence and faith in it, knowing full well that praying in aid of national security is capable of being intensely scrutinised for the justifications in so doing?

Sir Declan Morgan: I agree that the fact that we have the appeal power in itself should provide confidence. If we use that power in an appropriate case, I suspect that the confidence will be increased.

Chair: Before we ask the next question, I want to make the panel aware that the Committee will be suspending to observe a pause for Remembrance Day.

Mr Kohler: I am due to ask you about Peter May, but before I do that, I will ask another quick question. If the legislation passes in its current form, broadly, we will end up with two parallel systems: the Legacy Commission and the historical coronial system, with regard to already open inquests. What is your view on whether it is sensible for those two parallel systems to coexist?

Sir Declan Morgan: I suppose I have a personal affiliation with the inquest system, since I set up the inquest plan, which was travelling along quite nicely until people came along and mucked it about. The important thing with the investigative and inquisitorial processes is that we need to get them much more in line with each other. We have a very efficient investigation process, so why do we not take some of that process into the inquisitorial regime, and ensure that we start every case by looking at it and conducting an investigation? Then, we can do what we want to do: focus in the hearings on the thing that actually matters.

That is where we, as an inquisitorial proceeding, have the opportunity to take a view that is different from the approach that has generally been taken in public inquiries, to make this work as an efficient, cost-effective and focused way of producing outcomes. That would mean that the bits that we establish through the investigation would not need further investigation at any open hearing—that would simply be a given—so you would go on to the things that really need to be examined. It would also mean that you are largely aligning the investigation process under the commission with the inquisitorial proceeding. The inquisitorial proceeding would have the add-on of the hearing, in relation to the things that remain.

Mr Kohler: But that is not how it is going to happen, is it?

Sir Declan Morgan: Well, I am not so sure that you are right about that. I think there is some enthusiasm for looking at that model. I would be quite supportive of it being examined to see whether it might provide a more focused and effective way of doing these things.

Mr Kohler: Will that require changes to legislation, or is it possible within the current formula?

Sir Declan Morgan: You might need to do a few tweaks here and there.

The Committee observed a two-minute silence.

On resuming—

Chair: We resume the session with a question from Paul Kohler.

Mr Kohler: In the Peter May review, serious concerns were expressed, as we all know. In June, you gave us an update that 11 of the 19 recommendations had been implemented or substantially completed. May we have an update on where we are with the remaining ones now and whether they have worked?

Sir Declan Morgan: I will pass this to Ronnie in a moment, but I just want to say that after the publication of the review, I had weekly meetings with Peter May on the implementation of the recommendations, and fortnightly meetings latterly, as we were coming towards the end. As Ronnie will explain, towards the end of last month we evolved into business-as-usual sponsorship engagement in relation to it.

Ronnie Armour: Significant progress has been made since the last time we reported to the Committee. Only three of the 19 recommendations are still active and in progress. The remainder have been implemented, and we continue to monitor that implementation.

To be helpful to the Committee, I will give a flavour of some of the structural and governance changes that have taken place over the past number of months. One of the key areas that Peter identified was finance, so we instigated a finance committee with an independent chair to look after that. We strengthened our governance relations with the Department and we now have a new sponsorship arrangement in place that oversees the work of the commission, such as our corporate services. Engagement with the NIO is now much more regular than it would have been in the past.

We have improved communications with our staff. I would be the first to recognise there is still more to do in that area, but it is much more positive now than it would have been. We have formal industrial relations mechanisms in place. We recognise the Public and Commercial Services Union and are working very closely with it. We have introduced independent external assurance of investigative practices, which I think has been a positive, and we have strengthened oversight of investigations processing right through to findings. An incredible amount of work has been completed in a relatively short space of time, when you look at the detail of the report and the 19 recommendations.

I know that you are particularly interested in those recommendations that we have not yet fully delivered. I want to assure the Committee that work is actively in progress on each of those. Work is under way, for example, to clarify the respective roles of line management and the corporate centre, which is recommendation 4. That is a detailed piece of work. We have a project lead taking that forward and we are working to achieve that in the next three to six months, or thereabouts.

We are advancing on the development of a formal lessons learned project, which is recommendation 18. Our senior leadership team has agreed that a structured exercise will be undertaken in that respect once we have a sufficient number of cases that have reached the reporting stage. That is very much work in progress.

The final recommendation that we are still working on is recommendation 11, which goes to the heart of our awareness and understanding of the AME budgeting process. I think that we have come a long way there and that we now have a much better understanding than we did a year ago of AME and its implications. We are still working at building in the headroom for future budgets. That will be a work in progress, moving forward.

In summary, significant progress has been made overall in terms of implementation. I take confidence from the fact that we put in place a joint oversight group to monitor implementation, working closely with the NIO. At our last meeting in October, the oversight group agreed that, as Sir Declan has indicated, the remaining actions should be treated as business as usual. They are now being monitored under the sponsorship arrangements I mentioned, as part of our restructured and strengthened governance processes.

I think that is positive. I think it was a vote of confidence from the Department that we are progressing, but there is still work to do in terms of three recommendations. We want to continue to monitor ongoing implementation of the remainder of the recommendations.

Mr Kohler: You are monitoring implementation. How are you assessing whether it is working?

Ronnie Armour: We are doing a number of things, obviously. We have brought in some external support. I mentioned the finance arrangements. For example, we now have the independent chair of the finance committee, which brings with it assurance—

Mr Kohler: It is more the cultural things, though.

Ronnie Armour: In terms of the cultural things, we are working with our staff and our trade union partner. We recently undertook a pulse survey among our staff, which was encouraging but challenging at the same time. The pulse survey tells us that we have a staff who are very committed, very focused and very determined to deliver the business of the organisation, but they still have some concerns around internal communication and the cohesion of leadership. We are working at those things. In terms of the cultural stuff, we have also brought in a consultant, who is working with the senior leadership team and who has had a number of sessions with us as a team. I think that that has helped to bond the team. It has been encouraging in that sense. We still have a number of sessions to go, but that gives a flavour of what we are trying to do around the cultural piece.

Mr Kohler: Are you still meeting Peter May fortnightly, or has that stopped?

Sir Declan Morgan: That has stopped. I am now reduced to meeting him at events such as the 25th anniversary of the PSNI, but Northern Ireland is a very small place, so we do not miss each other.

Chair: Does he not have a formal role in the commission?

Sir Declan Morgan: No, he doesn’t. He was brought in as an independent contractor.

Chair: Ronnie, you mentioned the NIO a couple of times there. Do you work with a group of civil servants at the NIO? Do you have a specific contact? What is their role?

Ronnie Armour: Different people in the commission with different responsibilities engage with different people in the Department. As for my role, I work closely with the chief operating officer and her team. When I talk about the new sponsorship arrangements, that committee is chaired by the chief operating officer. I will attend that committee along with my senior colleagues, and she will attend with her senior colleagues. There are a range of interactions on a daily basis with different people within the Department, but in terms of that structured mechanism, it would be the chief operating officer.
