Andrew Isaac: Thank you very much for inviting me here today—it is a pleasure to be here and to try to help with your inquiry. I am chair of the Children’s Services Development Group. It was founded in 2006, I became involved in 2008, and became its chairman in 2016.

Dr Mark Kerr: Good morning. Thank you for inviting me to come and give evidence. I am the chief executive of the Children’s Homes Association, which is the membership body for children’s homes in England and Wales. We are a fairly broad church, with members from local authorities, the independent sector and the charitable sector, so I would like to think that we are representative of the sector you are here to talk about today. The Chair: How many members do you have in your association?

Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson: Good morning, and thank you so much for being here today. We have heard a lot from different stakeholders across the sector, but one thing that I am really trying to grapple with is how we should think about what good outcomes for children in residential care look like, how we measure that, and to what degree the market is currently delivering that. I would be grateful if you could give the committee your thoughts on that: how do you define a good outcome, how do you measure it and track it, and to what degree do you think the market is delivering good outcomes at the moment?

Andrew Isaac: A good outcome for a young person in the residential sector is realistically a healthy and positive transition into adulthood. That is terribly important. Currently, they have a single corporate parent up until they are 18, and then they have to navigate up to nine different agencies to go on from there. What worries me in that instance is that the care and attention that goes into them in their previous years is lost. The CSDG has looked very carefully at some of the things that happen to them. In 2025, 112 children leaving care committed suicide. I was unaware of that until a couple of days ago, and I found that very sad. If you look at people in the criminal justice system, 23% to 25% are care-experienced. If you could prevent that happening, that is a cost of £1 billion saved. I always believe that, once they get to 18, there should be a much better structure around them as they go forward, because of silly things such as “Where do I get my driving licence from?”, “Where do I get my driving lessons from?”, and what about homes? If you talk to Centrepoint and similar charities, most of the care leavers from residential sofa-surf. There are so many things that could be helped, but it really needs the dots joining up. To me, that is the best outcome we could give a child.

Dr Mark Kerr: As I said, I just want to give an opening statement to posit the questions that you are going to ask in a wider context. I lived in children’s homes as a child and subsequently completed four multidisciplinary degrees focused on children’s social care. That culminated in my doctorate, which examined policy for children’s social care with a very distinct focus on residential. I have had about 20 years since then, researching, working and challenging the system, so I care passionately about the residential sector, which has the potential to be transformative and give a child a life that they would have never had without it.

Although I am representing the sector today, my concerns extend far beyond its needs; the current risks that are facing children, providers, local authorities, the state and society are more acute than ever. We are in the middle of a storm, and successive Governments have contributed to this by failing to steward the sector in line with evidence and the changing needs. Although there is conviction in terms of change, a lot of it is not underpinned by evidence. Without an implementation plan, a workforce and a budget, which is the current situation, it is all very wishful thinking.

I just want to remind members of the committee how we got to this point. Back in 2012, in a previous Government, the Education Secretary, Michael Gove, set out that he believed that more children should be taken into care, and he also called for the care system to expand to be able to accommodate them. Then fast forward to David Cameron, who in a keynote speech—it is rare for children in care to feature in macro policy—set out a plan to break state monopolies, to bring in new providers and open services to competition, which shaped the sector that we have today. Yet just last week, the Children’s Minister outlined that he wants a smaller, more purposeful role for residential care.

We have shifted from the sector being asked to expand, based on one political lens, and now to contract. The extraordinary thing about the policy pendulum is not that it swings but that we seem to forget what happened last time. We have been here before. In 1998, Parliament warned that residential care had been cut back too far after a significant retrenchment in the early 1990s, yet, 30 years later, we are looking to do it again, although we already have a smaller residential sector than that, proportionately. We are at a critical juncture, and I fear that current policy risks steering further into the storm than out.

Turning to your question on outcomes, I founded the Centre for Outcomes of Care, which is a charity, and our focus is on improving outcomes. Outcomes are distance travelled in what children’s needs are, and it is important to recognise that children in residential start from a different place from those in foster care. At the moment, we have a very diverse group of children in residential. You will probably be interested and will know about the children that are in illegal settings, in unregistered settings, et cetera, who will have the worst outcomes—somewhere between 700 and 1,000. We have been analysing these children. To give context for them, if we look at where they came from—we have been really trying to understand that to be able to accommodate them, so if I look back in history and we go back 20-odd years—we had around 3,200 children in youth justice settings. Today we have 383, which is a drop of 88%. Where have those children gone? We have seen a drop of 15% in improved tier 4 mental health beds, which is a significant drop on its own. However, I do not know whether members will be aware that there has been a 65% increase in acute mental health admissions to hospitals.

That percentage masks a number of 39,000. Eating disorders alone have gone up by 515%. These are the children that the sector is grappling with caring for while receiving significant criticism. Those children will feel the most difficult outcomes, and at the moment they are poor because the sector cannot function to meet their needs. The lifetime costs that we have been modelling for that cohort each year represents about a £5.3 billion exposure to the Treasury.

Outcomes for a lot of children are fantastic. They really are tremendous and they have the life that they would not have had. They transition and they go on to stable lives—college, university. Younger ones go into foster care, hopefully, or return home. But it is not one sector. We are going from what is the equivalent of an A&E crisis—ICUs, down to out-patients—and we do not have enough of that top level. We are fine in the middle—in fact, we have too many, as we will come on to discuss. Outcomes are great for some but there is a huge catastrophic failure for others. Apologies for the long-winded answer—that is the only one that you will get from me at such length.

Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson: Thank you so much. That was incredibly helpful, Dr Kerr. Mr Isaac, I wonder if I could push you a little bit. Your answer really focused on what happens after children leave residential care at 18 and on the support post 18. Could I ask you for your reflections on what happens to them before 18 when they are in children’s social care, about the outcomes, and whether you think that, in the market, as Dr Kerr was very clear about saying, there are some children we are catastrophically failing and other children who are doing really well?

Andrew Isaac: There is a point I want to bring out with you. If you go back over a period of 10 or 12 years, for example, the number of 14 to 17 year-olds entering care has gone up by 43%, and those are domestic entries—there are some blips with asylum-seeking children, but that is if you strip those out. If you reflect back, it started at the time when Early Starts were reduced dramatically. An Early Start situation helps young families, young children and young parents and puts them on the right road in the first place.

By the time youngsters get to 14 or 15 and are in a residential home, there is a lot of work to be done around them. I agree with Mark; a huge amount of work is done to stabilise them and get them interested and engaged in education and in what will happen to their future, which is why I think you have to look before and after 18; that is terribly important. We also need to address the early years. If you can stop them going into residential care in the first place, then that makes a difference to the family environment. As we all know, fostering, if a child is in care, really is the best setting for them, and it is the most cost effective in a family environment.

Baroness Nichols of Selby: Sorry, I have just a few questions. Going back to the point about when they leave care at 18, do you think that part of the preparation should be done before they get anywhere near the age of 18? You mentioned driving licences and driving lessons. Those could be done while they are in the residential care setting; people could look at that.

You mentioned as well that the worst outcomes seem to be from unregistered homes. I wonder what evidence you have around that. We have taken evidence on that, and it has disturbed us all that we have children in settings that are not registered—it really has had an impact. My own local authority initiated the “no wrong door” policy for children, where it does not matter where they turn up and it is dealt with by that particular person. Could that be something to look at?

The last thing is that residential really is not the right place for a lot of the children, because they are egging each other on—I suppose that is the phrase—and it is very difficult for people who want to get on. Should there be more support for families in their own homes? The Sure Start project was really good, and the changes to that have impacted all this. I also wonder whether there are fewer and fewer foster carers because the children may have more and more complex needs.

Andrew Isaac: I totally agree with you on several of your points. Foster caring is the best environment for them, yes. There is a decrease in the number of foster carers available in the independent and public sectors. I do not know whether it is that they are scared of managing this increase in age: if you take a 14 to 17 year-old now, they are very independent in their thinking and their thoughts, and, sadly, some of them know their way around the system extremely well. It is very difficult for people to want to be able to manage that. That is point number one.

The second point you had was on the cohort in residential care now. The anecdotal things that I have heard are that 20% of children who are in residential care should not be in there. That is to do with the system itself: there is a bed there, they put them in there, and that is it. Not only is there a cost factor there, but I do not think it is any good for them. When they then get to 16, it is quite right that they should be coached in life skills and where they are going; it does not always happen, nor do they always have the right mentoring scheme.

I do not think unregistered provision is the answer. It needs to be registered and monitored. I can give you an example from one of our CSDG members. Because they were concerned about this, they set up a hub and spoke system in Greater Manchester for accommodating about 21 to 25 young people. The average stay is probably 80 to 90 days. Within that, they have residential people there in the hub 24/7, who act as sort of mentors to each individual as to what they need. As a result of that, there is more engagement in education, further education, vocational training and life skills. Then, once they are already, there is a helping hand back into society. If we start adopting that in other parts of the country, it is almost like a step down from residential but not taking somebody between 16 and 18, putting them in some sort of lodging and leaving them to it. We would not do it with our own children, and I do not think that for those within the Government’s responsibility it should happen that way either. Did that cover your points?

Baroness Redfern: My notes mention “inadequate help from both children’s and adults’ services … upon turning 18”. I think you mentioned that in your introduction. What extra support can be given for children who are outside their area and want to come back? Every young person needs a place of their own to help them with education and possibly employment. That is an area I am very concerned about. What can local authorities work together to try to improve, so that young people can possibly get a place of their own and have a first stable part of their life?

Andrew Isaac: Using the statistics that are available at the moment, roughly about 85% of children’s homes are within the independent sector, one-third of local authorities have no children’s homes, and one-third have probably a couple. In total, the balance is probably wrong, but that is historic: the independent sector was asked to fill the gap in. I would be encouraged if local authorities, either through the RCCs or in groups themselves, would work with the independent sector. By default, the intellectual capability and the property vests in the independent sector; they are the ones actually doing the work, particularly with very challenging young people. It is about working together to develop some of the innovation, such as the hub and spoke scheme, and developing them locally. You should have a planned transition so that, when they get to 16, they are carefully managed until 18 to move into a hub and spoke where they learn the life skills. While I have not read Alan Milburn’s report from yesterday in detail, it would fit in nicely with that—in taking forward how are we going to get them into more training, vocational training or basically into jobs. They want to become committed members of society.

If you take some of the outcomes from children in fostering, I know that children who entered the fostering system have got double firsts from Cambridge, or have been fostered—and come from overseas as well—and are now doctors and lawyers. That is because they are nurtured through those early years in a family environment. Post that, we need to nurture them at the next stage. Sorry if that is a bit complicated. I feel that, if we could work with the public sector in geographical areas, that would help.

Baroness Redfern: It would help with the total amount of suicides, which you mentioned. The number is so very high. They have no hope and no prospects.

Dr Mark Kerr: We get fixated on chronological age rather than where these children and young people are in their development. Some are far advanced and vote with their feet; some, through pre-care experience, et cetera, have that delayed development—not as in a learning disability, but they are that bit behind, particularly those who come in as late entrants. We have a limited time. It has been referred to for a long time as the care cliff. The reality is that, on local authority funding, a lot of the things these children and young people need are non-statutory. On the statutory minimum, there is lots of signposting that they are expected to do for housing and health, but on the actual delivery of services, if we go back to one of the core mantras for children’s social care from Frank Dobson, would this be good enough for my child? We have a boomerang generation, where children and young people are leaving much later and then potentially coming back. For children and young people leaving care, it is very varied across the country. Somebody asked whether we start earlier than 18—yes, we do. From about 14, a lot of the organisations start some of the softer preparation for independent skills.

On the question of whether they can come back, we do get into statutory problems here with housing. That is where it crosses over into housing: care leavers in most boroughs and LAs qualify for priority housing but only in their home area. There is some discretionary housing, if they are placed out of area.

There is also another problem. We are talking about those who want to come back, but there are also those who do not want to come back—those who have been placed out of area. I used to be at the University of Kent for many years, and Kent, for example, used to get a lot of kids placed from London. They have been there for several years, their friendship networks and homes are there, and they are at college there. They hit 18 and Kent says, “Well, unfortunately, we can’t deliver the leaving care statutory duties for you. You need to go back to Bromley, west London or wherever, because they have the statutory duty, which we do not”. This is a real problem with the localised statutory duties. We have the regional approach, but I do not know whether that is going to happen. It is a huge problem, and we have not caught up with changes in society. I think that the average age at which they leave care is still 17.

Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson: I just want to pick up on what Mr Isaac said about the relationship with the public sector. I would be interested to hear your view on how well local authorities interact with independent providers and where it works well and where it works badly. Often, one of the criticisms of the public sector is that there is not enough procurement commissioning expertise to hold very strong relationships with the independent sector, and that the independent sector has much more expertise than the public sector on contractual renegotiation or trying to work out the appropriate balance. Could you say more about how you see those relationships between the public sector and the independent sector? Dr Kerr, I would also be interested to hear your views on that in relation to your members.

Andrew Isaac: In the first instance, certainly from the perspective of CSDG’s members, where they are present in a geographical area, they will go and visit the local authority to talk about their future sufficiency: “What is it you need and what type do you need? What is the level that you need that home to be at?” Once that is in place, that is the start of the relationship. There are two problems. One is that the changes in local authority structures happen quite regularly, so the relationship-building can be challenging. You probably need to have direct contact with the person above them and the person below them, to try to maintain some sort of continuity.

The second part is then establishing a children’s home in a geographical location. We get into the remit of Ofsted, which is the appropriate body to manage this sector. However, the registration periods can sometimes be quite extensive and the local planning laws can be challenging. In some instances, we have had members in a community where the local authority, the police and the community are objecting to a children’s home for three or four children.

The whole process is quite extensive, but if you look at the numbers of new children’s homes opening—there were 674 in 2025—you see that 92% of them were either for single owners or groups under four. There is only a small percentage for large providers. That can work only where there are relationships with local authorities.

We have to add into the mix the start of the regional care co-operatives now. The trouble you have with that is Sections 22C and 22G of the Children Act. I know the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act amended it with Section 22J, but it is the responsibility of the local authority to provide the appropriate accommodation for the child, no matter what the circumstances are. Are those RCCs robust enough with everybody joining in? At the moment, they are voluntary, and there is no ministerial directive for them to come together.

The Chair: We will come back to that a little later.

Dr Mark Kerr: For me, this is really various. We have strong proactive partnerships with some of the LAs we work with and some of the regional approaches. West Sussex, for example, have a very strong provider collaborative; they work up close with their providers, who are responsive. There is trust and a relationship between them. But there are several other areas where it is borderline toxic. The narrative and the rhetoric that is being pushed out very often from the County Councils Network is that everybody, apparently, is profiteering—the whole sector is profiteering. That is materially untrue; it can be evidenced that it is not true. They take some single outlier numbers and then create this toxic atmosphere, which then feeds into commissioners.

I never thought, in my role, that I would be coming to the defence of local authorities so often, but they are broke. They are negotiating with some who may have ideological views: “I would like to use this charitable provider or this not-for-profit provider. I don’t want to use this tax-haven provider; I want to use something else”. This comes down to one thing: the fee. Even the Lancashire LA, which has its problems with the saturations of children’s homes, has been very clear that, once it identifies homes that can be a potential match for a child that needs a placement, the lowest fee gets the child. As I said, I understand that context, but it has a similar feeling to 2011, 2012 and 2013, when austerity started. That is the situation that we are in. I do not see it improving. Clearly, fees are going to go up significantly—and that is not profiteering; that is the changing nature of input costs, which I am sure we will come on to.

The reality is that local authorities are moving children in an unplanned way based on fee. Every week, we have providers come to us suddenly saying that they are moving the child. We know the reason: because it is based on a fee, they can make savings by moving a child from here to there. I sympathise, but it is the children who lose out in this.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Thank you for coming to see us this morning. Andrew, you touched on the subject that I want to raise with you: Ofsted delays. I think it was also in your written evidence. As a relative newcomer to this area, I was shocked to discover that there are delays on registrations of up to 18 months.

Andrew Isaac: Absolutely.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Can you tell us a bit more about why you think that has come about and what can be done to deal with it?

Andrew Isaac: It has been an undercurrent for many years. I think it might probably be to do with the need to review the process. Perhaps Ofsted needs to look at how it goes about it. Part of what happens when you have permission to open a home is that you then do the physical building work and get it into an appropriate state. Then you have to employ a registered manager and the staff team; they all need to be in place and trained prior to full registration being granted. Again, what we would like to see there is Ofsted working with those who are opening new homes, so as to get that process down—six months would be difficult. The operator or the provider would have to take huge financial costs until that home is being used.

If it is a particularly specialist home and you are investing in the staff training, which needs to be quite established, and then there are no children in the home, the staff themselves are going to ask, “Why am I here? When is all this going to happen?” There is a tipping point where they say, “Oh, I could go and work for so-and-so and practice what I’ve been trained for”. It is probably a bigger problem than I have an answer for, but, again, I hope that Ofsted would want to work with the independent sector to try to put it into a perspective where it is not just a tick-box exercise but part of the reality of life.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: How have they allowed for delays to mount up in the way that they have?

Dr Mark Kerr: We work closely with Ofsted. First of all, at a leadership and a senior leadership level, there is a commitment here, and we agree on 90% of this. I can give you theories, but I can also give you consequences. The theory for me is Ofsted resource. In the space of just over five years, we have gone from 2,500 to 5,000 children’s homes. I am not sure whether you have taken evidence from Ofsted yet; I would be interested to know whether its budget and resources have increased in the same proportion.

I know that, about a year ago, when push came to shove, it had to make a decision on whether to focus on inspections or registrations with the resources it had. The decision was made to focus on inspections, which is probably right—it wants to keep children safe. But one of the things we have been saying to them is that the reality is that residential care is the most scrutinised and inspected sector that there is. It is proposed that it is going to extend the frequency—the time between inspections—of LAs from three years to four years. Children’s homes are inspected every year. Fostering is not; no other sector gets that level of scrutiny. If it was to say that children’s homes that are good or outstanding—those it does not have a concern with—go to two years, it would free up resource. But, politically, that does not seem to be permitted.

The delays are real. It varies around the country. Bear in mind that 16 weeks was the target timeline. There is another real issue. Stopping the high-need, high-risk children—the ones in unregulated, which we touched on earlier—feeds into this. One of the reasons providers we need to open up which could meet the needs of those children currently in illegal settings—let us call them what they are—will not open is because of Ofsted delays, the Ofsted environment and the inspection regulations for those children.

But we have quantified the cost. A lot of cost-shunting goes on in children’s social care. Those lifetime costs I talked about—worker pensions, health, et cetera—go to the Treasury and not the local authorities. But our reasonably informed modelling—we have many members and we know the delays—says that the Ofsted delays are potentially putting on a cost of about £15,000 a month. That has to be absorbed initially but will then go on to a fee, which means that, over 12 months, this amounts to around £180,000. This could equate, for an average home, to around £687 a week, on top of the new fee that we have now. There is a cost implication for this, but Ofsted does not bear that: it goes to the local authorities’ fee.

The other fundamental problem that we have is that we know we want a strong, rigorous safeguarding process in place, but we need a regulatory framework that distinguishes between these different cohorts of children. The primary need for the children that I talked about earlier on, the ones who will go on to cost the state billions, is emotional and psychological health. I would question whether the Office for Standards in Education is the right department to be inspecting it; I would say that that is questionable. Children’s social care has been a hot potato over the years. It has been around government—under the Home Office, and under the Department of Health, where it made the most strides—and it is under the Department for Education with the Office for Standards in Education. Is it equipped to deal with the child that should be in, or is coming out of, a tier 4 mental health setting? These are all key problems we have in the system and, unfortunately, local authorities are picking up the tab and having to put them in unregulated inspection settings. Who regulates the regulator? We have the problem, and it must be grasped, because the consequences are severe at the moment.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Would you both agree that the children with the most complex needs are worst affected by these delays?

Andrew Isaac: Yes, and it is compounded as they grow up. One solution is not difficult: the Department for Education is Ofsted’s paymaster. One of the quicker solutions could be to increase the resources available to Ofsted to fast-track some of these registrations, particularly in areas where there is a need for it.

Dr Mark Kerr: But fast track now is longer than the original, normal registration. I have a tangible case now where I cannot get Ofsted to move. We have a GP who already has one home for children with significant mental health problems, and there is a second home. They have been waiting for months—there is a child waiting to come out of a tier 4 bed. This is in Staffordshire. Ofsted will not register them quickly. If it wanted to, it could do it in a week. There are these consequences. A lot of this is: horse, stable, door, bolted. It really annoys me when we have children who are stuck in institutions and could be coming out because of Ofsted delays. This has been a long-standing problem. We have been making the representations to them and Government for a considerable amount of time. We are not short of placements here.

The problem is, about a year ago, there were supposed to be changes coming into legislation that would have restricted the opening of homes. As I say, we have gone from 2,500 to 5,000. We have about 17,000 registered beds. There are only 9,500 children in residential care. There are 1,000 more homes in the queue for Ofsted registration, which will be 2,000 to 3,000 beds. We are going to see catastrophic failure across the residential sector. We have lots in that middle level of homes for EBD—emotional behavioural difficulties—but nobody will open our A&E and ICU wards because of registration delays. There are going to be failures. The sector cannot run with only twice as many beds as it needs. The Government and Ofsted between them have let this go. Last July, at our conference, when we had Ofsted and the DfE there, I said, “Are you going to announce the changes to restrict the registrations?” This growth has not come from experienced providers but from property developers and speculators with no practice experience. The horse has bolted, and we are going to have to feel the consequences.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Is there any way in which the inspection process can be speeded up? Is the problem that it does not have enough inspectors or that the process is too long and drawn out?

Dr Mark Kerr: I do not think it has enough resources. The reality is that an inspection takes two days and then writing up, so three days. We could inspect every two years: if there are no concerns about a home, why should we be inspecting every year?

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: You made that point.

Dr Mark Kerr: But my point is that that would free up resource. An inspector takes three days, but a registration visit takes only one day, so they could register three homes in the three days that they were not tied up in inspections. There are solutions. They just need political will.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: I want to ask a supplementary on the 17,000 registered beds. We know children do better in foster care—you have said that, and we have had advice from several areas. Why are people still investing in new homes that require Ofsted inspections in the environment that you have just described?

Dr Mark Kerr: Can I just qualify that, only if the children are suitable for foster care, and a lot are not—

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: I realise that.

Dr Mark Kerr: We would like every child in a family environment. The solution to this is fewer children in care, just to clarify. I do not understand—

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: If I look at healthcare—I am a mental health nurse by background—people are not building lots of private hospitals at the moment, because they know that they could not fill them. In other words, I am quite comfortable that people keep inspecting current homes, because I want the standards to be right. Two years seems perfectly reasonable to me, rather than annually. Dr Mark Kerr: My only theory is that there are people with properties who need to find another purpose for them, or people have not drilled into the data. I have had two careers: my early career, before I went back to university, was in hospitality. There are two jobs that people think are easy from the other side: running a pub and running a children’s home. People learn the hard way. There are property investors, changes in taxation and landlords—people are looking at what other things they can do with their property.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: Thank you very much for such an honest answer.

I am a little bit out of date—I do not think it has changed much—but if you are to be pursuing apprenticeship or some other vocational route that does not give a full salary, you cannot afford to pay rent but you also cannot claim benefits because you are not available for work. There is a real chasm between them.

Obviously the reality is that children in care also do not have the social capital of parents helping them—you know, getting them a network. I actually sit on Southwark Council’s Corporate Parenting Committee, not far from here, and I am constantly challenging them, saying, “You are the biggest employer around here, or one of the biggest employers. You can be the corporate parent”. It is about ensuring that there is consideration that they do not have the normal social network around them. Benefit rules may need to be changed to allow them to pursue vocational routes. A lot of them are the furthest from the labour market. We just need to recognise their personal situation. They do not have the traditional Mum and Dad and cannot stay in the spare room while they pursue their apprenticeship. We need to make sure there are no barriers through the benefit system to discourage them.

Mental and emotional health support as well is a key thing for a lot of them. There are some great organisations. There is one called Radical Recruit in London that does fantastic work with children leaving care, and it does the work with the employers. These kids are coming with a different kind of journey behind them. If you educate the employers, there is a lot more chance of compassion and patience and understanding from them.

There is a lot of work that can be done. We have asked, “What do we know that we can solve?” That is one area we can solve. We know what to do.

The Chair: A final question: you have touched on many of the major problems within the area that we are considering. You may feel you have already said this, but what would you prioritise to improve the outcomes of children in the short term? What would be your key changes you would like to see implemented?

Andrew Isaac: From my perspective—and in no particular order, is how I should say it—it would be the development of the staffing base for the whole sector, and that includes fostering, residential and special needs education. That is critical for its success going forward. I also suggest a serious look at looked-after children going through to the age of 25, particularly those with disabilities, and particularly where we move them in a transition from having a corporate parent to being in charge of themselves. Those two particular things for me would help solve an awful lot as, ancillary to that, I would absolutely ask local authorities to work very closely with the experts that are available within the independent sector, as the independent sector will want to try and work with the experts in the local authority. We are in the same boat trying to achieve the same thing. We do not really need internal battles.

Dr Mark Kerr: One of the biggest things is to stop thinking about structures and ownership, and instead start with the needs of children. We really need to start that if we are going to rebuild the system in the short and long term. There needs to be a coherent national response to those children that are falling between the gaps at the moment. It is a small number of them, but the cost implications for the local authorities are massive. The long cost to the Treasury and the state is huge, and the cost to the children is immeasurable. There is a cohort there that requires a commitment with cross-department and Ofsted support. That should be the immediate priority. There is good work going on from the Department of Education with their Home Again programme, but we need to do more and faster. As I say, there needs to be joint responsibility between the NHS, the Ministry of Justice, Ofsted and local authorities for how we respond to them, because these children used to be in closed settings but nobody made a plan. It is great that they are not in youth justice settings any more, but nobody made a plan for where they were going to go instead.

My second priority would be a national programme to end the use of illegal children’s homes. We have an objective at the Children’s Homes Association that we should not have a child in an illegal home by 2030. Those two areas do overlap.

My third priority is a more sophisticated understanding of the capabilities of children’s residential. We talk about capacity all the time, but capacity is not capability, so we really need to have a better understanding of what the specialisms are of children’s homes around the country and try and match that to the needs. There are two sides to the equation.

Overall, it really is about understanding the children better and cross-department support to make sure the sector responds.
