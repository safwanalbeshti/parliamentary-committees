Q1 Chair: My first question is to Josh: how far does the public perception of high street decline reflect the reality on the ground?

Josh Nicholson: At the Centre for Social Justice we have just conducted a massive nine-month inquiry into the state of crime and disorder on the high street, looking at illicit trade, dodgy shops, shoplifting and ASB. There is a real perception that crime is out of control. We know from polling that 79% of adults are concerned about the decline of the high street. Nearly nine in 10 say they believe that crime and ASB are big issues in their community, so there is a growing perception.

Some will point to declining crime rates over time. This perception is a bit of a mismatch with the reality, but what we have seen in our report is that there has been a significant increase in what is termed so-called low-level crime, volume crime, that is incredibly visible to the public on the high street. So-called dodgy shops or, as we have called them in our report, criminal high street businesses are a real symptom of that. We have seen 1,000 more shops raided for illicit tobacco. That is what our FOIs found last year compared with the year before.

Part of the reason that perception has increased is that there is a felt lack of consequence. What repeatedly came up in our work was that the public feel that the high street has become a consequence-free zone, where crime is able to go unpunished and is increasing. We know that that is true, because nine in 10 criminal high street businesses stay open even after they have been raided for illicit tobacco. So fewer than one in 10 are closed—just 8%. That is from our FOIs to all local authorities with responsibilities for trading standards.

This is therefore a problem that is perceived to be great, in terms of crime, disorder and decline—that link between crime and decline is particularly important—and the reality is that it is true as well. Tackling that sense of consequence-free high streets is crucial to any attempt to drive renewal through the high street strategy. That has been our core ask.

Q2 Chair: I want to go back to your point; you said that there is a perception that crime goes unpunished. The Government are looking at giving councils more powers. What do you think those powers should look like, and do you think that that is actually going to address the issues that you have highlighted?

Josh Nicholson: We very much welcome the Government’s ambition on tackling both the visible decline on the high street and criminal high street businesses. The planning changes are welcome, but we feel that they need to go further.

This debate can often get tied up in aesthetics and what we want our high street to be: “What sort of shops do we want?” Actually, the issue with the shops that are associated with crime, be they vape shops, mini-marts, barbers or nail salons, is not that they are kind of aesthetically non-ideal, or that we want different sorts of shops on our high street; it is that there is criminality associated with them. It is about the crime that is going on.

The Government have made a welcome change to closure orders, with the length of time increasing to 12 months—that is a great first step—and they have hinted that they are looking at new powers. One of the core recommendations in our report was for a much stronger closure order power to tackle crime when it occurs in those shops. As we found, fewer than one in 10 are closed, so there needs to be a stronger tool for the police and trading standards to use to shut down those shops when crime occurs.

We have said that part of that should be an instant closure order, which should not include the magistrates court in the first instance. At the moment, closure orders have to go through the magistrates court, which can add significant delays. We have said that trading standards and the police should have a tool, similar to the article 31 prohibition orders that the fire service has, to allow for the instant shutting down of those shops, obviously with a route of appeal where that is necessary. We need to make sure that those shops can be closed much more quickly, and reduce the burden of evidence; when the law is broken on more than one occasion, that should warrant the use of those powers for a certain period. That new closure power, which the Government have hinted at looking at, is really needed and is what we are recommending in our conversations with them.

Q3 Sarah Smith: This is such an important issue, certainly in Accrington and across Hyndburn. Josh, I think there is also a recommendation in your report for an independent review of HMRC’s methodology for estimating the illicit tobacco market. HMRC’s duty gap estimate and UCL’s survey of smokers are reporting illicit purchasing differently, so what further evidence would help policymakers to understand the scale of illicit trade and its impact on high streets and legitimate retailers, to build that evidence base for the sorts of steps that you are proposing?

Josh Nicholson: That is a great question. HMRC would say that there is huge uncertainty around its estimates of the scale of the illicit market, which is a massive problem when we are trying to understand how this issue is affecting the sale of illicit tobacco and vapes on the high street. If we cannot even estimate the true scale of the market, that is a real problem. That was behind our recommendation for HMRC to commit to a revision of its methodology.

For example, other estimates have indicated that about one in three cigarettes smoked in the UK are illicit. That is a very different estimate. UCL has done its own analysis on this as well, and it has quite different estimates from HMRC. It is therefore really important that HMRC commits to a revision of, or a consultation on revising, its methodology. There are other ways of trying to estimate the scale of the illicit tobacco market, such as through empty-pack surveys, which is what some in the industry do, to estimate what is going on. That is absolutely an important part of the process of us understanding what is going on on the high street.

Q4 Chair: As you know, the Government have announced they are looking to do a consultation on that. There have been quite a number of reviews over the years. What specifically would you like to see from the Government on that?

Josh Nicholson: Stronger closure order powers are absolutely necessary to tackle that problem. We need to see the consultation on licensing for tobacco and vape shops brought forward. Obviously, the planning changes are welcome in giving communities a greater say in what shops set up, but licensing for tobacco and vape retailers is incredibly important here, because that has the opportunity to become a much stronger conduit for enforcement. If you are selling illicit tobacco, violating your licence or selling without a licence at all, it gives local authority teams a much easier way of enforcing the law. If you are selling without a licence or in breach of your licence on repeated occasions, that should warrant the closure of your premises. It should lead to prosecution for those offenders who are not engaging with enforcement. That will be a crucial part of the arsenal available to police and trading standards.

That consultation has been announced, and we are expecting it in the new year. We have said that it needs to be brought forward much more urgently, before the year is out, because that will be the crucial way of increasing enforcement capabilities to ensure that shops are abiding by the law and to tackle those rogue retailers.

Q5 Chair: That is really helpful. Just so that I understand that better, do you feel that enforcement would help shift the dial on perception and trust, to help improve the high street?

Josh Nicholson: Absolutely. The core issue that we are seeing on the high street is that the public simply do not trust that there are consequences for crime. That is reflected in the data on this issue of illicit trade. Increasing the powers available to enforcement agencies is vital. That means stronger closure orders and robust licensing for tobacco and vape retailers. We have outlined a range of other measures, for example, stronger penalties to tackle landlords where they are complicit in making money from criminal activity.

Lord Bichard: Perhaps I could just pick up some of the things that Josh has been talking about, because I agree with nearly all of it, and pick up Sarah Smith’s point about data. There is enough data already for people to realise that we have a serious problem here. At the end of the day, you can only go so far with data. At the end of the day, it is down to policy will, and certainly in trading standards we would like to see a bit more of that.

The problem on the ground that we see is that a lot of people think what is happening is quite low-level crime, but it is not. It is increasingly linked to organised crime, child exploitation, money laundering and immigration. I could go on. One of the things that we want to do, and that the Government could do more of to help us, is bring home to people that when they buy illicit tobacco they are actually funding child abuse. Not many people—I am not being flippant; I have a history around child abuse—would want to fund that. The way in which we communicate that should not be about “This tobacco may not be safe” or “You’re funding dodgy providers,” but about where that money is going.

The reality is not understood. That is the first point I would make. In terms of the changes, I completely agree with closure orders. Closure orders need someone to have a good look at them again, because they were brought in under antisocial and disorderly conduct, and what we are worried about is crime. You cannot even get a closure notice if it is just about crime; you have to show that it is disorderly conduct or some antisocial behaviour, and that is ridiculous. The Government need to get on to that sooner rather than later.

The practical side of crime on the high street does not seem to be fully understood. By that, I mean there is no real action we can take against landlords. There are a lot of landlords out there who do not look very closely at who they are letting premises to, and unless there is some accountability for that, they will carry on doing that. People do not seem to understand the issue of multiple premises. With help, we can shut down a shop for a time, but that owner probably has two or three other shops and they will just move the stuff to those two or three other shops. We need closure orders that take account of multiple premises and of the fact that a lot of shopkeepers now stock their goods off premises. If you raid a place, you get a small amount. Most of the stock is in a car somewhere down the road—I kid you not—or in some other premises that is leased by someone who does not appear to have any connection with the people who own the shop.

There does not seem to be a practical understanding of how this criminal behaviour works, and you need that if you are going to make successful legislation. I am afraid it is the same old story with Whitehall, and sometimes with Westminster, that policymakers tend not to have a sufficient connection to the people on the ground. I will leave it at that for the moment.

Q6 Andrew Cooper: Welcome, Jim, and thank you for joining us this morning. You have been running quite a long investigation through London Centric about phoenixing and enforcement. You wrote an interesting piece for the Financial Times recently about what Andy Burnham should do if he wants to get a grip of this issue. Many people assume that dodgy shops are a front for money laundering. What did your investigation find?

Jim Waterson: London Centric is focused on the capital, but I think it is relevant to people whether they are in Accrington, Hertfordshire or Cheshire, like you. We set out to go, “These dubious gift shops—these Harry Potter shops, these royal souvenir shops—all over central London are in some of the most expensive retail properties in the world. These places have six-figure rents. What is going on?” Like most people, we thought, “This must be money laundering. No one with any taste or decency would be spending money in there.” Then we went in and found something else. We found that a lot of people were spending money in there. So what is actually going on here? What is the trick?

The more time we spent looking at it, we kept finding tax evasion. It came back time and time again to the fact that if you do not pay VAT or corporation tax and simply opt out of your PAYE obligations and all the other things that any small business owner has to do, you have a much more profitable business. If you have a much more profitable business, you can pay a higher rent than someone who is paying their taxes. It comes back to this time and time again. In every single example we looked at, we kept finding this. The reason these shops are springing up everywhere is that bad pushes out good.

I really want to explain the extent to which, having talked to dozens of people in this trade, from the suppliers to the shop owners to the landlords, they all come back to the idea that they have largely realised, particularly post-pandemic, that paying tax as a high-street enterprise is optional. Once you get over that mental hurdle, you have the ability to repeatedly phoenix your company and discard previous limited companies. There is a ready supply of patsy directors willing to sign the paperwork for a few hundred quid. Crucially, as has been alluded to here, landlords have no responsibility beyond collecting the maximum possible rent. They have almost no other legal duties. If someone comes and says, “I can offer you £15,000 a month for this unit”, and someone who wants to pay their taxes says, “If I pay my taxes, there’s no way I could pay more than £10,000 a month in rent”, they are going to go with the person who pays £15,000 a month. It is just the way the world works at the moment and the incentives that they respond to.

Q7 Andrew Cooper: So you would argue that these shops are reducing the number of voids on the high street, which is supporting higher rents and squeezing out legitimate operators.

Jim Waterson: Absolutely. Could I talk the Committee through an example? The landlord of one of the biggest gift shops in the UK, which is right at Piccadilly Circus, is a major London landlord called Criterion Capital. They are completely within their rights to rent to who they wish, and they have repeatedly chosen to rent this shop to a series of limited companies that are run by 20-somethings with no retail background. Every few months, it shuffles through to a new limited company. The previous one gains the responsibility for all the taxes—the council tax, the business rates, the VAT, all of that. Then every few months that company is discarded in favour of a new operator.

We at London Centric often try and track down who these people are. You go and knock on the doors of the directors, who are supposedly overseeing maybe millions of pounds a year in revenue. They are often students in overcrowded accommodation, or people who have just arrived in the UK and are in a vulnerable position; in one case, we found that that gift shop I referenced at Piccadilly Circus, which must have a six-figure rent and substantial taxes to that effect, was being run by someone who gave their home address as a disused car park in Canary Wharf. I get the feeling that these people aren’t the ones who are actually in control of the business.

Q8 Andrew Cooper: Often you hear as a solution to this problem that we need to beef up directors’ duties and there needs to be real jeopardy if you get caught. But that is not going to be a solution if you are just finding an impoverished student and putting them up as a patsy to run the business.

Jim Waterson: The unfortunate truth is that I think if I walked out of here with 200 quid in cash and went up to enough people, I could find someone to set up a new limited company in their name and pass all the ID checks by the end of this.

Q9 Andrew Cooper: I know your investigation was centred on London, but are you aware of how widespread phoenixing is across England? And what is your remedy to stop it?

Jim Waterson: For phoenixing as a whole, because it is hard to break down what is a high-street issue and what is to do with phoenixing in other forms such as construction or whatnot, HMRC’s latest figures show at least around £1 billion in 2022-23 was being lost to phoenixing, and that might have gone up in more recent years.

I just see it time and again. When a unit has had three or four tenants who have all not paid their taxes in the last year or two, you can see that there is a problem here.

Landlords are not responsible. Quite understandably, they do not need to exercise any legal checks on who they are renting these things to at the moment. That is what is creating the problem.

Q10 Andrew Cooper: Which body in your view should be leading on action against high-street tax evasion? The National Audit Office found that HMRC doesn’t treat phoenixing as a strategic risk. It highlighted that HMRC and the Insolvency Service should be working together. Should it be one of those bodies or should it be another body entirely? What is your view?

Jim Waterson: When I talk to these dodgy shop owners, the one group that they really fear is HMRC. HMRC has the power and the ability to really dig into it, and that is what they are really scared of. If I talk to the operators, they say trading standards—it’s the cost of doing business; the council can’t really do much because the patsy director takes the blame. HMRC and its ability to go up the chain to the real people in control is what they fear, but HMRC is underfunded and does not have the staff.

Sue Nelson: I would not want to comment necessarily on the phoenixing side. I would back up what was said in relation to the licensing of tobacco and vapes. We are very keen to see that regime come in and be implemented in a sensible manner. We have fed into that process.

Q11 Chair: I want to go back to what Jim said—that HMRC is underfunded and does not have the staff. Do you know whether work has been done to see how many staff they would require to be able to deliver on this?

Jim Waterson: There is an interesting debate here. In terms of the total tax take, this might not be the biggest thing in HMRC’s remit. Dan Tomlinson, the Treasury Minister who briefly had responsibility for HMRC, did a lot of work on saying, “This matters in terms of the tax morale of the nation, that you are being seen to do this; even if it isn’t in financial terms your biggest priority, it is undermining confidence in the entire system.” I have talked to people and there does seem to be a sea change there.

But, fundamentally, a decade and a bit of austerity has undermined the ability to have people on the ground who just go, day by day. If I was anyone on this Committee, the first call I would make would be phoning up HMRC and saying, “Can you come to my local high street with me and have a go?”

Q12 Chris Curtis: You talk about the scale of HMRC. My understanding is that HMRC, if it goes into these buildings, does not have the same power as the police do to investigate. Is that correct? Is there anything we need to look at changing there?

Josh Nicholson: One stat that has been around for a while is that there are over 100 different bodies responsible for tackling organised crime in the UK, so we have an incredibly fragmented system. I remember going out with trading standards in one area. They had a multi-team day of action, which is great—all the agencies coming together—but I couldn’t help thinking, “This is incredibly inefficient.” It is why national police service reforms are incredibly important. They are crucial and we have recommended in our report that the Home Office follows through with those reforms.

Reforming our police is important. We have also recommended a broader review into the way that trading standards is structured. We feel that a more national enforcement body and approach would have a better joined-up way of tackling this issue. Bringing together those bodies is a broader issue that we need to be looking at as well.

Q13 Mr Mohindra: Just before I ask my question, I should say that Warner Bros is in my constituency, and so Harry Potter world as well. If those shops are properly licensed, we are fully supportive of the global brand that is “Harry Potter”.

Lord Bichard, on trading standards, the Department has said that it cannot be confident that trading standards are sufficiently funded and resourced to meet the challenge of illegal activity on the high street. What level of funding and new resource do trading standards teams require? Could you also tie in with the previous comments about how many different agencies are involved. What would you like to see and who needs to be leading the charge on sorting this out?

Lord Bichard: Trading standards are leading the charge on vapes and illicit tobacco, but we are not leading the charge on nail bars and things that are the responsibility of environmental health. How much more money do we need? I do not like going along to Committees such as this and saying that we need more money—it is not my instinct. However, trading standards have been starved and starved and starved of resources, to the point where one London borough last year had no trading standards officers—none at all. I am not going to tell you to give us a few more million and that it will be all right; we need a serious investment of resources in trading standards.

Coming back to Mr Cooper’s question, there is no one body that is going to resolve this problem, whatever you do. On Pride in Place, I was the instigator of “place” as a concept. I think that we need to have greater focus on places, and we need more working between agencies in a place to solve this problem. I say that having started my career doing prosecutions for trading standards. I am a very old man. One of the things that I learned from doing prosecution for trading standards was how important local intelligence, network, knowledge and contacts are. You know why that shop has suddenly become vacant. You also know where that owner’s other premises are and can work with others to take some action. If you set up a national trading standards organisation, you lose that.

I chair the national trading standards board. People have difficulty getting their head around that, but trading standards has actually taken the initiative and now has arrangements at a local, regional and national level. National trading standards deals with the big prosecutions that local authorities can no longer cope with because they take up to eight years. I am kidding you not—we have recently taken eight years on a prosecution because professional enablers, as they are now called, find ways of making sure that these cases go on and on and on.

I warned Josh I was going to disagree with him when we were sitting outside, but with great respect to him, I do not think national trading standards is the answer. You have got to maintain local knowledge and intelligence and couple it with quite unusual arrangements for Government, because we all like to have something that is centralised and we all like to have structural change. However, structural change very rarely delivers you anything. That is why I would defend what we have at the moment.

Q14 Mr Mohindra: Do you think that the current model is working?

Lord Bichard: It is working as well as it could possibly work. The relationship between local trading standards and the national board—I would say this wouldn’t I—is excellent and we work through local trading standards. We fund them. If they have a big case, we will fund them to take that case to fruition, but if it takes eight years, they would not be able to do it on their own. I think that it is working and we have had some huge successes recently.

I did not think that I would get towards the end of my career and judge my success on the length of sentences, but I have. We had a case of a rogue builder a couple of weeks ago where eight people got a combined sentence of 67 years. The courts are beginning to take it seriously, but the courts do not have resources either. They have never seen high streets—retail trading standards cases—as the priority. Some investment needs to go into the criminal justice system so that we can get cases through the courts quicker so that people see action being taken. However, please do not blame the people who are struggling on the ground to deliver what I think you want: a more attractive high street where people are not put off because when they go to the high street they meet pretty unattractive characters. I do not know if that answers your question.

Q15 Mr Mohindra: You have spoken about the money side of it. What about the powers?

Lord Bichard: Yes, the powers—absolutely. I agree with Josh. The closure order power is a case study. If you go for a closure notice because you know there is criminal activity on that premise, you are probably not going to get it. If you get it, it works for 72 hours, and then you have to go for a closure order. If you go for a closure order, it is not long enough at the moment, so that needs to be extended.

County councils cannot go for closure orders; it is only district councils and the police. Post local government reorganisation—if we ever are post local government reorganisation—that may not be a big issue, but most of us think that is going to be another three or four years, so in the meantime we would like county councils to have those powers. I know that might be controversial. I used to run a county council. Sorry—before you point it out, I was chief executive of a county council, which is different from running it.

There are things that you can do with closure orders and with landlords. There are all sorts of things that we could do to give more powers to the people who know what is happening on the ground. Sometimes, they get very frustrated that it seems to take so long to get legislation on to the statute book to deal with this problem. I think that is partly because people do not realise the crimes that we are talking about here.

Q16 Chris Curtis: To change tack a bit and think about planning powers, the Government have singled out vape and betting shops in recent policy announcements. I will come to Josh first, because you sort of touched on this in your first answer. Do you think that those are the right outlets to target, and why?

Josh Nicholson: They are, to the extent that criminality is associated with them. We know from polling of trading standards officers that, in some areas, over half of vape shops and minimarts are thought to be linked to organised crime groups, which is an astonishing stat. We are perhaps not making a mistake, but emphasising the wrong issue, when we talk about the aesthetics of a shop, be it a vape shop or a minimart, and think, “I just don’t want another vape shop or minimart.”

For us, the real problem is not the aesthetics. The planning powers are good but limited, in that they focus on allowing certain shops to set up, or they provide greater control over what shops set up, whereas the real issue, as Lord Bichard said, is the crime associated with these premises. We have to focus on tackling the criminality, which goes much deeper than illicit trade.

We have spoken about the resourcing of the criminal justice system and local authorities. Those things will take years to remedy, but there are powers that we can bring in in the immediate term. Planning powers are absolutely part of the equation, but we need really robust closure orders that allow for the instantaneous shutting down of these shops, on the spot. We should make it a police priority too. We should bring the police into this, alongside trading standards, so that we can do this quickly. We should mobilise all that resource, with stronger closure powers, tobacco and vapes licensing, and much more robust penalties for landlords. One of the things that we have considered is making landlords who have been warned about criminality but have taken no action liable for unpaid business rates. You can talk about broader reform of the business rates system, but in the short term that is something that we can consider doing.

Absolutely, vape shops and minimarts are the right retailers to go after, to the extent that criminality is associated with them. More broadly, we need to be focusing the conversation on the crime, rather than on the aesthetics of the high street, even though that is important too.

Q17 Chris Curtis: Let me take that on to the next stage. This is the interesting question. You have a Government talking about freeing up the planning process more broadly, but on high streets they want to be far more interventionist. That is probably where most of Parliament and political will is. Jumping back to first principles for a second, let us say that in a hypothetical world you have solved the criminality problem, and Jim’s tax avoidance problems are also solved with a new beefed-up HMRC. In that universe, do you think we should still be talking about more control over what goes on a local high street, through the planning process or any other process, or would you be fine letting the free market make those decisions?

Josh Nicholson: That is a great question, and that is why we have chosen to emphasise in our research the role of licensing for control rather than the planning process, because it works well when you allow the planning process to be as free as possible so that high streets can change and evolve over time. That is really crucial.

It is also why we have outlined in our report successful examples of where design codes have been used to de-risk development, for retail to residential conversion and for shopfront design codes, and how we could use a much stronger brownfield passport, as the Government have hinted at, to fast-track development. Using local development orders is one mechanism to increase and incentivise development on the high street and remove the hurdles that are often are in place for developers.

A welcome principle to have is that the planning system should be as free as possible to allow the high street to change over time while making sure that there is robust enforcement where we need it. That is why tobacco and vapes licensing for those retailers is crucial, because it would make sure that every retailer that is selling those products is required to have a licence, and the enforcement would therefore become much easier when you notice someone violating that or trading without one.

Q18 Mr Mohindra: As someone who introduced the Essex design guide, I totally agree with your point about making each high street different. The point I was going to make is about betting shops. Do you think it is unfair for the Government to single them out?

Josh Nicholson: This comes back to my point about emphasising the aesthetic of the high street and tackling the issue of vape shops and mini-marts through that lens—betting shops are included within that—rather than focusing on the underlying criminality. Obviously, betting shops are very different entities from criminal high street businesses, as we have referred to them—often seen in the form of minimarts or vape shops. We might not like betting shops, but they are not necessarily run by organised crime, and the organised criminal element absolutely has to be the priority.

That is why when we consider questions of vacancy and tolerating vacancy rates, obviously no one wants a high street full of vacant shops, but it is much more preferrable for there to be a vacancy in the short term than to tolerate organised crime on the high street, because that is what we are tolerating at the moment. We know this crime goes much deeper. We have spoken to charities that are supporting families whose kids are being exploited by the criminals running these shops and being given vapes that are laced with Spice and other forms of drugs. It is not just a few dodgy cigarettes being sold; this is much deeper organised crime, as Lord Bichard has pointed out.

Lord Bichard: A third of people in this country say they are putting off going to high streets because of what is happening on them—criminal behaviour and some of the shops that are there.

Mr Mohindra: As a former retailer, I get it.

Lord Bichard: Absolutely.

Q19 Lewis Cocking: Josh, I did not really understand your point about design codes and all the rest of it, because I do not think they go far enough. I think the Government are right to look at the planning system, because when I think about my high streets in Broxbourne, Cheshunt, Waltham Cross and Hoddesdon, they are all market towns, and people do not necessarily get annoyed with one vape shop, nail bar, Turkish barber or minimart; they get annoyed when it is like the town is taken over overnight. Suddenly you go from having none to having five vape shops all in the same market town, and you walk past them or all the barbers and there is no one in there, so you think, “How can they be making money?” I understand the organised crime part of that, but surely a quick solution to it is giving the council powers. Everyone thinks the council has powers anyway, and people moan to me, “Why has the council let another one of these open? Andrew Goodacre: Unfortunately, it is largely negative. I welcome that, since coming to power in 2024, the Government have talked about transforming and improving business rates. But effectively, they have tinkered around the edges, and we end up with unintended consequences.

For example, this year in a large retail park near where I live a large supermarket has seen its rateable value increase, but because of the changes to the system it will pay £70,000 less in business rates for each of the next three years.
