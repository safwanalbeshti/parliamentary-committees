Chair: Welcome to the Public Accounts Committee on Monday 2 November 2026. Since 2014, accident and emergency departments at the national level have failed to meet their constitutional target: that 95% of patients be discharged, transferred or admitted within four hours of arriving at A&E. In addition, the proportion of patients waiting in A&E for more than 12 hours has tripled, from about 3% before covid-19 to about 10% in 2025-26. In May 2026, during any 24-hour period, almost 3,000 people were waiting in corridors.

Not only have wait times risen, but costs have, too. The cost per patient in A&E has increased by a third, from £211 in 2018-19 to £280 in ’24-25. Overall spending in A&E has increased from £4.3 billion in 2018-19 to £6.6 billion in 2024-25. That represents a wholly unacceptable level of care, with the increase in long waits and the rise of corridor care significantly impacting patient safety. Analysis by the Office for National Statistics estimates that patients who wait in A&E for 12 or more hours are twice as likely to die as patients who wait for two hours.

Today, we will therefore challenge NHS England and the DHSC on A&E waiting times, patient experience in hospital, and rising costs and flow within hospitals. We have a very senior team here with us. Will each of you please introduce yourselves?

Samantha Jones: Good afternoon. I am Samantha Jones, permanent secretary at the Department of Health and Social Care.

Q86 Rupert Lowe: Having listened to the debate, I have a question. The NAO found that NHS England has no means to enforce recommendations on diverse NHS trusts—that is one of the points that it made. Sir Jim Mackey: We have powers of enforcement and intervention in extremis. From a regulatory perspective, that act has to proportionate, and it has to be a series of actions that a reasonable organisation would carry out if able. A lot of things we have talked about are changes to clinical or operational systems. In this context, if a regulatory body intervenes with or directs them, there is a significant chance that you create more risk than you resolve, so we have to exercise those powers very carefully.

Q87 Rupert Lowe: Have you ever used them?

Sir Jim Mackey: We are in the process of using them somewhere in England on another matter, so these things do happen. Generally, our intervention will involve the removal of key individuals or a board, implanting a turnaround director, and so on. These powers are used. There is a process to go through and you have to have the legal instruments in place, but as I say, as of today, a set of actions were initiated last week to enforce some clinical change where we did not have confidence that the local leadership were doing that of their own volition.

Q88 Rupert Lowe: It just struck me that it is difficult for you guys to sort it out if you do not have the power of censure over the people who are failing to deliver.
