I am extremely pleased to be joined today by three people who I think will allow us to look at trade and trade opportunities from a slightly different angle from the simply commercial one: Rosa Crawford, the head of international at the Trades Union Congress; Tom Wills, the director of the Trade Justice Movement; and Amrita Saha, who is a senior fellow at the Institute of Development Studies. Thank you very much indeed, all three of you, for coming.

I think we have a full complement of the International Agreements Committee. Maybe we are waiting for one more member to join us online, but this is a great opportunity for us all to inquire from you your views on the work that we are doing. Not every question has to be answered by everyone but, please, if you feel that something has not been said, do say so. We have a list of questions which I think you have been sent, but I hope you will give us the opportunity to ask supplementary questions as well. With that, are we ready to start? Lord McDonald, maybe you can ask the first question.

Lord McDonald of Salford: Thank you, Lord Chair. For the purposes of my question, let us assume that the Government have a trade policy. My question is to all three of you. From your perspective, what have been the principal strengths and weaknesses of the Government’s trade policy in the areas of labour, development and the environment to date, and what do you think could be improved? I ask that to Rosa first.

Rosa Crawford: Thanks very much for the question and thank you very much for the opportunity to give evidence. As TUC, we very much welcomed the approach flagged in the trade strategy, where the Government said it would “seek strong labour provisions” with trading partners and engage with those trading partners to hold the UK Government to those commitments. It is fundamentally important to trade unions that we have strong, enforceable labour commitments in trade agreements to make sure that we have fair trade and that countries are not gaining an unfair advantage on the basis of exploitation of workers.

With the UK-EU negotiations and the reset that the Government launched, we very much see that approach put into action, where the Government have a trading partner in the EU that upholds very high standards on workers’ rights. With the UK-EU agreement, we got a path-breaking commitment—what was called the level playing field commitment—whereby both parties agreed that they would hold workers’ rights to the level they were at in 2020 and not regress from those, and that if they did regress there would be a sanctions mechanism levied. That is a very welcome approach that was a result of trade union lobbying, I should say, on the UK and EU side for many years. That agreement being in place was a huge step forward. The fact that the UK Government are now in the process of building a closer relationship with the EU is very welcome to the TUC, because the Government have been clear that the closer relationship would be founded on mutual high standards of workers’ rights, and we want the UK to commit to implement any improvement made in the EU. That has been a very welcome approach.

In the UK’s trade agreement with Switzerland, the labour provision was also subject to dispute settlement. That has shown that the Government are also able to implement a sanctions mechanism for labour commitments with a non-EU trading partner.

It has also been very welcome that the Government have engaged closely with trade unions in their approach towards trade negotiations and trade policy, which is an important break from the past, and we are involved in the UK Government’s Trade and Sustainable Development Domestic Advisory Group for the UK-EU, and in the Trade and Labour Forum.

It should be said that UK trade unions have not yet been consulted on the text of trade negotiations, and that is somewhere where we think the Government should go further so that we can fully inform the negotiations with the needs of working people. We also want there to be a role for Parliament, because we know that you have not been consulted on the text of trade negotiations either, so we want that increased scrutiny as well.

I will just pick up on some areas where we think there is not enough consistency, though, with the approach flagged in the trade strategy about seeking high standards of labour protection. The Government have concluded trade agreements with partners where we have concerns, in particular the Gulf states and Vietnam, which is one of the CPTPP countries where trade unions are banned. In Vietnam, independent trade unions are banned and, in half of the Gulf states, independent trade unions are banned. We are concerned that in both those trade agreements—CPTPP and the Gulf states agreement—there is no effective enforcement for the labour clause. I also mention that we are concerned about trade agreements with India and South Korea, where there is also widespread repression of labour rights. So we think the Government can do more to actually put their commitments into practice.

I then just flag that, while we have really very much welcomed the approach with the steel strategy, we have some concerns that good jobs are being undermined by the tariff-free quota that has been set, particularly for India, South Korea and Vietnam, because that is allowing too many cheap imports to come in. That also undermines good jobs, so we think there are some areas for improvement there.

We also have concerns about investor-state dispute settlement undermining the Government’s ability to act in the public interest and to do things like improve workers’ rights and bring public services back into national ownership, because, of course, members of the committee will know that ISDS allows for Governments to be sued by companies for actions like renationalising public services. Indeed, the Government are subject to three ISDS cases currently that I am sure we will come on to. We want ISDS written out of all trade agreements and the commitment made, as Australia has done, that there would be no ISDS in future trade agreements.

There are areas we want to improve. As I say, there is much better engagement from this Government than the previous Government with trade unions, so we are hoping that we can do more to make that commitment in the trade strategy around high labour standards a reality.

Lord McDonald of Salford: Thank you. I understand why you concentrated on labour, but I point the other two panellists to development and the environment, and go to Amrita next.

Amrita Saha: Thank you very much for having me here. On development, based on our work at the Institute of Development Studies and University of Sussex, I would like to talk specifically about how the UK has provided support strengthening developing countries’ participation in trade. This has been through the UK’s trade and investment advocacy fund, which has been running since 2011. It specifically provides trade and investment negotiation support to developing countries. As we know, it can be really challenging to negotiate effectively due to costs—practical, resource-based operational costs. Many developing countries struggle to play a full role, and many have bare-bones operations in Geneva at the WTO. Our research suggests that the negotiations window of the trade and investment advocacy fund has been effective and has proven good value for money. It has been unique in addressing the imbalance of resources and the imbalance in the influence between developed and developing countries. It has worked on developing countries’ capacity to negotiate and has been most effective in helping to solidify negotiating positions.

We all know that negotiating outcomes are inevitably the product of multiple factors, but very specific contributions can be tracked down to negotiating texts that have either maintained or advanced the interests of developing countries. This is good, and we would advocate for this support to continue.

Secondly, UK trade policy has improved market access or, in many cases, provided continuity. The UK’s unilateral preferential market access scheme, the DCTS—the Developing Countries Trading Scheme—has made it easier and cheaper since 2023 for developing countries to export to the UK. The objective has been to stimulate trade but with a vision to promote growth, support economic transformation and help reduce poverty. The DCTS can support developing-country exports; however, it can also lower costs for UK businesses. Our research has found early positive signs that DCTS is succeeding in encouraging developing countries to make greater use of these trade preferences, and this translates into higher tariff savings. Post-Brexit free trade agreements and economic partnership agreements are also preserving preferential access.

My third point is that UK trade policies and programmes certainly articulate very strong commitments to inclusion and gender equality. The UK supports the International Trade Centre’s programme SheTrades, which works with women entrepreneurs. This is a very strong and powerful case. Export credit arrangements—for example, UK Export Finance—explicitly link its sustainability strategy to positive impacts in developing markets. There is the UK-New Zealand FTA as well as the UK-India FTA, which is focused on trade and development and labour provisions.

That is the positive story, but we find that there is a lack of coherence. There is no effective integration of inclusion and sustainability goals. What do I mean by that? You look at these ambitions often in trade agreements, but are they very closely linked with trade and development programmes that sometimes run in the same countries? That remains an open question. Sometimes short-term commercial interests can dominate; LDCs receiving enough sufficient priority, as well as the issue of LDC graduation, is an important one. More fundamentally, I argue that commitments alone do not demonstrate improvements in development goals or, for that matter, inclusive growth. We need to think very carefully about how funded implementation and meaningful participation by business organisations and worker organisations can go side by side with evaluating distribution outcomes. Where are the gains and where are the losses, and how can we really connect these trade preferences that are being utilised with very targeted interventions in a context where aid has gone down—where there are aid cuts? How do we make sure that the support that the UK does provide is targeted to our preference users?

Tom Wills: Thanks for the invitation to appear today. Similarly to my fellow panellists, I say that the positive has been rhetoric and the area for improvement has been delivery. In the trade strategy released last year, again as a result of advocacy from charities, trade unions and members of the Trade Justice Movement, the Government recognised—explicitly for the first time—that trade does not exist as a set of commercial relationships in some kind of vacuum, but rather has important consequences on labour rights, emissions and international relations.

There are a couple of quotes to pick out from the trade strategy that particularly reflected our analysis. The Government said that trade “can—absent safeguards—blow away important protections regarding the environment, working conditions or product standards”. They recognised that, alongside free trade, we need a shared set of fundamental social and environmental standards if we are not going to see trade being used as a kind of engine for pushing rights and standards protections down. Another quote is that “Trade policy is one expression of the UK’s international agenda”, and the strategy links to environmental protections, labour rights, corporate accountability and human rights. All of that is very positive. We are yet to see how that very strong and mature analysis actually impacts or shapes what appears in UK trade agreements.

Since the trade strategy was published, as Rosa said, we have had flagship free trade agreements announced with India and with the Gulf Cooperation Council. With India, there was a labour chapter, an environment chapter and various provisions, but none of them was actually subject to the dispute settlement provisions within the trade agreements. Essentially, they are second-class citizens within the structure of the trade agreement and are, to all intents and purposes, not enforceable. They are warm words. They perhaps express a direction of travel, but they do not actually have any utility for Governments or for campaigners looking to protect rights or the environment.

In the case of the GCC agreement, while we have not yet seen the full text, the Government have released chapter-by-chapter summaries and, in the “General collaboration and sustainable development” chapter, while again, there are bits of language covering labour rights, environmental protections and climate action, none of that is subject to the dispute settlement provisions. What is more, it also seems to not apply at all to the United Arab Emirates, which demonstrates this extraordinary position where the UK is, on one side, saying that it understands that trade policy is an expression of the UK’s international agenda on protecting labour rights but yet, when it comes down to it, it is content not to apply even the weakest labour provisions to a country where 88% of the population are migrant workers operating in the kafala system.

There are some really concerning disconnects between rhetoric and delivery that hopefully the Government will be able to make good on in the next trade policy steps that they take.

Q62 Lord Anderson of Swansea: You have all emphasised the difference between aspiration and achievement. Is it your view that the agenda—the international climate for the areas that you and ourselves are all concerned about—has darkened in recent years?

Rosa Crawford: We work through the International Trade Union Confederation, which we are a member of, and with trade unions in global South countries, around the multilateral trade environment and the WTO in particular. We know that there are really existential challenges facing the WTO. Obviously, the US has been blocking the appellate body for a number of years. Then, more recently, the Trump Administration have posed really severe threats as well.

We know that there is severe reform needed from the WTO and we think there is potential through that body. We talk to global South partners and we ask them, “Do you just want no WTO?” They say no—that there needs to be a global framework, but it has to be a framework that is founded on the principle of fair trade. That means, for the trade union movement, that labour standards need to be embedded within WTO rules. They have not been since the organisation was founded over 30 years ago. That has meant that you have members like China—it is not the only member, but it is one of the most significant members—where independent trade unions are also banned. That means they also gain an unfair competitive advantage because one of the ways that they can hold down prices is that workers are not legally able to strike for better pay or conditions. For us, you need to have binding labour rights commitments within WTO rules. We also need to see more flexibilities allowed for global South countries to be able to invest in decent jobs. That means flexibilities around the subsidy rules, around the TRIPS rules and around technology transfer and the rules around special and differential treatment for developing countries. Global South countries have not been allowed to invest in the industries that they need—the clean industries as well—to have decent jobs. They need the flexibility that global North countries have essentially been able to have, because of power asymmetries.

There are other improvements we want as well, including a trade and decent work working group set up at the ILO, which involves trade unions. We have historically been shut out of negotiations at the WTO, which has meant that there have not been enough initiatives to promote decent work. The e-commerce moratorium was a big issue at the last WTO ministerial. We want an end to that moratorium so that global South countries in particular can collect the revenue that is often very much needed for the public purse from digital transactions. On the issue of public health, which is obviously not just a workers’ issue but does affect workers, we want an extension of the suspension of the IP rules that were applied on the Covid vaccines to other vaccines for life-saving medicines, because we need that as an issue of public health concern.

Reforms are needed, and obviously we need to get over the block on the appellate body. The UK Government have shown a leadership role in the WTO; at the last ministerial it was taking a leadership role. We would like to see the UK Government using that leadership role to press for those changes that the global union movement is calling for around the multilateral trading system and unblock the appellate body so that we can actually make it function again.

Amrita Saha: Thank you for the question; it is critical. I was talking about the UK’s preferential tariff scheme, which is the Developing Countries Trading Scheme, and how the UK has facilitated market access. But your point is well taken: market access is not market participation. If investments in productive capacity are absent, countries will not be able to utilise those preferences. The challenge and goal for development policy in trade right now is how we address that disconnect. Q63 Lord Hannay of Chiswick: Perhaps drawing on the first question, which I do not think we have had a very full discussion about, and the inclusion of environmental provisions, I would like to ask you, Amrita and Rosa, whether you are familiar with the agreement that the European Union has reached with Mercosur. That is quite a specific question. More generally, what should be the UK’s priorities in reforming and strengthening the rules-based international trading system in these different areas—development, the environment, labour and so on. This is bound to be a hot subject in the years ahead. What do you think we should be aiming for, in generalities?

Amrita Saha: I would like to answer the second, more general, question, if that is okay. I am sure my colleagues will want to come in on the first part of your question.

In thinking about the global trading system, I start with the point that that is not just the WTO; it is spread into a series of interconnected agreements and that is at different levels. Strengthening the global trading system as such requires building and defending coherence and co-ordination. Trade agreements need feet on the ground and we have to be at the table to ensure that co-operation turns them into opportunities. So, in thinking about the WTO specifically, the UK should certainly prioritise a fair rules-based trading system. That requires, as my colleagues have pointed out, effective WTO dispute settlement.

Through the Trade Advocacy Fund, the UK is already helping developing countries, and it is really important that we continue that, because negotiating on an equal footing can make the system fairer and also helps the system to run more smoothly. Helping developing-country negotiating partners to conduct the right kind of analysis and have informed stances can avert the case where they just say no.

Secondly, the UK should make development a substantive part of the WTO reform, we argue, and this is with commitments matched by resources. While developing countries need a voice in negotiations, talking about productive capacity has to be matched by the capacity to implement agreements. One thing which is really critical in this is the appropriate balance of support at Geneva and country levels. Often we find that Geneva-focused support is economically efficient, but the trade-off is that it can exacerbate a capacity imbalance between Geneva and national capitals, and that could lead to negotiated agreements failing to reflect domestic needs and political realities.

My third point is really just a reality check. This was a question from before, about the ongoing lack of progress. The likely future geopolitical directions could limit what engagement at the WTO could achieve. We should of course continue to take part, not least because the opportunity costs of not doing so are very high. Given the challenging context, it is also a chance for us to think what could be done with a constrained budget by spending where it counts, to ensure that the UK continues to exert lasting influence. We should see if support may be better directed at specific negotiations alongside the WTO. This could be the AfCFTA or with ASEAN countries, and this has been done in previous trade advocacy fund programming. Identifying very specific areas of support, combined with supporting developing countries at the WTO, is quite important because the opportunity for impact might be much greater sometimes in these cases.

Rosa Crawford: If I might build on that last point that Amrita made, we think it is very important that the Government have increased the funding around Aid for Trade and increased that support at the WTO level for trade union or covenant capacity in influencing those negotiations. For us also, as Amrita says, country-level capacity building and capacity building for trade unions specifically is something we want the Government to have a fund to support, which we had under the previous Labour Government. Up to 2010, the Government were funding trade unions to do capacity building, including in the area around influencing trade negotiations. That funding was cut in 2010 by the coalition Government and has not yet been restored. We are engaging with the Government about possibilities for restoring that funding, albeit we know it is a difficult financial climate, but it is not a large amount of money and it actually creates a huge outcome. Increasing union capacity to influence trade negotiations means that you can have the concerns and the needs of workers, who are going to be affected by this agreement, adequately affected in the agreement.

As the TUC, we have done self-funded projects with unions to build capacity that have had a real impact. For example, in Cameroon, on the AfCFTA, we funded a project which increased their capacity to influence negotiations. That has meant that labour considerations and the needs of workers in Cameroon have been factored into negotiations to a much greater extent than they would have been if trade unions had not had that capacity support.

We would like the Government to direct support also to trade union capacity building in their aid spend as a way to reinforce the role of workers, to have their voices heard and to influence the multilateral trading rules. I covered quite a lot of the multilateral trading asks in the previous question, so I might not go into those. I can touch on Mercosur, but I want to give Tom some space.

Tom Wills: I completely agree with the spirit of the question. We need to be designing trade and development policies that reflect the realities of the supply chains that you are mentioning. Q65 Baroness Anelay of St Johns: Earlier this year, we had evidence given to us by the then Secretary of State on these matters, Peter Kyle, and he confirmed to us—I quote him—that “we are entering a period where we are moving not just to FTAs, but to other forms of negotiations, agreements and MoUs”. All three of you so far have been referring to different types of agreements. Can you explain your position about whether you think that really matters—that the Government go after one and not a different one? Against the background of that, from what you have seen so far from the Government in these two years, have they got that balance right? Perhaps we can start with Tom this time.

Tom Wills: We are absolutely seeing a shift away from these traditional comprehensive, all-singing, all-dancing free trade agreements towards different types of trade instruments. I guess there are two things to say on that trend. One is that, where agreements do contain binding provisions on tariffs or quotas or standards, our position is that they should also contain equivalent binding provisions on labour and environmental standards. That is a core precept of everything that we argue for. Ultimately, it is the way of delivering trade that is sustainable, resilient and values-driven.

There is also a democratic deficit that we are at risk of here. As we move away from the processes, which in our view were inadequate, of understanding, scrutinising and approving free trade agreements, we are moving into a bit of a Wild West. The UK in the last few years has finalised 16 critical minerals agreements with partners around the world. We finalised three separate agreements with Trump’s America. None of those is a binding traditional free trade agreement. They are all interesting, important, and potentially deeply consequential to our economy and our society, but none has had any kind of scrutiny from parliamentarians. They have not been subject to debate, amendment, approval or any vote. That seems a huge democratic problem.

I guess the clearest indication of why that is a problem is the UK-US pharmaceutical agreement from last December. That was agreed with Trump, as committee members will know. It entailed the NHS making changes to the prices that we were willing to pay for US medicines and, according to some very credible academic analysis, could lead to 229,000 excess deaths by 2035. That is more than in the whole Covid pandemic in the UK. It was never subject to any kind of parliamentary discussion or debate. What we are now seeing over the last couple of weeks is six parliamentary committee chairs in the Commons writing to the Government and the NAO. The NAO has started its own investigation into that agreement. It has taken 11 months, but we are starting to develop some slightly ad hoc processes to hold the Government to account for this. That is obviously not a sustainable model for how we should be scrutinising agreements going forward. We need to update our democratic processes to ensure that, where there are relevant agreements, they are properly scrutinised by parliamentarians.

Amrita Saha: I will just add something very briefly, if that is okay. The form of agreement does matter. Q66 Baroness Bonham-Carter of Yarnbury: Picking up on your mention of the democratic deficit, I believe that you and Rosa Crawford have a concern about trade and investment rules constraining government’s ability to oversee labour and environmental standards. How well do current UK trade agreements strike the balance between promoting trade and investment on the one hand and preserving government ability to regulate in the public interest on the other? I think you are probably going to be a little bit critical, so what is the way forward?

Tom Wills: It will not surprise you to hear me say that the balance is currently struck extremely poorly. That is particularly relevant when it comes to the UK’s 80 agreements that contain investor-state dispute settlement. Rosa mentioned earlier that ISDS offers investors an avenue to sue their host state for compensation if they are able to convince the tribunal of three private lawyers that public policy decisions made by that state have unfairly damaged the value of their investment. Our position as the Trade Justice Movement is that, in privileging the protections given to investors to continue to make a profit over the freedoms afforded to democratic Governments to enact policies in the public interest, ISDS really strikes that balance poorly.

The UK takes a case-by-case basis position on ISDS, which is a frustration given that the arguments in favour of ISDS now seem remarkably defunct. The arguments in favour of ISDS that have been put forward by government officials and Ministers in recent years rotate around two key points. One is that it encourages foreign direct investment. That might seem to make logical sense—an investment agreement leads to investment—but actually it is not backed up by any academic analysis. The most exhaustive academic analysis—one of 74 separate studies—produced in 2022 found that the impact of ISDS on foreign direct investment was so small as to be considered zero. There is no upside.

However, the UK also argued for years that it had never been sued and therefore was only a beneficiary of the ISDS system. That again is now a defunct argument. Over the last 18 months, the UK now faces three ISDS lawsuits, which between them perfectly demonstrate the absurdity and anti-democratic nature of this system. One is from the investors behind the proposed coal mine in west Cumbria. That had its permissions rejected in the High Court. The investors have come back saying that they were unfairly treated, asking for £300 million from the Government in compensation for a mine that does not exist and never had permissions to go ahead.

We are facing an ISDS case from a Russian oligarch, Mikhail Fridman, one of the richest men in Russia. He is contesting the sanctions that were placed on him after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine. We do not know how much he is suing the UK state for, but he has a separate claim against Luxembourg for $16 billion, which is more than his entire net worth and half of that country’s national expenditure. That has actually caused the EU to take Fridman off the sanctions list. The threat of an ISDS claim has led to this extraordinary situation in which the EU has rewritten its whole sanctions policy, which demonstrates the power that this system gives to investors over, ultimately, national security policy in the EU.

The third case is a threatened case being brought by the Chinese steel company Jingye relating to the bringing of the Scunthorpe steelworks into public control. That is reportedly £1 billion—

The Chair: Sorry, I am going to interrupt and ask a supplementary question as I am quite interested in this, because investors need to be protected or they will not make investments. ISDS works both ways. Vodafone in India is a very good example of where a country came out of the ISDS process and then behaved in its own way according to the assets of a corporate entity. Are you suggesting we should not give protections to investors? If you do not have property rights and the rule of law, then you cannot have a good investment climate, which means you cannot have a good, strong economy. My question to you is: is the ISDS structural process different from normal compensation? If the Government take my house, I should be given compensation. If they take my company because they change the law or decide not to pay a subsidy, then there is clearly financial redress—there is a contract. Are you seriously saying that we should not do that? Maybe, if there is a confusion over the terms, could you clarify why ISDS is different from a normal commercial compensation claim, to which most companies would be entitled, and the Government are being sued all the time? Maybe you could help us understand that.

Tom Wills: We are not saying that investors should not have protection. Investors are able to access protection through domestic legal means. They may take out political risk insurance. They may access state-to-state dispute settlement mechanisms. What ISDS provides is this extraordinary additional power for investors over the freedom of states to operate in the democratic interest.

The Chair: Sorry, I do not want to dominate this conversation. We are not prohibited from exercising our democratic interests. We are prohibited from confiscating someone’s assets because the politicians want to do it. All these laws are to protect the investors from a contract undertaken by a Government, like a treaty, which then the Government change and have to pay compensation—unless there is something specifically different with ISDS, which may be the case.

Tom Wills: ISDS provides extraordinarily broad protections. It is not simply something that applies to, for instance, direct expropriation of assets. ISDS treaties contain national treatment provisions—fair and equitable treatment provisions. Over the years, we have seen those provisions interpreted extremely broadly by tribunals, leading to some absurd outcomes. For instance, $80 billion of public money has been paid out to fossil fuel companies over the last 10 years. We are not suggesting that investors should not have some degree of protection, but it is clear that at this point the balance has been struck completely in the wrong direction.

Increasingly where states do strike agreements, the majority of those exclude ISDS, because states are starting to wake up to the danger of ISDS for their public policy. Over the last 10 years, 300 ISDS agreements have been terminated by states, including by the UK, which withdrew from the Energy Charter Treaty on the basis that—this is a quote from the Minister at the time—“Remaining a member would not support our transition to cleaner, cheaper energy, and could even penalise us for our world-leading efforts to deliver net zero”. That is a quote from a Conservative Minister from three or four years ago, understanding that the protections given to energy companies in that case were not compatible with the kinds of policy decisions that the UK wanted to take.

Rosa Crawford: I will just add that, on the UK’s relationship with the EU, we do feel that the right to regulate is protected because there are specific carve-outs in that agreement. But with the other trade and investment agreements—the ones that Tom has mentioned, and the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership—we are very concerned about ISDS being present because of the threats that Tom has described, where it has undermined abilities for the Government to move away from high-carbon industries, to have an active industrial policy and to pursue foreign policy objectives.
