Chair: A very warm welcome to our new inquiry on local delivery of employment support. It is a pleasure to welcome Professor Dan Finn, who is emeritus professor of social inclusion at the University of Portsmouth. It is nice to see you again, Dan; thank you for coming along. We also welcome Ben Paxton a senior researcher at the Institute for Government, but also, I believe, a doctor—are you still practising?

Dr Paxton: That is correct, yes.

Chair: Fantastic; you will have an insight into the relationship between work and health, which is very valuable to us—lovely. We are looking forward to hearing from you.

The UK has a very centralised system for employment support. Could you explain how that has come about and what the pros and cons of it are?

Professor Finn: I will try not to get into lecture mode because I live and breathe this stuff and there is tons of it. It also helps to have a little bit of the history of how the shape of our “public employment service” developed to get a sense of why it is locked into certain kinds of ways of doing things and certain kinds of priorities, even though there is always the—“veneer” is too strong a word—impulse to localise, partly because of the pressures it is under, partly because it knows it needs to do that to deliver.

In the 1980s we had a formal public employment service in the European sense, under a tripartite type of system: unions, employers, that typical kind of structure. It was created in response to what was seen as a benefit-policing, second-class unemployment benefit system, and it was designed as a tool for labour market management—manpower planning, as it was called in that era. The tricky thing was that in concentrating on skills, skill shortages and labour mobility, as mass unemployment arrived in the 1980s, we saw the claimant unemployed accelerate pretty massively while the jobcentre system was delivering its targets, so there was a mismatch.

Over time, the Government basically harnessed and reformed the system to focus more on the claimant unemployed, partly driven by the Treasury, partly driven by where the savings were. In that period, the newly arrived labour force survey showed that the number of people who were claiming unemployment benefits was far higher than the number of people who said they were available for work and looking for work. The thing was, how do we get this system working more and directed at the claimant unemployed?

That led in turn to a thing called the stricter benefit regime within the system. The focus was on activation, in the language of that time, so that people who are signing on and claiming benefits—the regular unemployed—should be required to do more things to look for work. The role of the employment service is not necessarily to match people to jobs—if it does that, then fine—but to get people looking for work and to help them do those things themselves.

From that period on, as the terms of reference of the jobcentre network have widened towards, first, lone parents, and then more recently, people on disability benefits, we have seen effectively a claimant employment service rather than a public employment service. The advantages of that for central Government are two things. One is you have a service delivery mechanism that is primarily focused on getting the benefit bill down, as well as getting people into jobs, which is very appealing, as you can imagine, for the Treasury and for value for money, as it is measured. You also have an instrument that is incredibly useful for central Government when they need to respond to crises and the arrival of economic shocks to the system.

I think that is one of the two tensions that are still there as we go into the next wave of localisation, which is the conditionality and sanctions regime. If you remember, we have something like 7 million working-age people on universal credit, and 2 million of them are subject to work-related requirements of one sort or another. That is the bread and butter work of Jobcentre Plus.

The other thing is that, as Government want to do big things and want to respond to economic crises, the temptation to steer nationally through one Department is incredibly strong. Again, in the period coming out of the 1980s, there were attempts to localise. We had whole networks of training enterprise councils and things like this, but then when new Labour came in and wanted to deliver its new deals, it went for a highly centralised, top-down kind of model where the centre and the Treasury knew best about what would work on the ground and you had this uniform, standardised delivery network created.

Those are some of the tensions, and that is why I think we have the type of system we have, operating to the types of structures we have. When I say “system”, I am explicitly talking about the Jobcentre Plus network rather than the other things that we are probably going on to talk about now.

Chair: Okay. Ben, do you want to add to that?

Dr Paxton: Yes, I will add to that. As I think Dan was saying, pulling out the different groups that the system is trying to support is really important for people who are thinking about this. There are the unemployed, often going through jobcentres, even though there is some kind of more contracted programmatic provision through the Restart programme now. Then you have the economically inactive group, and our focus has been on that health-related inactive group, where we have had a series of different programmes, most recently Connect to Work, which is a more localised system of support than we have had in the past.

As for the reason the system has become so centralised, I agree with all of what Dan said. Another factor to consider is that the UK state in general is quite centralised, and that overlays the kinds of differences we have seen between the UK and other countries. DWP as a Department is quite centralising. Part of the reason for that is that it has a huge role in delivering the benefits system set centrally. I think that is the right balance to have, but because it is used to deliver the large network of jobcentres through the central Department, with no independent kind of inspection or regulatory regime, which is a very unusual model of service delivery when you look across the UK state, it drives that centralisation.

Then there a few other things. There is the capacity and capability question. We sometimes get into a bit of a Catch-22 situation with devolution in the UK. We say local areas do not have the capability and capacity to deliver that support, so we should not devolve it until they are ready to have it, but having some powers and responsibility is often important for building that capacity. That speaks to the importance of central Government working with subnational Government to develop capability and capacity.

There are a couple of other things. One is around evidence. I think we have evidence for some very large employment support that worked in the past. I am particularly thinking about those for people who are out of work due to ill health—recently, the Work Choice programme, and the Work and Health programme. Where there is an evidence base, that can lead to a tendency to design programmes that mirror what has been done in the past. I think that has sustained quite a centralised approach, in some ways at least, in recent years.

A final point, which I think is relevant to the last few years, is around the OBR. There has been a pressure to get programmes scored by the OBR, and that has led to some centralisation or trying to enforce uniformity so that they get scored. That is less of a problem now, because it has changed some of its supplier-side scoring, but I think it has been a relevant factor in recent years that has stopped some of the devolution that maybe some people want to see.

Chair: Picking up on that last point and what you said about standards, Ben, what is the difference with the SEQF framework that ensures that there are quality programmes that are evidence-based and allow more local approaches?

Dr Paxton: There is a real tension here between what we know works to an extent—and the extent to which those interventions work is different depending on the programme—and what I think is a very reasonable impulse from central Government to say to local areas, “You must deliver a programme according to these principles, but with a typology approach.”

Chair: It is different with principles and standards, though. In the same way that we had national service frameworks in the NHS for a range of different disease conditions, which were then replicated and rolled out across the NHS, surely there needs to be an evidence-based approach that reflects those standards. That is slightly a different point, is it not?

Dr Paxton: Yes. I think you can have those standards in place. There is then a balance with how you make sure that, combined with minimum standards of how you provide a service, you also give flexibility to get the benefits of that local approach and allow areas to set up a service in a way that does genuinely join up with other services locally, such as health services. I think about some of the flexibility in programmes like WorkWell, where they have managed to combine that with Connect to Work in areas where there is more flexibility—Greater Manchester, for example. I am interested to see what evidence comes out from that about how areas have managed to combine those minimum standards and framework for delivery with that flexibility. I do not think we yet know exactly what the right balance is there.

Chair: That is a very good point. Dan, can I pick up on the points you were making about risks in a centralised approach and in the devolved approach? Would you like to expand on that a little bit? What do you think are the risks associated with a devo-by-default design?

Professor Finn: There is a number of them when you start to look at comparators, which we will probably get on to in a bit, and at how other countries have approached various forms of devolution or delegation—however you want to characterise it. From central Government’s point of view, one of the great risks when you start to devolve and shift things down is cost-shunting. Basically, if you create the wrong kind of incentives for the local level, to get outcomes in a way that mean it is more important to get people off benefits rather than into a sustained job, those people can then pop up on other benefits.

One of the things that happened in Finland was that, as they experimented with devolving, they found that something like 10% of the people who were successfully taken off the caseload of municipalities ended up on other disability benefits that were funded by central Government. That kind of cost-shifting that can take place is one of the risks. Another type of cost-shifting is when people become detached from the benefit system, and then you end up with consequences around the criminal justice system or the social care system, and the costs come back at you in another kind of way.

There is also just the loss of focus by the Department. I noticed, in the language of the evidence it submitted this time, much more emollience about the notion of devolution—which probably has something to do with the change in Prime Minister and approach—than in its previous responses to Committee inquiries into this type of area. However, you can see the real concern that if it does not have a strict—how can I put this—rules-based or accountability approach, the claimant count will increase.

On the ground, you might have very nice programmes that treat people very well, but do they get people into jobs? They are good at social inclusion, but are they increasing economic participation? There are those kinds of risks, let alone other risks such as financial risks of dodgy contractors because there is no rigorous assessment process.

John Milne: Good morning. One of the advantages of this area is that every country faces the problem, so there are any number of examples for us to look at and see how they are getting on. Looking at the international evidence for the relative success over decentralised versus centralised models, what can you tell us about that? I think Professor Finn, you mentioned it was a special area for you.

Professor Finn: Yes. A grand sweep of OECD countries do it, and many of these other countries are constitutionally very different. There are federal Governments where different tiers of government have embedded responsibilities and rights in a way that does not exist in the British system where you can just reinvent things every time there is a change of Government. However, there has definitely been a move towards devolution, particularly across northern European countries, from Germany through to the Scandinavian countries.

There was a parallel move in the United States and Canada, probably 20 years ago, so there is a lot of learning in how to do it and the mechanics of moving. Canada, for instance, moved from a federal public employment service that covered the whole of the country to one that was devolved with staff transfers, IT transfers down to the level of provincial government and below provincial government to the local delivery units, often based around cities and city governments.

There are a lot of lessons about what to do and what not to do, how long it takes, the kind of cultural and staff changes that would take place. One of the key lessons there is that these can improve things, but there are risks, going back to the cost-shunting and things like that, Equally, you can grab the benefits of more place-based delivery systems that can join up services, can get economies from joining up services in a way that a top-down, narrowly-focused public employment service might not.

We could talk about the mechanics of a lot of this and about a lot of the concerns that the Department would have previously expressed about a loss of focus, what would happen to the conditionality and sanctions regime under a more devolved system, how they would manage those things in different types of systems, but they can work. They can produce benefits, but you have to be very careful about the design, particularly of things such as financial incentives to the local level. Do you incentivise the local level to simply tell people to go away and not claim benefits because that keeps their bills down, keeps central Government’s bills down—those people pop up somewhere else—as opposed to the focus on getting people into employment? That has been a big issue in Finland, for example, which is the most recent example of a big devolution down to the local level. They have been doing it over the last three or four years.

John Milne: Do you think, from your studies, that there is a real clear pattern of the things we should do and the things we should not? Do you think the Government are picking that up, or is it just too complex for that?

Professor Finn: No, no. The Government are picking that up, to be fair. After a quick gallop through the Milburn report in the last 24 hours—

John Milne: Haven’t you read every word?

Professor Finn: —you can see that the people there have very much taken on board the whole move towards the employment and skills strand of devolution in the integrated settlements. You can see that there is an awareness there. How do we build capacity? How do we have the technical expertise at strategic authority level to be able to commission, evaluate and advise? How do we get from half a dozen good examples of authorities that are in the lead—your Manchesters, your Birminghams and places like that—to build a system that is capable of taking on the kinds of responsibilities that especially the Milburn report envisages within the three-year period, so that capacity-building issue?

I can give you one example. There was real concern when Canada devolved. They devolved the delivery of the programmes, the budgets and how you did it, but they also devolved a requirement that people evaluated and assessed their own programmes and delivery. One or two of the provinces already had good capacity and technical capacity, so they did not really need federal department supports, but other areas were required to develop joint evaluation frameworks with the centre in the initial periods and that built up the capacity of the local areas to deliver. Then on the second iteration—these are usually three to five-year agreements—the areas were much better able to do these things themselves, and you could just assess at the centre the results of the evaluations, knowing that they had the capacity to do these types of evaluations and assessments.

Dr Paxton: I do not have much more to add, particularly after Professor Finn, who is a real expert on this. When we looked at Denmark as a case study, we found that there were benefits to having clarity within the system about who is responsible for what. In Denmark, where there is a high degree of devolution to municipalities in their wider employment support system, having that kind of single point of access is valuable for citizens to know where they can get support, and it is really clear who is responsible for the range of support.

That contrasts with the system we have in the UK, and indeed actually what the Milburn review set out yesterday, where we have some devolution to quite a high degree in employment support, perhaps for young people or disabled people, but then have jobcentres still running other parts of the employment system. It is not that that cannot work—lots of other countries do it, too—but there are benefits to having a neater set-up. Within our current system, a broad estate, which is more centralised and does not have formal devolved responsibilities in the constitution, it is just more difficult to do that.

John Milne: Thank you. Moving on to employment support, how well do you think employment support functions are addressed in the devolution White Paper?

Dr Paxton: I think I am right that the White Paper that came out about a month before the devolution White Paper in 2024 was the “Get Britain Working” White Paper. That is the headline document that sets out the Government’s intention for how employment support should be devolved. There was a requirement for every area to produce a Get Britain Working plan. It set out the details of Connect to Work and said it would be funded through the integrated settlement for strategic authorities, and it set out the trailblazers as well to test new approaches to tackling economic inactivity.

There was then the English devolution White Paper about a month later, which set out and confirmed that the funding for employment support would go through the integrated settlement to those most mature strategic authorities at the time. Largely that White Paper has been overtaken, or it will be overtaken soon, by the “Rewiring the State” Cabinet statement that came out over the summer, which set out the ambition to have strategic authorities rolled out across the whole of England by 2028. I hope for much more detail on what that means in practice in an upcoming White Paper on devolution, which the Prime Minister has talked about.

John Milne: Are you expecting any particular recommendations from that?

Dr Paxton: “Rewiring the State” was very encouraging in many ways. It set out what that broader devolved system should look like, including, as I mentioned, strategic authorities in place everywhere by 2028; a recommitment to fiscal devolution—there was a bit more detail on that in the Budget—commitments on improving capability and capacity in strategic authorities, which is really important for employment support, as Professor Finn was saying; and having strategic authority chief executives as local accounting officers. I think those are all positive things and steps towards having a cleaner map of what English devolution looks like, but there are lots of questions about how it works in practice, a few things on employment support specifically.

The Cabinet statement mentioned devolving employment support to mayors. I think there is an interesting question about what that looks like in the areas that don’t have a mayor. I don’t know whether the White Paper touches on that. The “Rewiring the State” Cabinet statement was also very ambitious, giving mayors power for supporting young people into work through skills, education and health. We saw some detail on that yesterday in the Milburn review. We will have to wait to see the extent of the Government’s response to that. I think it is still to be seen how accountability would work in practice in the system that the review yesterday set out, a kind of participation agreement between strategic authorities and central Government, through a broader accountability framework, particularly in a world where you have more fiscal devolution of tax powers.

The other big thing in “Rewiring the State”, for which there is still a lot of uncertainty, is around devolving employment support for people who are long-term unemployed. We have the Restart scheme, which is coming to an end. “Rewiring the State” specifically said that employment support for the long-term unemployed would be devolved to mayors. I think there is a lot of uncertainty in the sector about what that will look like. There is a history of gaps in employment support programmes, and that is bad for the people who rely on them to help get into work. I do not know if we will get more detail on that in the upcoming White Paper, but the Government should be looking to bring forward more details on that as soon as it is possible to do so.

The final thing I will mention is the 16-19 education budget, which the Cabinet statement mentioned possibly being devolved to mayors. It was not entirely clear whether that was the whole of the education budget for 16 to 19-year-olds, or a subset of it. If that were to be true, it would be a pretty significant advancement in devolution to mayors in the size of funding that would be devolved. That budget is pretty huge and so it is really important that Government provide clarity on what that looks like in practice and ensure that there is the right kind of accountability and financial capability within strategic authorities to manage what would be a significant increase in their day-to-day spending.

John Milne: Thank you. Professor Finn?

Professor Finn: To add one thing, going back to risks and things like that, devolution frequently comes with reduced budgets: “Here are loads of flexibilities and freedoms for you but you are going to have to deal with a lot less money”. For scrutiny, I think about trying to keep an eye on what is happening to that resource. I will give you a practical example. We mentioned long-term unemployment support. What is going to happen to the Restart budget? Will there be a Restart budget? At the moment, there is a Barnett consequential for Scotland and Northern Ireland with the Restart budget, but there is no Restart programme in either country, so there is no dedicated and specific programme for the long-term unemployed that guarantees a level of support for people at a certain point of claimant unemployment. When you try to dig into this—and I have looked at both jurisdictions—you just cannot find out where the money has gone, because it is not ringfenced; it just dissipates into the system.

John Milne: Does that show up in different outcomes?

Professor Finn: Well, exactly, and that might be a good question to ask Ministers, because I do not know. Back in the day, if I was still doing this full time, we would have started to break down and look at our flows in the benefit system and try to track if there had been an increase in long-term claimant unemployment that was different in Scotland and Northern Ireland compared with the rest of the UK. I do not think so, but they have a big increase in incapacity benefits and disability benefits. How much might that have to do with the thing? But then what we might find out is that these programmes do not have that much of an effect. There is risk, once you devolve and un-ringfence the budgets, which is what people want and what Milburn is talking about a lot. The dilemma is then how do you steer to make sure that people are getting the services you want them to get?

John Milne: It is the freedom to do exactly what I want you to do anyway, really.

Professor Finn: Yes.

Liz Twist: I would like to ask about capacity. At the same time as the strategic authorities are taking on responsibility for employment support, they are also getting new devolved functions from transport through to housing. What is your view on whether or not they have the bandwidth, or can develop the bandwidth, to successfully deliver employment support among all those other priorities?

Dr Paxton: I will go first. I think it is very hard for us to assess the extent to which every strategic and local authority will have the capacity for this. It will vary substantially. One of the differences between the next stage of devolution and the current stage is that it has been quite gradual in the places that have the most mature and often coherent geographies already. It has often been more of a partnership with central Government slowly giving power, so there has been an opportunity to develop those capabilities more slowly. The next stage would see a much faster roll-out of the capability needed to deliver these employment support programmes.

Our research found three particular capability gaps that we think need to be addressed to successfully deliver devolved employment support. One is policy design capability. One of the benefits of devolving employment support should be areas being able to design a new system that works for their local area. That requires policy capability, and often the new strategic authorities have a very small number of people working across the different responsibilities on policy.

A second gap is around analytical capability. It is really important that the policymaking process is informed by the best evidence available and data on local need, local demographics and the local labour market, and that evaluation is built into policy designs from the beginning so that we can learn what works and areas can iterate their approaches.

The third area where we think capability needs to be strong is the commercial capability to go out and procure services from what might be large providers, SMEs or the voluntary sector locally, getting good value for money from those contracted services, making sure those contracts are delivering good value for money but also allow flexibility. Some of the roll-out of Connect to Work to date has shown some variability in the approach that has been taken there, maybe reflecting some variation in capability between different areas. However, as I said earlier, it is important that DWP does not say that we will wait for areas to have their full capability in place before we do any devolution, because otherwise we get into a cycle where we never have the necessary capability. This is about partnership working between central Government and local areas.

Liz Twist: You have also mentioned the different levels of maturity between authorities. Have you seen that make a difference?

Dr Paxton: I do not know the details of how Connect to Work has been rolled out, which I think in the current state is the best test case of how this works. I know that the Committee has done an inquiry into Connect to Work. However, from speaking to people in the sector and in local and strategic authorities, I do not think it has been straightforward. The most mature strategic authorities have seen a really smooth roll-out and the clusters of local authorities that may not be or may not reflect strategic authorities have done this really badly. It is much more variable. I think that speaks to the importance of individual people working together, having a history of collaborating across boundaries and having those skills individually, rather than necessarily always having a system solution of getting the right kind of overall capability in place. It is often much more individual than that.

Professor Finn: I think it is also the responsibility of the centre to help build that capacity. DWP has good commercial expertise that it has developed over many years. How do you share that? How do you learn from it? How do you understand it? We should not underestimate the amount of acumen that exists at local and strategic authority levels. The authorities buy lots of services already, they manage lots of things. There are levels of expertise there, but there is a transitional phase that we will have to go through, where it will be very uneven. There will be performance lags and impacts as a consequence, and it will be difficult to marry up some of the delivery with the rhetoric that has been used to describe what is taking place. I think it is the responsibility of both centre and the strategic authorities themselves to build up that acumen. Expertise is one of the most important things that they must put in place and very quickly, to be honest, given the new responsibilities that are coming their way, in employment support and those other areas.

Liz Twist: Is there any key thing that you think is needed to help work it out?

Professor Finn: One thing is about data and information sharing. The local level has to have a line of sight of a lot of this information to be able to track where it needs to target services and then evaluate and assess the impact of the services it is delivering, as well as performance-manage the public, private or voluntary sector organisations that will be delivering the services in the first place. We can say that grandly, but data sharing is a real bugbear in the system.

Dr Paxton: I agree with the data sharing point. I will add the potential use of secondments from central Government into strategic and local authorities to help support. The DWP has built up a large amount of commercial analytical capability over many years. In a world in which you have a completed map of English devolution—and this is being delivered at strategic authorities across the country—you would expect there to be less need for some of the capability that has sat in central Government to date, and thus the opportunity to share some of that expertise through secondments. It is true for employment support, but it is an opportunity across a range of other areas too. Bear in mind, though, that local delivery of support might be quite different from doing it at a national level for very large regions, so we should not always assume that people who have worked in central Government have all the answers. A lot of expertise already exists at local and strategic authority level.

Professor Finn: Also, let us not forget that there is a district structure inside Jobcentre Plus. It is not all in Westminster. A lot of the programme managers and contract managers are operating at that level anyway through the districts. Secondments backwards and forwards between them is one way to increase that crossover.

Liz Twist: I want to move on to local government budgets, which are already under massive pressure. What do you think are the resource implications for local government as it takes on responsibility for employment support? Will any additional funding that is provided be enough to match those demands? It is what you were just talking about, isn’t it—taking on new responsibility with less money?

Professor Finn: Yes, that is just one of risks and the tension then is between—what is the most recent language?—delegation or devolution. Do you try to ringfence the money? In which case, are you then just simply making the local level a deliverer of national programmes but in a different way from prime contractors, or do you give them the flexibility and try to hold them to account through different kinds of mechanisms? It may be that they can save money and be more efficient and use that resource to do other things.

Again, there is a hint in Milburn about this—the notion of sharing gain and pain in the benefit system. An example from quite a few of the devolved systems is how the centre tries to incentivise local, particularly municipal, government in Europe: “Here is your allocation for benefit money. You are getting a block grant. If you manage to get people in work more quickly, you keep the additional resource that is in that block grant and you get flexibility, you can reinvest it in services for the unemployed, or you can use it for other kinds of services. However, if you overspend, you have to make up the difference from your own resources.” There is something appealing about creating that kind of incentive structure inside devolved arrangements, but it always comes with a whole set of other risks.

Finland created this kind of cost mechanism in response to results from their trial period. This cost-shunting was taking place, so they shifted the risk on to the local authorities. Now they are delivering in practice, some local authorities are finding that they have these fines, effectively, and that means they cannot provide services because they are paying these fines. In one municipality it is something like €200 per citizen in that particular area. That is the equivalent of what they have lost in funding, because they are not actually getting people into employment, but they are responsible for making sure that the benefit system is paid there. It very interesting that that is hinted at in Milburn. Who knows? But that is one way of doing it.

How do you unlock the resource otherwise in the benefit system? We are paying people long term on disability benefits, possibly for another 10 or 15 years. If you can get them into work more quickly than that, why should the local area not share in the benefit saving, the tax generated from employment and so on? That would be one way in which maybe you can start to open up resources in a much more significant way than simply the traditional allocation of programme money as you negotiate with the Treasury.

Dr Paxton: I would flag two other kinds of risk. One is that the proposal is to devolve employment support to the strategic authorities, and there is a separation between their budgets and local authority budgets at the moment anyway. That separation maybe protects local authorities that have seen significant real-term squeezes on per-person funding over the last 15 or so years. However, in a world where you have more fiscal devolution, where strategic authorities are raising tax revenue, there is potential—we know this through Connect to Work, for example—that a lot of strategic authorities are just passing funding to local authorities and they are getting grant funding to deliver the programme.

A second risk is around piling on legal entitlements or guarantees in a system without providing the funding for it.

Liz Twist: A very topical issue.

Dr Paxton: Yes. In the Milburn review—as the example of where we have the most detail on this at the moment, although I think it applies more broadly—there are some really encouraging points on having a genuinely outcomes-based framework where strategic authorities are held to account for delivering outcomes on NEETs or potentially particular subgroups of NEETs. I think that is the right kind of headline approach.

There is a role sometimes for minimum standards and making sure that people are delivering a certain standard of support and ensuring access to it. There is a risk that if you make those guarantees on minimum standards too specific and onerous without providing the funding, you skew service provision towards just meeting standards rather than actually delivering better outcomes at a system-wide level. There is a tension potentially between an outcomes-based system and minimum standards.

In local government we have a lot of piling-on of statutory responsibilities on adult social care, children’s social care, homelessness, SEND, which have squeezed the non-statutory part of budgets. I think one solution to that if you want to fund support for NEETs is to make NEETs a statutory duty. That potentially squeezes out other things and you end up maybe not doing the activity that is most valuable and end up doing what will meet your guarantee requirements. That is a real risk that, as funding has been agreed, really needs to be considered.

I was encouraged that funding was going to come through a participation agreement between strategic authorities and central Government in the Milburn review recommendations, which speaks to collaboration between central Government and strategic authorities over what is the right amount of funding. Obviously when the rubber hits the road on the amount of funding that might be needed, there are difficult questions about reprioritisation.

Liz Twist: Yes. Professor Finn, I want to come back to the international experience. With devolving employment support comes a major transfer of power from central Government to the regions. What can we learn from other countries that have undertaken a similar kind of transfer? You were talking about some of that in your last answer.

Professor Finn: There is a raft of things. In the evaluation and intelligence-led approach to doing this, the comparative dimension always has to be part of it. Dr Paxton: I think What Works Centres are a useful part of the architecture of how to improve evaluation of these kinds of interventions and devolution. Neil Duncan-Jordan: I want to go back to the importance of having an evidence base for decision making. You have spoken already about jobcentre performance data and the potential of having a league table or whatever. You have also talked about the straitjacket of evaluation, so that is in contradiction to some of those other things and the DWP reviews that they hold. Are there lessons that the UK could learn from other countries about how we measure the outcomes of success, rather than the inputs that you mentioned earlier, to ensure proper accountability and delivery?

Professor Finn: I think they reinforce things we know that are hard to do, such as data and data sharing. The integration of tax and benefit records, for example, is a classic one. One of the great things in the United States is basically a first earnings survey. This is just regular stuff that the tax authorities produce, and they use it as a performance measure of the impact of their programmes, delivered through their structure of workforce development boards. Essentially, it enables you to capture not just the people who are getting jobs, but what the earnings gain is over a period as they first start making their entry into the workforce, so the real difference it is making. Then you have that data that you can use to compare with other data.

It is about getting that kind of data and having it quickly. Our system is much more integrated now, so it should be possible to start to get at that micro level of data that helps you with performance management of delivery as well as more rigorous evaluations of net impact value of how much of a difference this is making relative to what might not have happened without the interventions in the first place.

It is one key area, and in each of the systems you will see a version of that, but again, the institutional architecture in these countries is very different. For example, in most continental European countries, they have strong data-rich social insurance-based records of employment entry, earnings and employment exits. It is less so in the UK, so the potential to build that up and to use it possibly in real time—that is essentially how it is used in the USA—would be a very different way of assessing it.

We have not talked about how we test the employment outcomes. At the moment with DWP it is the joined-up system for assessing the outcomes and the payment system that goes with that. It becomes tricky because one of the things we have not talked about is that there is a whole population out there who do not claim benefits who are economically inactive, and we might want to engage. How do we assess the value for money of those people when there is not a benefit record in that simple way?

Neil Duncan-Jordan: Is there evidence that other countries that have that sort of data have then adjusted their social policy on the basis of what the data showed them?

Professor Finn: It is not just a simple evidence-based thing. There are policy and political dimensions always to these kinds of choices. For example, some of that within the United States goes with a big shift towards sector-specific types of skills-based and employment-based interventions. It is about trying to shift away from the simple work first model where it is just about getting people into entry jobs. It is about where the career pathways are and how we help people not just get into the job but then make a progression in that kind of job and the metrics are used to make that assessment. There has definitely been a shift towards sector-based models rather than the generic “any job” model.

Neil Duncan-Jordan: But the evidence alone does not dictate the social policy in that sense?

Professor Finn: No.

Neil Duncan-Jordan: That is interesting. Are there other countries that are particularly successful in measuring outcomes?

Professor Finn: No, because they all come with different kinds of qualifications, different kinds of issues. We are better at it, but as I said, we talk generically about the economically inactive but, as I was explaining before and as the Committee well knows, the focus of our jobcentre network is claimant unemployed. That population—and half the NEET population I think it is—are not claiming benefits, so how do we capture the employment outputs for them and measure that relative to the benefit savings? The best way is surveys.

As I mentioned, one of the advantages about participant surveys—they do not have to be massive, just good representative samples such as the labour force survey and so on—is that you can start to capture some of the transitions without overburdening individual providers to track down from people they worked with, to chase them and their employers to prove they were in work for 13 weeks, where their payslip is and so on. That is the audit trail that can be set up if you go down that route.

Neil Duncan-Jordan: Picking up on the devolution agenda, which several Members have already mentioned, are there other specific countries that give local government much more influence over the outcomes and the framework in which they work?

Professor Finn: Yes, but again there are big lessons. Denmark went through a devolution process about 10 years ago and devolved the whole of the public employment service to municipal government. However, that came with a massively reduced set of expectations and requirements. To have a jobcentre, people had to go through these kinds of processes, and Denmark invests something like four times the amount of money that we do in active labour market programmes.

They are now going through another big reform process that is getting rid of all that regulation, because all they are doing is processing people and they are just going to pay them on outcomes. They have a payment-by-results system there. However, they have cut the budget by half in doing that, and they say they have cut it in half because they do not need to do all these processes. The problem is they are back to square one, because that means that a whole bunch of people are not getting seen. Those are the kinds of lessons we can learn.

Neil Duncan-Jordan: Absolutely. The final point, which links to all those aspects that you have mentioned, is whether we have enough good data collected locally about the outcomes that we have. I think the answer will be no.

Professor Finn: If I was hypercritical—if you started looking at Manchester, the Local Government Association and all the programmes that they bring out to say that this works, which I do not gainsay—you will find that the outcome evidence they use is pretty shonky. We are relying a lot on what providers are saying. There is no audit test or trail in it, but the results look great. That is not evaluation and that is not real technical assistance.

We need another level of it, and hopefully the What Works Centre can do it. The preliminary work that they have been doing on good practice guides is pretty good. I think you are going to have the Learning and Work Institute come at some point, and it will be very interesting to see what its programme of work will be. My message is that the What Works Centre needs to produce stuff that is real time.

Dr Paxton: I will raise one thing that is related around engagement. I think the point has been made here that employment support programmes today have only engaged a very small minority of the total group of people who are out of work due to ill health. That then shapes the evidence base. The evidence base for these large programmes that have worked is based on a quite small group who voluntarily choose to engage. Often those evaluations use a design method that compares those who choose to engage with those who choose not to engage when invited. That means our evidence base does not necessarily carry across to a programme that is trying to engage a much broader swathe of people who are out of work due to ill health, who might be further from the labour market and less keen to engage.

People are quite fearful of DWP, and I think an opportunity of devolution is that it is possible, if the system is delivered at a more local level, that people might be more willing to engage. We do not know that, but that should be a focus of evaluation. It is very hard to access those people because they are not always necessarily connected to the benefit system, and where they are at the moment they do not have conditionality requirements that require them to engage. An important part of local area responsibility is knowing their populations and being able to access those people who to date have not engaged with employment support services.

Professor Finn: One element of that, which is implicit in some of the things that have been happening around the health service, is to capture more information from the health service about employment, employment outcomes and employment experiences. The royal colleges, on service delivery, talk about asking the work question at the right time. From personal experience and knowledge of how the health services work, they do a lot to help people keep jobs and to keep in work, but it is not captured and certainly not integrated in the kind of systems that we hope—gradually and in the future—will be one of the possibilities that is coming.
