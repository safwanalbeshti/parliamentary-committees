Witness

I: Lisa Tennant, Government’s preferred candidate for Chair of the Environment Agency.

Examination of witness

Witness: Lisa Tennant.

Chair: Welcome, everyone, to this joint meeting of the Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee and the Environmental Audit Committee. Before I come on to the business of today’s meeting, I would like to remind everyone that, at 11 o’clock this morning, we will observe a two-minute silence for Armistice Day. When the Division bell rings, the sitting and the recording will therefore be suspended for five minutes.

Today, we are looking at the candidate for chair of the Environment Agency. Welcome to the Committee, Lisa. Could you introduce yourself for the benefit of the Committee?

Lisa Tennant: Hi, I am Lisa Tennant. I am the preferred candidate for chair of the Environment Agency. I am currently chair of the Scottish Environment Protection Agency and chair of the NHS pension board.

Chair: Thank you. At today’s pre-appointment hearing, we have a range of questions from members of both Committees about your experience, your motivation for the role and your vision for the Environment Agency and its areas of work. I will start with some questions about your motivation for applying for the role. First, what made you apply for the role of chair of the Environment Agency?

Lisa Tennant: I have been passionate about environmental issues since I was a child. I grew up in an area of former heavy industry, with ironworks, coal mining and a chemical factory. I grew up on a council estate, and every weekend my parents would take me out of town to the countryside to spend time in nature. I swam in clean rivers and learned the names of all the plants and animals. It was a real escape for me, and I have been passionate about it since then.

I have worked on environmental issues throughout my career, both running sustainable businesses and sitting on and chairing boards. My motivation for coming to the Environment Agency is that I have been doing this role for three years in Scotland, chairing the environment agency there. I think that I can bring the skills and experience from that to bear on the Environment Agency, where the scale is larger and some of the issues are similar.

Chair: You have been in your current role since January 2024, and that term was due to end in December 2027. Can you explain why you decided to change roles before the end of your term?

Lisa Tennant: I came into the Scottish Environment Protection Agency with the remit to transform the agency post cyber-attack. That transformation is now almost complete: we have restored the public register after the cyber-attack; we have made cyber a board issue, not just an IT issue; we have rebuilt confidence in the agency; and I feel that I have completed the task that I came in to oversee.

The agency will be merging with Zero Waste Scotland and NatureScot. I have been heavily involved in that public service reform agenda for the past two years, helping to ready the agency for the merger. I think that now it is time for someone else to come in and see that merger through from start to finish.

Chair: If you are successful in getting this position, do you commit to serving the full term as chair of the Environment Agency?

Lisa Tennant: Yes, I do.

Chair: Taking on this new role will obviously be a challenge on a different scale from the Scottish Environment Protection Agency; it is 10 times the staff. How will you handle that challenge?

Lisa Tennant: The first thing that I would like to do is learn more about the EA, its work and its partners. Similarly, when I started at SEPA, I spent my first 100 days listening, building relationships and travelling the country to see the work on the ground. I would expect to do the same here. There are a lot of similarities in the challenges facing the agencies—for example, water, sewage and farming. There are differences, including the mayoral authorities. I would be very interested to find out more about that, to visit with mayors and understand their work, and to visit the regional offices of the EA.

Chair: You say that that is one clear difference you see. Do any other clear differences strike you between the two organisations and on the scale of the challenge?

Lisa Tennant: Apart from the scale, the privatisation of water is a clear difference. Also, responsibility for flooding and flooding infrastructure is different between Scotland and England.

Chair: On the point about mayoral areas being a difference, a lot of the country is still not covered by mayoral areas. Can you comment on how you would work with those areas?

Lisa Tennant: There are 190 regional offices in the EA, and staff are located all over the country. There is good relationship-building potential in all those areas. I would like to visit as many of them as possible and find out what is happening on the ground. The EA is running a board recruitment exercise at the moment, and I am keen that one of the new board members comes with local government and community expertise.

Chair: Did anyone encourage you to apply for this position?

Lisa Tennant: Only a friend of mine who thought that I would be good in the role. I actually applied for the position myself; I was not headhunted.

Chair: We are now going to move to some questions about your vision for the agency, which will be led by Barry.

Barry Gardiner: Welcome, Ms Tennant. You will be aware of some of the inquiries that Parliament has recently conducted—both this Committee and the EAC—into clean air, forever chemicals and water companies. What would be your comment on the reports of those Committees and how they have scrutinised the EA? What have you learned from them that would inform your approach as chair of the Environment Agency?

Lisa Tennant: First and foremost, I think that that approach is one of partnership working. It is really key to work in partnership with Parliament, to understand from those reports what Parliament is looking for, and to work in partnership with other agencies. In terms of PFAS, there are thousands of chemicals in the environment and they will last for a very long time. It is a pressing issue. The EA’s role in that is to monitor, research and enforce around those chemicals. It is at the forefront of European practice in that.

Water companies are an area of increased scrutiny, and I will expect the board to look at that very closely. The EA is improving performance on water companies and monitoring. Personally, I—

Barry Gardiner: Sorry, but could I just interrupt you? You say that, but you will know that in 2024-25, of the 98% of serious water company pollution incidents that were reported, fewer than 20% were attended by the EA. It has increased its inspections to meet its KPI—the 10,000 target, which it has met—but the non-attendance comes from downgrading the category of incident to category 4, when it has no evidential basis to do so apart from what the water companies say. Surely, you cannot be satisfied with that.

Lisa Tennant: I think I need to look into that more when I start in the role. I do not have all the information to hand. I am happy to come back to you on that. I am, however, interested at the board level in how these outputs and activities translate into outcomes on the ground and what the public see as those outcomes. I would also say that rivers take years to clean up, so those outputs and activities will not have an effect over months; it will take years, but be assured that I want the board to be focused on that area.

Barry Gardiner: Going back to what the Chair said about your vision for the EA, it has a changing role. What would be your three key priorities that you would need, as chair, the agency to focus on?

Lisa Tennant: I would call those “safe”, “clean” and “grow”. “Safe” is about keeping communities safe from flooding and drought. “Clean” is about keeping our water and air clean and free of major pollution incidents. “Grow” is the mission to support sustainable growth. Those are the three areas that I want the board to be focused on.

Barry Gardiner: That is a very good answer; thank you. Your achievement on KPIs has been quite remarkable. You have achieved almost all the KPIs that you have had in Scotland. You will be aware that the EA is overdelivering considerably against some of its KPIs. When you see an organisation overdelivering on its KPIs, sometimes you think, “Maybe those KPIs aren’t stretching enough.” How do you balance that? How do you set realistic targets that are stretching and meet the expectations of both the public and Parliament that the regulator is fulfilling its role to keep the environment healthy?

Lisa Tennant: The environment is the No.1 priority. If I might talk a little about my experience at SEPA, I have a particular interest in outcomes-based performance measurement, which I have already talked about. I have a specialist qualification in outcomes-based performance reporting. When I came to SEPA, one of the issues the board was facing was the performance framework and the KPIs. I do not think performance reporting was robust enough and I do not think the KPIs were outcomes-based enough. That is something we worked very hard on together as a board and executive. You have picked up on some successes around that area.

At the EA, I would start that exercise to see where the KPIs are now and how meaningful they are. How do the outputs translate to outcomes? Are we being risk-based and proportionate? No agency has the resources to deliver everything. Where is the Pareto principle? Where is the 80:20? How do we see where we have the greatest effect and how do we measure that we are delivering that?

Barry Gardiner: One final question, Chair, if I may. How do you strike the balance between the independence of the Environment Agency and the implementation of Government policy when you are an organisation that has more resources than DEFRA itself? Sometimes, one can feel that the tail is wagging the dog. In the past, some of us might say that has happened. How do you ensure that you are following through on what Parliament and Government are demanding?

Lisa Tennant: For me, there are two things here. The first is that the agency must listen to Government and Parliament to understand those demands. I feel that a key role of the chair is to build those relationships and understand what is being sought from the agency. Secondly, the agency is data science and evidence-based and must comply with law and regulations. When it comes to that, I see total independence for the agency from Government and from policy.

Barry Gardiner: How tied would your hands—or, more importantly, your mouth—be in speaking those scientific truths to Government?

Lisa Tennant: I am very happy to speak those scientific truths. They come from an evidence base. If I might give an example from Scotland, SEPA identified over 150 unregistered, unregulated private reservoirs in Scotland, which carries a real risk for Government. Those reservoirs had the potential to flood 5,000 properties. SEPA staff were raising that issue with Government for two years and we did not have the funding and resources to deal with it. That was raised to the board. When the board became aware of it, as chair of the board I encouraged the board to write to the Scottish Government and to our Cabinet Secretary outlining the issue. We presented the data and evidence on the risks and what was needed to remediate the issue. The Scottish Government gave us a £1 million uplift in our budget last year to address the issue. We are now part-way through a programme of remediation on that.

Barry Gardiner: Congratulations. Thank you very much.

Chair: How do you see, operationally, your working relationship as chair with the chief executive and the day-to-day staff of the EA? How do you see that working?

Lisa Tennant: Fundamentally, for me, that relationship is about holding to account and scrutiny. That is the first area that I will be looking at with the board when I come in—to understand how that holding to account has been performing. For me, a relationship with a chief executive is about the right level of challenge and support. That varies with the circumstances and the personalities involved, so I would want to understand that more before commenting further.

From my experience at SEPA, there was a lot of work to do on building trust between the board and the executive. It has been a real focus of my board development programme after the last three years. That trust has really started to develop: we can see actions on the ground, the executive bring issues very early to the board and trust that the board will support those issues, and the board feel that they can speak frankly with the executive and deliver that challenge and support.

Chair: We will now move to some questions about major changes in an organisation, which will be led by Manuela.

Manuela Perteghella: The Environment Agency is going through major changes due to the Dan Corry review and, most significantly, the creation of a new water regulator. What experience have you had in delivering major organisational restructures? How have you tackled risks and challenges, and what have you learned from that?

Lisa Tennant: If I might start with what I have learned, I have learned that it is challenging. There is no one right answer for these situations. Part of my experience comes from overseeing the working group, which delivered the Scottish Police Authority merger of the nine police forces in Scotland. It was a major transformation programme under political and media scrutiny, and it faced opposition from the public and from the unions. In terms of managing the risks and challenges, for me, that is about very robust processes and having a clear delivery plan and milestones. Supporting staff and culture through the process is also really important, and managing expectations is key.

In terms of more recent work on transformation, I have been working for the last two years with the Scottish Government on plans for public service reform. In the First Minister’s programme for Government, he recently announced that SEPA would merge with NatureScot and Zero Waste Scotland. I founded and chaired the public service reform chairs cluster group within Scotland. That brings public chairs across sectors together to talk about common issues and have a voice with Government, and to act as a conduit for Government to talk and relay with the chairs easily.

Manuela Perteghella: The changes, especially on the formation of a new water regulator, could lead to a loss of expertise from the Environment Agency and require a move from process-driven to outcome-focused working. How will you manage that cultural shift?

Lisa Tennant: The No. 1 cultural issue for me is clarity and what staff want is clarity. Sometimes, it can be very difficult to give that clarity. When the merger was announced at SEPA, we immediately had an all-staff call with a Q&A and committed to publishing every question we were asked, which we have done. That does not mean that we always have the answers to the questions, and some of that clarity is not there.

As a chair, I have been clear—so have the board—that there are principles that we can work to through periods of change, and one of those is transparency and honesty with staff: giving staff sight of information as soon as we have it, so that what we know, staff will know, when we know it. It is also about understanding concerns about building up confidence in the organisation and keeping it an attractive place to work. At board level, it is about understanding the transition plan, really clear milestones in delivery and keeping on top of that.

Manuela Perteghella: As part of that change, the Environment Agency may need to embrace greater risk taking to enable change and innovation. What is your attitude towards such risks?

Lisa Tennant: It is a big question. For me, there are two buckets of risks. There are those risks that we do not mess around with. An audit committee would say those are our core strategic risks. Our risk appetite on those is very low and the board is going to keep close oversight on them. Then there are areas where our risk appetite can be different, and innovation is one of those.

I am very keen not to have a risk appetite statement that we leave in a drawer, because I see that happen quite often. The challenge in public agencies is to bring that risk appetite to life. It is one thing to say that we want to take risk and be innovative, but it is another to enable a culture and processes that actually allow that to happen.

Manuela Perteghella: Finally, looking at the proposals from the Independent Water Commission, is there a clear way to differentiate between the work of the Environment Agency and that of a new integrated water regulator? How will the relationship with the new regulator work?

Lisa Tennant: It is not for me to say what that regulatory landscape should look like. The relationship with the EA should be very strong. I want to build those relationships early and understand more about what is planned. I want to keep those relationships strong through the process. The EA should be a trusted partner in the new organisational design, and I expect that the EA will continue to be a partner after the reform.

Chair: Just on that section, can you tell us about your experience of major organisational restructures and, looking forward, how would you work with trade unions?

Lisa Tennant: I might go back to my example about Scottish policing. We worked very closely with the unions, particularly through our human resources and people committee. Again, for me that is about early engagement—really understanding what the core issues are, listening and then working in partnership to address them.

Again, in my experience, unions are looking for clarity for their staff. As soon as we have more information, we have a duty of transparency to pass that on so that people know what will happen with their jobs. Again, I am very happy to work in partnership with the unions.

Chair: We now turn to some questions about balancing environmental protection and economic growth, which will be led by Sammy.

Sammy Wilson: Thank you for your answers so far, Lisa. Both Committees have raised concerns about the lack of requirement for environmental experience or focus in the job description for your role. We know from your CV the environmental experience that you have, but what role do you see the Environment Agency playing in enhancing and protecting the environment?

Lisa Tennant: I think No. 1 for me is environmental protection; that is what comes first. I do not believe that that cannot enable economic growth. That economic growth can be enabled in a win-win situation as long as environmental protections stay intact. An example of that is faster permitting. Simple permitting cases should be able to go through much more quickly. I see from the figures that that is starting to happen now.

Complex cases are taking too long—they can take thousands of hours. For me, the complex cases are where the environmental risk lies. I want to understand more about why those are taking so long to process and what we can do about it to enable that faster growth. I see planning decisions and planning advice going through more quickly. Both are above 90% now, so that is on an improving trajectory.

In terms of sustainable growth, I would give the example of SEPA working with the whisky industry. The whisky industry brought to the board’s attention that they wanted to use by-products from their processing to produce renewable heat and energy for their plants. They were actually being hampered from doing that by the regulations in permitting that SEPA had put in place.

We took the board on a visit to one of the whisky distilleries and saw the process happening in front of us. We got to understand it, and we were able to change the permitting framework to speed up those decisions. That is the kind of win-win that I am thinking about.

Sammy Wilson: Just on that, one of the criticisms we often have is that industry feels that they do not know for certain what kind of standards they have to meet and how regulations are going to apply. How would you address that issue while at the same time ensuring that the environment is protected?

Lisa Tennant: I think that companies and investors need certainty for growth, and I am very alive to that. Again, at SEPA we simplified that: we took eight laws into one and simplified the permitting and regulation process through our integrated authorisations framework. I would like to look at something similar within the EA. We have thousands of laws and it is very complex. I see that some of the reports have said that that process should be simplified, so if there is any way that I can support that as chair, I am very happy to.

Sammy Wilson: As chair, do you think it is necessary that job descriptions for senior posts include a requirement for environmental expertise? If you do, how would you ensure that?

Lisa Tennant: What is really key for me is the right person in the right role. That does not always mean that that person must have environmental experience. It means that they have the key skills and values to excel in that role. I have seen great people come from financial regulation into environmental regulation. What is important for me is the principles of a regulator. Can this person work in a risk-based way? Can they take decisions around that constrained discretion? Do they have the right mindset to do the job? I think those are more important than environmental credentials.

Sammy Wilson: Although economic growth and natural capital go hand in hand, there are trade-offs. How would you balance those priorities as chair? You have given us some examples already in some of your answers, but how do you think that those trade-offs can be balanced in a way that satisfies the need for economic growth and deals at the same time with the real economic concerns that people have about some of the developments that are necessary for growth?

Lisa Tennant: I think we both agree that it is a trade-off, and it has to be traded on a case-by-case basis. I think I would want to understand more about the current situation in England to give a good answer to where the trade-off should be. I would go back to protection of the environment as No. 1. Nature-based solutions in natural capital are extremely important, and one thing I have been clear about at SEPA is that climate change is a huge issue, but so is nature and biodiversity loss, which may affect us even more than climate change. That is where nature-based solutions can really step in and help. I do not think that is the solution everywhere; we need to understand that local area and work out what those trade-offs are and what the best solution is in a regional context. Nature-based solutions are preferred where they are the best option.

Sammy Wilson: Finally, the Dan Corry review proposed changes to the way in which DEFRA’s arm’s-length bodies could operate and promote growth, moving away from the rigid application of the precautionary principle to more place-based outcomes and looking at individual cases. A number of recommendations—29, I think—were made; have you had any discussions with Government about those recommendations and, if so, which would you prioritise in your role?

Lisa Tennant: I have not yet had discussions about those, but I fully expect to. It will not surprise you that I am a fan of the outcomes focus in the Corry review. Again, an example from Scotland would be our work on the River Leven, where we worked in partnership with other agencies and Government to restore the River Leven catchment area. I visited the site with the First Minister when the works were complete, and it was one of the best visits I have ever done.

You can see from that example that the outcomes-based approach does not deliver just an environmental benefit. I watched people from the local area walking up and down footpaths that did not exist before and fishing in a river where fish did not live before. Children came on a school trip to see the river that had been restored and the nature around it. For me, that is where outcomes-based comes to life for people on the ground.

Sammy Wilson: You gave an example of the move from the precautionary approach to more outcomes. What practical steps are necessary to ensure that, in moving away from a tick-box exercise where we do not take too much risk towards being less risk-averse and saying, “Here are the changes we want to see,” we deliver the necessary outcomes?

Lisa Tennant: There are a couple of things on that. The first, which I picked up on earlier, is a cultural issue: regulators tend towards the risk-averse. It is about changing the culture to say that we can take appropriate risks and use constrained discretion, but that has to be brought to life for staff. They have to see that in practice, maybe by going to one of the projects and seeing where it has worked. They also have to know from their line managers that that is expected and acceptable.

One of the things I have encouraged at SEPA is that where we want to change this kind of culture, we can change the performance metrics for our staff to say, “We reward innovation and creative thinking.” That has helped to start making the culture less risk-averse.

Chair: From what you have seen to date and read in Dan Corry’s review, do you think the Environment Agency has struck the right balance to date between protecting the environment and facilitating economic growth?

Lisa Tennant: I will be able to better answer that question when I have been in the role for a while. I am very happy to come back with my thoughts on it. I repeat: regulators tend towards being more risk-averse. I feel that the agency has had some difficult times in public as well. When that happens to an agency, confidence tends to dip and people retreat back to enforcement of regulations and taking less risk. That is certainly what we saw at SEPA after the cyber-attack. Staff morale and motivation was low. When confidence is low, people are not in a frame of mind to take calculated risks. I would want to understand more about where the agency is currently and how we get it to where we would like it to be.

Chair: We will now have some questions about operational issues led by Terry and Ben.

Terry Jermy: There is long-standing concern around recruitment and retention of staff. In particular, skilled staff have been a major concern in the Environment Agency, which has led to some of the performance issues that you referred to. As the Chair mentioned at the outset, the Environment Agency has 10 times the number of staff that SEPA has. Can you expand on your experience around improving staff retention and capability? That is crucial to the Environment Agency’s work.

Lisa Tennant: At the Scottish Police Authority, we had oversight of an organisation with 25,000 staff, and I have sat on the board of an NHS trust with thousands of staff, so I have overseen staff at scale. For me, it is key for staff retention that the board understands the culture of the organisation and the risks around retention. SEPA has a people committee, which I changed to a people and resources committee. I felt that the focus on people needed to be much more tied to outcomes and to the resources needed to deliver for people. One of the first things I did was instigate a staff survey. That is how we find out how people within the organisation are feeling. I would be keen to find out where that is at within the EA.

For me, retention is about making people feel that they are valued in the workplace. They need to be able to see career progression. Again, that is something that we looked at within SEPA. When you are in a technical specialist expert position, sometimes the only career progression is into a management role where you manage a team of people. That is not always attractive to people with that skillset, so we have looked at alternative pathways for retention and how people can be promoted into expert roles that do not always carry line management responsibility. However, where roles do carry that responsibility, we have provided an enhanced programme of training; we do not expect people who have come from a technical background suddenly to manage a team of 10 people without leadership experience and training. That has really helped with staff retention.

For me, it is also about bringing new talent and younger people into the organisation. At SEPA, we have a great programme of apprenticeships. However, I see that the EA is a Times Top 100 graduate employer, so I am interested to find out more about that.

Terry Jermy: One of the biggest barriers—I think this is fairly widely accepted—is pay and pay restraint, which I appreciate is somewhat out of your control. The previous chairman and chief executive of the EA spoke to the EFRA Committee about pay restraint being a mistake and a major barrier to recruitment and retention. Would you want to work with Government to change pay policy, so that you can improve retention?

Lisa Tennant: I do not know enough about the issue yet; I am very happy to come back and talk about that when I understand it in more depth.

What I would say is that it is important that people feel they are paid fairly for the roles that they do, and the EA needs to be competitive in attracting talent. Matters of policy around pay are for Government, but I am very happy to have those discussions.

Terry Jermy: Earlier, you mentioned permits and improving performance around permits, but there are still incredible delays in accessing some permits and licences. Part of that problem is the lack of experienced staff who are able to process some of the more complicated cases in a timely way.

I have farmers in my constituency whose extraction licences are up for renewal next March, but they need to make financial decisions now to buy the seed that is necessary. It is an incredibly risky situation. What will you be doing to improve performance and offer us more timely service? Given the importance of both food security and economic growth, performance around licences and permits is absolutely crucial.

Lisa Tennant: There have been some improvements and 40% of the backlog in permitting has been cleared. However, I appreciate that it is difficult for people in your constituency who are in that position and I do sympathise.

For me, one of the solutions to this is technology. Again, I would like to understand more about that once I have—hopefully—started in the role. I think that digital solutions can really help to drive speed around some of those complex cases, so it takes the focus away from having to recruit more specialist skills in. Again, I would need to understand more about it.

I undertook a ministerial review last year into McCloud remedy delivery within NHS Business Services Authority. A key part of that was similar; it was looking at these really complex pensions processing cases. There is a shortage of specialist skills to deliver those cases and a big part of the solution to that is looking at the technology that will enable those cases to be delivered more quickly, particularly around AI.

I see that there are AI pilots going on within the organisation, but I am keen to understand more about those and to see where AI can be applied to backlogs.

Ben Goldsborough: Welcome, Lisa. I want to focus on the budgetary element of the Environment Agency. In 2024, the EA said that it had cut back considerably on some areas of monitoring because of budgetary constraints. First of all, I would be interested to hear what experience you have of delivering in challenging budgetary environments.

Lisa Tennant: I do not think that I have had a role where I wasn’t delivering in a challenging budgetary environment. [Laughter.] It will not surprise you to hear my approach to that is prioritisation, and it is risk-based. For me, the big risks at the EA that we want to prioritise budget around are risk to life and serious risk to the environment, such as flooding, drought, flood protection and major pollution incidents. Consequently, I would want to understand how funding is being channelled towards those areas.

Going back to technology, that is usually a huge lever in reducing costs and duplication within an organisation, and in freeing up budgetary funds to go towards those areas where the board thinks they can have the biggest impact. At SEPA, we have been able to conduct a programme of transformation without any extra funding from Government, partly by delivering on those technological transformations, but a programme of cutting duplication and red tape has also freed up resources for people to work on the areas of greatest focus.

Ben Goldsborough: That is really good to hear. In the evidence you gave earlier to my colleague Barry, you mentioned the extra £1 million you secured from the Scottish Government to help with the challenges you faced. In the future, would you be willing to go into bat for the Environment Agency when it goes to the relevant Departments to secure additional funding, or do you believe that being more efficiency-led is the best way of delivering services through the EA?

Lisa Tennant: I think it is both. I am sure efficiencies can be made, particularly around technological transformation. I will be looking very carefully at that. It is incumbent on all public agencies to deliver as efficiently as they can.

On the extra funding uplift that we secured, that is on a case-by-case basis. We had a unique case that we had evidence for. If something similar happened, I would take a similar approach, but it is for the agency to work within the budgetary constraints that it has.

Ben Goldsborough: To take you off to a slightly different issue, but within operational concerns, the EA is obviously a massive stakeholder organisation. There are lots of different ways you have to face, and lots of competing challenges and different views from the stakeholders that you work with. As constituency MPs, one thing we hear a lot on the ground is a concern over the communication between the EA and those stakeholders.

Added into those issues are budgetary constraints and not being able to do the permitting as quickly and efficiently as my farmers would like. What would you do to improve the situation on the ground between those stakeholders and the EA?

Lisa Tennant: This is one of the reasons why I am keen on the idea of travelling the country to hear what is happening regionally and why I am encouraged by the EA’s regional approach. Those regional partnerships help us to understand the context and to have the back and forth communication.

For me, communication needs to be clear and repetitive—usually more repetitive and clearer than you think it needs to be. I looked at the farming rules for water. I am not a farmer, but they are quite complex in their own right. I am encouraged to see that the EA is putting more farming advisers on the ground, taking that advice-first approach and recruiting people from a farming background. That advice-first approach is key to helping people understand what they have to comply with. Progress has been made on clarity around comms, but I will be interested to see how we can improve that.

Again, to give an example from SEPA, the board requested a stakeholder engagement strategy, which has now been delivered by the executive. That is a very clear mapping of every organisation that is a stakeholder with SEPA, showing what our three core messages to each of those audiences are, what the three key messages coming back to us from those audiences are, and who will manage those relationships, whether it be a board member, myself, the chief executive or our regional colleagues.

Adrian Ramsay: Thank you, Ms Tennant, for being with us today. I want to talk about climate adaptation and resilience. You will be aware that the Climate Change Committee has said that we are, “woefully ill-prepared as a country for the impacts of climate breakdown.” What do you see as the Environment Agency’s role in addressing that?

Lisa Tennant: I would say that the climate is subject to increasing volatility, and the pace of change has outpaced the management of risks in this area. It is a key role for the EA and it comes under my heading of “safe”—keeping people safe from drought and flooding is really important. I think that the EA has been improving its work in this area. Again, for me, this is about a risk-based approach to the deployment of resources and asking what areas are most at risk from flooding and drought and deploying and making sure that the resources are being put to best use in those areas.

Adrian Ramsay: In doing that preparation, how do you account for the fact that the risks are growing and that those in the coming years are much greater than they are now and have been in even recent years?

Lisa Tennant: One of the things that we have to be is realistic about that. Again, this goes back to clear communication with the public and partnership working with others. The risks are increasing and I do not think that the resources to deliver against those risks will be able to increase at the same level. For me, the answer to that is clear prioritisation based on evidence and data.

Adrian Ramsay: It sounds to me like that is something that one or other of the Committees here today needs to explore in more detail with the Environment Agency. If the resources are not going to match the growing scale of risk, what does that mean for communities and how they are protected? We could probably do with a whole session on that. However, to explore the flood risk in a bit more detail, concerns have been raised regarding the Environment Agency’s ability to effectively co-ordinate with other responders when it comes to flooding emergencies. As chair, how would you plan to improve the EA’s co-ordination with public bodies?

Lisa Tennant: This is something that we did a lot of work on at SEPA. Historically, what has happened within environment agencies is that people who work in the agency have become, if you like, emergency responders, in their spare time or as part of their role. I think that with these increasing risks that we have identified, what we need to do is professionalise that emergency response. The EA is a category 1 responder, and it needs to be set up as a category 1 responder to be able to handle those responses. I know that work is under way to accomplish that, just as it is at SEPA. I am encouraging that work at pace to be able to deliver that emergency response. Going back to clear communications, there should be a clear port of call in one of those emergency situations and someone leading on that response to ensure co-ordination across all the agencies.

Adrian Ramsay: Looking at nature, which you talked about earlier, do you share the DEFRA Ministers’ view that nature is critical infrastructure and recognise that the Environment Agency has struggled to support nature-based solutions to date? For example, only 3% of water company capital investment is in natural solutions. If so, would you see a bigger role for the EA in supporting investment and upkeep of natural infrastructure in future?

Lisa Tennant: I am a big supporter of nature-based solutions and their being deployed where it is practical to do so. I think we need to look at the evidence base carefully for where those evidence-based solutions work best. They do not work everywhere, but I think where we can deploy them, we should.

Adrian Ramsay: Finally, you have spoken about your passion for the natural world going back to your childhood. Which UK endangered species are you most concerned about, or most appeals to you personally? What needs to be done to support it?

Lisa Tennant: You know, I am not even going to answer with an endangered species, because I do not think that it is an endangered species, but what I miss seeing is buddleia bushes covered in butterflies. That is one of the things I remember from my childhood, even in areas of environmental degradation—on those former industrial sites or along a railway, buddleia was growing everywhere and it was just covered in butterflies and moths. It was a real joy to watch them and I cannot remember the last time I went on a hike and saw the buddleia bushes covered in butterflies and moths. It is not a particularly glamourous endangered species, but it is one of those things that I often notice.

Chair: Lisa, you will be pleased to know that you are almost at the end of the session. I just have a question to add on climate adaption. Something the Committee has been looking at a lot is the issue of landslides, which can often sit between different Government Departments. It has not been clear whether it sits with DEFRA or MHCLG. Through the Committee’s work, we have managed to establish that it now sits with DEFRA, but there has been quite a bit of communication and a gap in who covers that issue. I appreciate that that is not a direct EA responsibility, but in your role, would you look to make sure that there is joined-up working and that that does not, to coin a phrase, literally fall between the cracks? It is an issue that matters hugely for the people whose homes, families and livelihoods are impacted by it.

Lisa Tennant: It is not something I have prepared for the Committee, but I am certainly happy to look at it. It is frustrating when we have these issues where it is not clear where they sit, as I said about the reservoirs issue in Scotland. I have a business in Argyll, near the Rest and Be Thankful pass, on the A82, where there are regular landslides, so I understand the impact that has on rural communities. When there is a landslide there, that road is closed and there is no way round, so I understand the seriousness of the issue and I am very happy to look into it.

Chair: You powerfully shared your personal reaction to the “Dirty Business” drama. We have had various questions about the climate emergency and the impact we are already seeing, particularly over the last summer, with wildfires and the hottest summer on record, and now going into a period of drought. You would be taking on this role in the organisation at a critical time for humanity. Do you have any personal reflections on the climate emergency and how you see your role in responding to that?

Lisa Tennant: My personal reflection would be to go back to my children. The question of what kind of world there will be for them to inherit is one of the motivations for the work that I do. My take on it is that variability is increasing, unpredictability is increasing and volatility is going up. I think that there is a human tendency to think that that will last for a period then things will settle down or go back to how they were. I believe that this volatility in climate will carry on now. What I have said that I personally believe is that nature is just as important as climate, and nature recovery and biodiversity recovery are a key priority for the Environment Agency, too.

Chair: I am looking to see whether colleagues have any last-minute questions, and they do not, so thank you, Lisa, for your time and attention to the Committees this morning.
