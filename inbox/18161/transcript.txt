Examination of witnesses

Sarah Cardell and Will Hayter.

Q1 The Chair: Good afternoon. I welcome everyone to this meeting of the Communications and Digital Committee. Today, we are revisiting a long strand of work for the committee by examining the regulation of digital markets. Last January, a new regime for managing competition issues in UK digital markets came into effect, giving the Competition and Markets Authority a substantial suite of new powers. Sarah and Will, you gave evidence to the committee at about that time, and I am very pleased to welcome you back today to give us an update on progress. Thank you for joining us.

Given the flurry of announcements that we have seen over the recent days, it would be good if we start there. Could you talk us through the key points of the announcements made in the last few days relating to investigations under the digital markets competition regime?

Sarah Cardell: We can absolutely do that. Would it be helpful to give a quick broad overview of our implementation of the regime more generally, or would you prefer to come back to that and we start with the most recent announcements?

The Chair: Start with the most recent announcements.

Sarah Cardell: Will, do you want to take those in that case?

Will Hayter: Thank you. As you say, we made two announcements in recent days as part of building momentum and delivery on the regime as a whole through the autumn. I will take them in order.

Last Friday, we announced three related steps. The first was that we proposed to designate Microsoft with strategic market status for its business software ecosystem. There are five services included in that proposed designation. They are services used by over a million customers in the public and private sectors in the UK, and around 20 million end users.

Secondly, to accompany that, we published an indicative road map, explaining the areas that we want to look at further to work out whether there are potential interventions that might be needed, subject to the final designation decision. Any such interventions would be intended to get better outcomes for UK customers in the public and private sectors.

Thirdly, accompanying that, we gave an update on the engagement that we have had in recent months with Amazon and Microsoft, through which we have been looking to reduce barriers to switching and to multicloud. The headline update there was good progress so far, with concrete steps to removing data transfer charges for switching and reducing those charges for multicloud, and having processes in place for supporting interoperability requests, as well as rolling out products to connect their data centres directly to further support multicloud and the ease of switching. Good progress, but more to be done. The firms need to continue to deliver on the steps that they have promised. We identified with them two further areas to look at, which are security across clouds and reducing friction so that applications built for portability can indeed deliver that portability. This is a progress update, with a further review from our board in the spring. That was last Friday.

Today, we have announced that we are consulting on a proposed conduct requirement on Apple, to require access to the NFC technology on iPhones on fair and effective terms. Contactless technology is widely used in the UK for payments and beyond, into things such as ID or physical access management to buildings and cars and so forth. Recent research shows that about 60% of adults use a digital wallet in the UK at the moment. Apple does provide NFC access, but we have heard widespread concerns from UK businesses that the terms on which it provides that access are not commercially viable. The particular concerns are that the fees and other terms are too onerous, and that the technical standards across platforms and across countries can make it difficult for competitors and developers to build and to compete.

The requirement we are proposing aims to address two main barriers: first, providing access on both the main technical standards to support payment and non-payment use cases; and, secondly, making sure that fees and other terms allow viable businesses to develop. In the round, we expect this to support growth in a key UK sector, fintech, and more broadly in choice and innovation for consumers.

Q2 The Chair: To go back to your point, Sarah, and thinking about the digital markets regime broadly, what do you see as the CMA’s main achievements since it came into force in January 2025?

Sarah Cardell: As you say, we were last before the committee back in January last year, at the outset of the regime. Over the subsequent 21 months, or thereabouts, implementing the regime has been an absolute priority for the CMA—for me and Will, as the lead executives, and for the CMA board. A number of our board members are directly involved as members of our decision-making sub-committees.

What has that prioritisation meant in practice? For us, it is about speed to impact. It is about making sure that we are taking action across a whole suite of areas, as Will has already touched on, where we can deliver tangible improvements for UK businesses and UK consumers who are dependent on these digital markets, with opportunities to innovate and grow in this space.

We have been very mindful as we have implemented the regime of the intent of Parliament that this is a regime that is participative. We have engaged very actively, not only with the prospective firms, and in some cases designated firms, but with a whole host of different stakeholders, UK players and internationally, that have a direct interest in these markets. That participative approach is absolutely key.

Another element is proportionality. We want to take action that is targeted, bespoke and does what it needs to do but does not go further than that. In particular, with a keen focus on impact, we are taking action in areas where the CMA is best placed to act, so that we can deliver that impact. That is our strategic approach.

What has that meant in practice? To put Will’s resumé of our most recent announcements in context of the totality of what we have delivered thus far, within the first year we launched and completed three SMS designation investigations. As I am sure the committee is well aware, that is a designation for Google in relation to search, Google in relation to mobile, and Apple in relation to mobile. As Will said, there is also now a provisional designation decision on Microsoft’s business software ecosystem.

As we are implementing and progressing those designations, we are mindful of striking the right balance between the breadth of areas that we are covering through those designation investigations and the need to move with speed to implement targeted interventions off the back of those interventions once they are in place. As soon as we had the designations completed for search and for mobile, we moved forward.

In Google’s case, for search, we set out in our road map a series of interventions that we expected to take over the first half of the designation period, and we have moved at pace to introduce a first suite of conduct requirements. We can cover the detail of those if the committee would like, but a good example would be the measures that we introduced to give publishers more control over their content for AI overviews. That is a world first, in forcing that change through, and it is something Google have rolled out. That is one example there.

The Chair: We have questions on that later.

Sarah Cardell: On mobile, we secured immediate commitments from Apple and Google to improve the fairness and the transparency for app developers—for example, for app reviews and app rankings. We have also consulted on conduct requirements for app store fees and steering, as well as, as Will just mentioned, in relation to NFC access.

We have published the road map for Microsoft. As Will said—this is an important point, and one I am sure the committee will want to come back to—we think that we can maximise the impact of this regime by using not just formal interventions but identifying the right opportunities to use informal engagement to secure change. Obviously, the benefit of that is that we can do that incredibly quickly. We have done that with commitments to some of those mobile interventions, and, most recently, as Will said, some of the progress that we have been able to secure in cloud. We recognise that there is an important balance to strike between where we use informal engagement and where we move directly to formal intervention.

Overall, I firmly believe that we have implemented the regime with absolute priority, determination and a good track record of delivery. There is obviously more to come. We recognise that there is a whole host of issues that we can and should be considering action on for the existing designations and potential future designations. We are absolutely determined to keep that programme of work moving.

Q3 The Chair: On the more-to-come front, what are the priorities and expected milestones over the next 12 months, including following your proposed SMS designation of Microsoft? I have another question that I can roll into that one. You have proposed designation without conduct requirements. You talked about conduct requirements in a different case. Will you cover why you have not put conduct requirements, as with earlier proposed designations, in the case of the Microsoft SMS designation?

Sarah Cardell: Will, do you want to pick that one up first?

Will Hayter: It is worth recognising where we are in the process, first of all, with Microsoft. We have just published a provisional decision, and alongside that we published a road map. There are some differences with this process on Microsoft business software compared to where we were at the equivalent point on search and mobile, but there are good reasons for those differences.

In search and mobile, those kicked off, as we said, at the very beginning of the regime. Before the regime went live, we had already done some significant pieces of work, including market studies on the search side and the mobile side, and had already been thinking about the potential steps that might be needed. So in those road maps, we laid out a bit more of a sequence and put them into categories: the first category to move to most immediately, and a second category that was also of a high priority but would take slightly longer. We had some issues in those road maps, where we were keeping a watching eye on international developments, and then there were some that we were leaving for the second half of the designation.

On Microsoft, this started only five months ago, in May, from not quite a standing start but much more of a standing start for the formal process and without that backdrop of a big programme of work in the run-up to it. At this stage, what we have done is not to specify particular requirements that might be needed, but rather to talk about four focus areas where we want to prioritise and consider further work, to make sure we have understood fully the concerns that customers have expressed in those areas, and then work out what interventions, if any, might be needed. The four areas are: cloud licensing, which is the thread back through to some of the previous work on the market investigation on cloud—a separate project; AI features in productivity software; communications and collaboration software, such as Teams; and security software. Those are broader priority areas, rather than specified potential rules.

Q4 The Chair: Do you have the power from the Act to do those things in parallel, and to specify conduct requirements as you work through?

Will Hayter: Yes, subject to a final designation decision, of course, we are exactly progressing the thinking in the run-up to that point.

Q5 The Chair: Could you say when you expect the first conduct requirement to be in force and what change customers could see? Can you do a bit of looking forward on that one?

Will Hayter: There is a bit of a limit to how much leaping forward we can do at this point, given what I have said about the stage of the thinking we are at on potential interventions. The focus for the nine months—the statutory period for the designation investigation—is very much on making sure that we get that assessment right. There is a lot to it, and we are considering a number of digital activities. As I said, as part of that we are making sure that we are hearing from customers on the things that are on their minds and where there might be issues. Those mostly tend to relate to the ability of competitors to offer services in parts of the Microsoft ecosystem but connect them to customers’ own deployment of the Microsoft suite, and issues raised on the way that Microsoft does its commercial bundling and the choices that might cause customers to make.

Cloud licensing is the one exception where we are a little further ahead in understanding what those issues are. The concern is that, given the way Microsoft licenses some of its longer-standing services to customers, that may limit the ability of some key competitors to offer a competitive cloud service to those customers.

Q6 The Chair: The final point from me, before we move on to other questions, is this. What lessons have you learned from implementing the regime so far and how will those inform your operations in future?

Will Hayter: I will kick this one off, and I am sure Sarah might add to it.

I will highlight three points. The first point, and this builds on what Sarah was saying, is recognising the value of having a range of tools to achieve impact. We are focused at all times on positive impact for the UK—UK businesses and UK consumers—and we are trying to take maximum advantage of the full range of tools. In some cases, it is formal SMS designation and conduct requirements. In some cases, there may be value in the voluntary commitment approach if we can get to impact faster. As I mentioned, on the cloud piece, we think we have been able to get material progress in the absence of an SMS process. That is the first point: the range of tools.

The second point is the value of a flexible legal framework and having principles in the law, rather than specific black and white rules written into the law upfront. You can see that in the way that our thinking on the publisher controls has evolved and continues to evolve. We put that requirement out in its final form on 3 June this year. If we had had to write that 18 months previously, it might have looked quite different. AI overviews and AI mode were only a few months old at that point, and it would have been quite hard to get that right. As it was, we were able to work closely with Google, and, importantly, on the publisher side, with all those firms, to get that rule right for the moment, recognising that it may need to change over time as the market evolves and as Google’s approach to the way it draws on publishers’ content evolves. That is the second point: the value of a flexible framework.

The third point is that we set out our stall, all the way through, in the run-up to the regime going live, on the importance of a participative approach, which we reinforced in our thinking on the four Ps and how we would implement that in the digital markets regime. We think that the value of that has been borne out in practice. I have mentioned in the context of the publisher piece that we are much more likely to get to actual solutions that will have an effect in the market more quickly if we work in a more collaborative, solution-focused way with the SMS firms, or possible SMS firms, while also making sure that we get a rounded range of input from everyone else in a given area, rather than defaulting to an adversarial approach, as might be the case in some other contexts.

Sarah Cardell: If I could answer that very briefly, building on Will’s middle point about flexibility and agility. This is not a surprising lesson, because it was very much the intent of Parliament, but we are seeing the real benefit of the way that this regime is designed, perhaps in contrast to some of the equivalent regimes in other jurisdictions, to deal with such dynamic and fast-moving markets.

On our ability to flex over time, what are the issues that we want to prioritise and focus on? This comes back to your question. There is a balance for us around how much clarity, predictability and certainty we can provide about that road map, or path forward, of interventions while retaining some flexibility to be able to respond.

A great example of that is the inclusion of the focus on Copilot in the Microsoft designation, which probably a year ago we would not have had in our sights in quite the same way. However, it became clear to us that was a very important part of that designation, and is one of the areas that we have highlighted for at least potential intervention going forward.

It is about getting the balance between making sure that we are doing enough to tackle those known, perhaps more historic but certainly very present harms, and recognising the agility to help to anticipate and keep open the ecosystems as they are evolving and developing. We all knew that that would be a measure of success of the regime, but we are seeing the benefit of the regime design now playing through.

Q7 Baroness Owen of Alderley Edge: Last year, you told us that the CMA would launch strategic market status investigations into three distinct areas of digital activity within the first six months of the regime being operational. Instead, it launched investigations covering only two areas—search and mobile platforms—and did not open another until May 2026. Can you explain what caused this delay?

Sarah Cardell: It probably links back to the reflections on lessons and experience. Our priority initially, as we said, was to launch those designation investigations for search and mobile. One of my reflections—I do not know whether Will shares this—is that we thought of those as two investigations. They really were very much three investigations. It was not the case that running mobile was a single investigation. We had to run it, unsurprisingly, quite distinctly for Google and for Apple, both of which, as the committee is well aware, are very well resourced and potentially adversarial firms, making sure that we had really strong, sound designation findings. It is fair to say that that took more resource and more time than we might have anticipated. In part, it was about making sure that we absolutely locked in those designations.

That goes to the previous question about how quickly we get to conduct requirements and why we are not doing those in parallel with designations. It is because the most important thing for us to do in a designation investigation is to land a solid, appeal-proof designation. If we do not get that off the blocks then we will spend a long time in litigation, before we can even start to make inroads to change. The first thing is that we had to focus on those, and that consumed much of our time and effort.

We were, in parallel, thinking hard about cloud. Cloud was very much in our sights as the potential next area for designation, as Will said, because the cloud market investigation was happening on an overlapping timeframe.

As we were working through cloud as a prospective option—this goes to the point about responding to the evolving dynamics of wider markets—we were looking hard at what was happening in the wider business software ecosystem. The evolution that we were seeing, particularly with Copilot coming in over that timeframe, made us stop and think very hard and ask: if we are thinking about our next designation, should it be cloud or should it be the broader Microsoft-focused business software ecosystem?

As Will said, because we had done less work on that, we were, in the background, using our formal powers and gathering information, but had not publicly announced, so that when we went to the board in the spring of this year we were able to give them two well-developed options: do you want to move forward with a cloud designation, or do you want to move forward with a Microsoft-focused business software ecosystem designation, within which are some alternative ways in which we can tackle the issues identified for cloud? That took time to work through.

It is fair to say that it was probably a combination of a bit of overambition on our part with the initial timeframe and the need to work up those two options so that we could have a well-informed discussion with them in the spring.

Q8 Baroness Owen of Alderley Edge: Thank you so much. Under the Digital Markets Act, the European Commission has already fined Apple and Google over their app store practices. Has the UK’s more tailored regime come at the expense of swift delivery? I would like to add for the record that I was a guest of Google at its AI policy conference in December 2024.

Sarah Cardell: I will make one general comment and then ask Will to come in on the details.

Going back to the design of the UK regime, the measure of success is not about imposing penalties on these firms; it is about driving changes in conduct. That is why we have a focus on doing that, whether that is through conduct requirements, commitments or other informal engagement. The first point is that the absence of a fine is, frankly, a success, because it shows that our focus is on constructive engagement and delivering outcomes. That is the better thing, where possible, to focus on.

On how we are doing relative to the European Commission on issues of mobile, there is not much between us. Will, can you pick that up in detail?

Will Hayter: I am happy to. The DMA came into force earlier, and, as I know you will appreciate, is set up in quite a different way, in that the designations and a whole set of requirements came into force immediately. That means that it is not tailored in quite the same way as what we are doing here.

As Sarah says, we are focused on impact. We did our designations as quickly as we reasonably could, recognising the nine-month timetable and the need to get those right. We then have legally binding requirements in search, as well as three conduct requirements now in place, and we are in the process of working through the final stages on the fourth. Practical changes came into force on 1 April on the mobile side with those commitments with both Apple and Google. That is on app store issues, app store review, app store ranking and app store data use, and Apple’s interoperability process, and then the concrete changes that I have talked about on the cloud side.

It is worth recognising that the process of getting these requirements right, and tailoring them in the right way, does not happen overnight. That is where the good work needs to get done, so that by the time that we put it in place we are confident that it will be effective. I am glad to say that we think that should be the case with the publisher conduct requirement, for example, and the other two that went into force with Google earlier in the year. We can explain more about that if that would be helpful.

Q9 Baroness Owen of Alderley Edge: Thank you so much. We have already touched slightly on capacity issues, but we understand that staffing in the Digital Markets Unit has fallen by 16% since last January. Does the CMA have adequate resource and expertise to deliver a digital markets regime at pace?

Sarah Cardell: Again, Will might want to come in on some of the details.

It would be helpful to understand what is behind that percentage fall figure. That is not something that I recognise as a drop in staffing. We are well resourced to support our digital markets work, and the set of priorities that we currently have in train and the future work that we are in the process of planning.

I am conscious that this is, rightly, an area of interest for the committee. I wonder if it would be helpful to take a step back—tell me if it is not, because I could get into unhelpful detail—on our budget and resourcing position, and how we resource the digital markets work as part of our wider resourcing model. It probably would help to answer your question, but I do not want to go down a rabbit hole if it is unhelpful.

Baroness Owen of Alderley Edge: You could touch on it briefly, but it is about understanding whether you have adequate resources to tackle the issue.

Sarah Cardell: By way of background—I will try to keep it brief—the CMA’s resourcing position is set through our overall resource budget. That is set by the Treasury and is set as a total figure for the CMA’s work as a whole. It covers our resourcing, whether it is for digital markets, the consumer protection regime that came into force at the same time as the digital regime, mergers, markets and all of our respective front-line areas of work. As chief executive, I need ultimately to make the decisions about allocating resource across those different areas. We have 100 people or so working on our digital markets cases at the moment. That is a combination of lawyers, economists, front-line leaders of those projects and technologists, with a whole host of different skills. As I say, we are very confident that we are adequately resourced for the work programme that we have ongoing.

The complication with the budget perhaps relates to the fact that our digital markets activity is levy funded, in the sense that we can recover the cost of that activity from the designated firms. However, that is not a levy in the same form as, for example, other regulated sectors. It does not enable us simply to grow our digital markets activity without that coming at the expense of other areas of our work. If I told Will that I was going to give him 20% more resource on digital markets, I would then have to take that 20% resource from consumer protection or another area.

So there is a question here. If there is a desire for the CMA ultimately to do more, there would be opportunities to relax the rules around the levy funding and grow our resource that way. Of course, you can always do more if you have more resource, but we are confident that we have the resource we need to deliver the programme of work that we are currently planning. That is my best, not so brief answer, but I hope it is helpful.

Q10 Lord McNally: This must be a fairly small pool of expertise. Are there any restrictions on or incentives for changing sides, with you recruiting from the big companies or the big companies recruiting from you?

Sarah Cardell: Will, do you want to pick this up, as you are deep in our recruitment? We have a good talent pool coming in and flowing out. Perhaps you could elaborate.

Will Hayter: It is fair to recognise—this perhaps lies a bit behind your question—that there may be quite different things that attract people to either side of that divide. I may be a bit biased, but I think that it is an excellent team, with all the professions that Sarah mentioned, across lawyers, economists and tech insights, and the core digital markets team.

We are always interested to see if there are people who are interested in coming with direct experience from inside the tech sector, and we have a certain number of those. Q11 Baroness Fleet: As a yes and no answer, have you had any whistleblowers from Apple and Google join you in the last six months?

Sarah Cardell: I do not think that we have had anyone directly.

Will Hayter: No direct joiners from Apple or Google.

Q12 Baroness Caine of Kentish Town: You are confident that you have the resource to deliver the programme that you have strategically identified. Do you think the programme that you have identified is commensurate with the scale of the challenge that you are there to deal with?

Sarah Cardell: That is a very good question, to which there is not a straightforward yes or no answer. I am confident that, if we continue with the resourcing set-up that we have at the moment, we will deliver substantial impact over the next, let us say, three years. I am confident that we have sufficient resource to deliver commensurate with the ambition of the regime. I do not expect to be saying that we have not been able to deliver because we have not had enough resource. I do not think that we are in that position. Could we do more if we had more? Absolutely.

Q13 Lord Storey: Good afternoon. Your investigation into SMS launched to date has drawn heavily on previous research that you conducted well before the regime took effect. What work have you conducted to identify and prioritise new areas for SMS investigations?

Sarah Cardell: Will, do you want to pick that up, and also differentiate the Microsoft piece, which is somewhat in between the two?

Will Hayter: We are trying to strike a constant balance between making sure that we deliver and follow through on the things that we have committed to—the SMS designations investigations and the associated conduct requirements and other steps—and keeping an eye open for other issues that may be arising.

As you say, the moment of kick-off for the regime was unique, obviously, in that it happens only once, but we had all that previous work, particularly on search and mobile. We had the previous cloud market investigation that overlapped the start of the regime. That one is a bit unusual as well, because that then has overlapped with the thinking around Microsoft and the cloud-licensing issue that I mentioned. The reason that may be a concern is that it draws on the market power that Microsoft has in some of those core software services. We had a connected set of enterprise-facing issues across cloud and business software to think about, which was the subject of the March board discussion that Sarah was talking about.

We are keeping a watching eye on whether we might need to do future SMS designations. As you might expect, there are people coming to talk to us on a reasonably regular basis to ask what about this company or what about that company. The question of what is next is something that we will keep thinking about.

Sarah Cardell: To make that more concrete for you, if we think about the fact that we have designations now for Google and Apple, and prospectively for Microsoft, it will not surprise you to know that we hear a lot from third parties, “What about Amazon?”, in relation to the prior discussion on cloud and AWS and Amazon’s retail activities, and, “What about Meta?” We are seeing a lot of development in the Meta space as well at the moment. Those are areas where we are thinking hard about whether that is a prospective case for future designation.

What we are planning to do, and have committed to do already on cloud, is to bring back to our board in spring next year a further review of the progress on cloud outside of designation and a further review of whether that is sufficient progress or whether we should be moving to formal designation territory. We have committed to the board that we will review the position on cloud in the spring. I expect that, alongside that, a bit as we did this year, we would want to bring two or three other well worked-up options, so that the board has a good set of options. I do not want to say definitively now what those would be, but the candidates that I have mentioned might well be among that list. That is work that we are currently progressing, so that we are ready to have that board discussion in the spring.

Q14 Lord Storey: How are you ensuring that this work is informed by the views of a broad range of stakeholders, including companies of all different sizes?

Will Hayter: That has been a mantra throughout, as part of this participative approach. I have said on a number of occasions, possibly even in front of this committee, that participative does not mean a cosy relationship with the SMS firms or possible SMS firms. It means an open and collaborative relationship with all players in the market, in whatever the context is. You would need to ask the publishers, for example, in the Google search piece, or some of the app developers in the mobile space, to get the authoritative answer, but I think that we have managed to do that reasonably well.

In mentioning the publisher community, the publisher conduct requirement that we announced on 3 June was quite striking, in being well received by Google and by the publishers as a good step forward. We do not expect to repeat that on every occasion, but I hope that reflects that we have worked hard to get out and about and talk to all players in the market frequently. Indeed, I am seeing the publishers later this week to get the latest updates.

Sarah Cardell: That is probably the key driver of our prioritisation choices. When we were having the discussion with the board, for example, about cloud and the business software ecosystem, we heard a lot from UK businesses about the dependence on the Microsoft business software ecosystem. That does not mean that there were not also issues on cloud, but we could see up and down the land, in both the public and private sector, that dependence, which influenced the board’s choice on prioritisation. For the announcement that we made today, on access to Apple’s NFC technology, we have had huge engagement with the fintech community, which has been driving the opportunity to improve that access. For us, it is a critical measure of success that we are responding to those third-party views.

Q15 Lord Storey: There is probably no answer to this question; it is a short one. It is a little niggle I have. Will Hayter: In a way, you have hit the nail on the head as to what this regime is all about, and the way we understand that position of market power for Apple.
