Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Kevin Craven, Julian David, Arnab Dutt and Stephen Phipson.

Chair: I call to order today’s House of Commons Defence Committee evidence session on the defence investment plan and, in particular, its implications for UK industry. It is a great pleasure to welcome back some of the good and the great from industry to give evidence, starting off with Kevin Craven, chief executive at ADS. We also have with us Julian David, chief executive at techUK. Welcome back also to Arnab Dutt, policy champion for procurement and social value at the Federation of Small Businesses, and to Stephen Phipson, the CEO of MakeUK. Welcome and, without further ado, let me pass on to my colleague Lincoln Jopp, so that we can get the questions and answers under way.

Q1 Lincoln Jopp: Thank you all very much indeed for coming in today and giving us your time; I know that you are very busy people.

We had the strategic defence review and then the defence investment plan. When I worked in the main building at the Ministry of Defence, they used to say that plans without resources are hallucinations. We are keen to know whether the MOD is doing stuff or whether it is still hallucinating. What I would love to hear are some concrete examples of where that cascade of the priorities in the strategic defence review is reflected in the defence investment plan and has turned itself into either orders or changes in behaviour.

If I were to characterise the two main priorities of the SDR, they were NATO first but not NATO only, so a shift very much to the Euro-Atlantic geographical focus. That comes with NATO and a recalcitrant US. Are there any orders to fill gaps that have been identified if the US does not play so fulsomely?

The second is a shift away from deterrence to war-fighting readiness. If any of you could give us some sense of where you have seen real, concrete changes in processes, behaviours or order book stuff, I would be very interested to know. Shall we go down the line, because I think you will all have insights into this?

Kevin Craven: First, we should say that we welcome the strategic direction of the defence investment plan. There has been a pivot towards integrated systems, autonomy, AI and a number of things that reflect the lessons that we have learned in Ukraine.

However, it is also clear that the DIP was reasonably granular around the first four years of what, in old money, was the defence equipment plan, but thereafter less so. Even in those first four years, it was not clear whether around a third of the £298 billion announced is unfunded or not articulated in the right way. Broadly, that clarity around the near term is welcomed, if not complete.

The lack of clarity over the 10-year plan makes it very difficult for industry to start thinking about longer-term investment and infrastructure, and, in particular, ramping up industrial capacity. We are very concerned that the lack of a pathway towards a higher level of spending, be that 3%—or 3.5%, as NATO has stipulated—makes it even harder for industry to then scale up our capacity and production lines.

In terms of orders, what is probably interesting to note is that the equipment spend to the year ending March 2026 was £5 billion less than in the previous year, which is an indication of the MOD looking to remain within its budget or live within its means. We are hearing all of this language. The lived experience on the ground is that it is still hard to get orders.

Against that, we can also look at the procurement announcements that have been made since the publication of the DIP, and there are quite a lot of those, such as the Royal Oaks plan for the mobile docks at the Clyde, et cetera. I would particularly point to the rapid procurement processes, with £1.4 billion around defence AI and some of the other elements that go towards SMEs.

We are seeing behaviours shift, and the shape of the machine shift towards more modern warfare fighting, but, in terms of longer-term commitments over a 10-year period that enable certainty, investment plans, readiness and deterrence, we are not seeing those in sufficient detail yet.

Julian David: I would echo the fact that the SDR was very welcome, and the direction of travel was very good. Now that we have seen the DIP, that also has a plan that is welcome. From a tech point of view, defence-oriented tech members expected, therefore, that there would be a big uptick in the focus on tech and that orders would flow, particularly to SMEs. We have not found that to be the case.

Last time we were here, we reported on our survey of members, and 73% said that the market has declined since the report; that was in May. Funding delays and reductions have hit 87% of respondents, and 93% of them are now reassessing investment or recruitment of people to focus in this sector.

The problem, particularly from an SME point of view, is that they live off the cash flow. If there is no cash flow or no predictable path to cash, they are going to either try to find markets elsewhere or cut back and wait and see. We have checked again with them since that survey, and I have to say that they have not really seen a lot of progress.

I would highlight the fact that, in terms of delivery, the MOD is still not properly staffed in order to deal with procurement, particularly of new things and from smaller companies. Sometimes that is disguised, in that one commercial team’s headcount is 70%, with a gap of 30%, but 40% of the team is filled by contractors who cannot fulfil the whole process of sign-off authority. This is a real challenge that has been reported by members in that way.

I would echo Kevin’s point about clarity in terms of how much is actual spend, whether it is committed, and when it is going to come. Again, we see that the spread of it depends on a few things, even if that spread is even. We are still waiting for the Budget to say whether or not it will be. I hope that the fact that the previous Defence Secretary is now the Chancellor will help with that. There is a dependency on capital cuts elsewhere, which may or may not happen, and there is still a gap. We all want efficiencies and a shift to new types of faster procurement, but that is a problem.

One of the other things that I need to highlight is the change in security clearances, which is having a real effect because of the change in the way that it is done on the sponsoring.

Q2 Lincoln Jopp: Do you mean a detrimental effect?

Julian David: Yes, absolutely. It is affecting small businesses, but it is also affecting large businesses. I am sure that colleagues can talk about that as well, with people losing 1,600 clearances, and SMEs having to reapply because their sponsor route has gone. This is a cross-Government issue, but clearly very important in defence.

You mentioned the international context and the shift from transatlantic to an Atlantic and cross-channel. If you think about tech and dual-use, which is really close to defence tech, the European Cloud and AI Development Act, which builds on the safe exclusion to say that non-EU companies cannot supply dual-use cloud or AI technology in the EU, that affects the exporting capability of our industry. That is a big blow to the economy, but also affects the capacity and the resilience of the industry that is based here, and will encourage it to look elsewhere as well.

Q3 Lincoln Jopp: Were the figures that you were quoting from your survey a result of asking a question about their relations with the MOD? We go around, particularly in the High North, where we have just been focusing, and we have countries out there spending like drunken sailors on shore leave. Are they spending that with your members, notwithstanding what you just said about the AI bit at the end?

Julian David: Yes, that is happening, but, equally, we have international members that came here to invest, and they are not seeing the resulting pathway to growth and business, and so they are also saying, “Maybe I made the wrong decision to come and expand in the UK, and perhaps I should retrench and go elsewhere”. We are not in favour of a “buy British” approach, because that just reinforces all the challenges that our industry, which is very international, would have, but we are very much in favour of looking at capability in the UK, and encouraging new capability to flourish here, with support for exports, which are essential.

Arnab Dutt: It is good to have the opportunity to talk about the wider environment of SMEs, and not just those that are already in defence pipelines. Speaking directly, the publication of the defence investment plan was a really welcome step towards transparency, but it is very high-level. The long-term equipment plans do not really provide viable commercial signals to small businesses outside of the defence perimeter. We are really concerned about how the MOD looks beyond that horizon and ahead at the environment. We would echo my two colleagues here, Julian and Kevin, and are concerned about capacity and capability building.

The fixed pipeline documents fail to drive SME engagement effectively. I will give you a few figures from our own research. Only 13% of small businesses use pipeline documentation to find public opportunities, compared to 28% who use active procurement platforms. That means that the vast majority are not even looking, so there is a question about pre-engagement and engagement with the external SME supply chain.

Three-fifths—62%—of small businesses are looking for public sector contracts but report that finding suitable opportunities is incredibly difficult. Demonstrating that a passive publication of equipment will do anything to improve that situation is rather fanciful, in our opinion. Contracts stemming from the DIP will continue to flow exclusively to SMEs already in those supply chains, and to the prime contractors.

So far, I have to say, as an organisation, we have had minimal consultation on the DIP with the MOD, which has been really disappointing, because it does not give us the opportunity to help the MOD and give it advice and support. That has been our main bugbear with this process since the publication of the DIP.

Q4 Lincoln Jopp: When you last came here, the MOD was talking about the launch of its SME hub. Is that real or is that just another case of all hat and no cattle?

Arnab Dutt: It may have happened, but we certainly do not know much about it, because the engagement has been incredibly sparse. We have had no cadence. We have been given no notification. All of those things were promised by the MOD, as well as the fact that we would be able to engage in a timely manner. That has not happened.

Chair: We will be coming back in detail on all this towards the end of the meeting and will have a specific section on the Defence Office for Small Business Growth, so just hold your thoughts there.

Arnab Dutt: Another awful piece of research is that 19% of companies that have already engaged with the MOD said that they will not be doing so again because of the systemic barriers and the level of hard work and engagement. Among that 19%, there could be some brilliant businesses, so that is another issue.

Q5 Lincoln Jopp: It is, I am afraid, a story that is only too common. Stephen, what is your perspective?

Stephen Phipson: The answer is that it is patchy. We see a slightly different perspective on that. The DIP is welcome in many respects, particularly around the autonomy brief and what is happening in terms of bringing SMEs into the innovation part of it. We see a number of actions that are happening, and different behaviours coming from MOD at the moment.

I would cite things such as the energetics programme, the low-cost one-way effectors, and what we are doing with drones, where we have brought not only defence suppliers, but also, because we have a very wide brief in MakeUK around the whole of manufacturing, non-traditional defence suppliers into those sorts of development activities with the MOD. That is a change of behaviour and is something worth noting, to give them some credit, but it is a very small part of what is outlined in the DIP.

The point that we get from many companies is that you cannot invest on press releases. We need detail and funding, and we need to make that work. In a number of those projects where they have focused, we definitely have seen a change in behaviour, which is bringing lots of new SMEs into the supply chain.

The point that I would make is that a lot of SMEs are doing very well on the export market. To your point about everyone spending like drunken sailors, we are seeing a lot of that. A lot of them are doing really well with lots of innovative technologies, going out and exporting to European defence authorities, and seeing it as very slow from the UK perspective.

The other point is that we have had a lot of manufacturing companies that are not normally defence companies wanting to enter the market. We have a big service helping them to make that transition. It is very difficult. There is a lot of enthusiasm, but there is no direct funding, orders or requirements.

We have the enthusiasm to do it in terms of supplying and fulfilling the DIP, but we need to see some certainty to get it delivered. I would just push back a little bit and say that there are some pockets where the MOD has changed behaviour.

Q6 Lincoln Jopp: One thing that I do not think will be covered later, to the Chair’s point, is that the MOD, as we know, puts massive barriers to entry in terms of SMEs doing business. Have we seen any change in the behaviours of the primes towards their SMEs, to the good or the bad? Our model needs totally reworking. Having 18 primes does not work for me. Have we seen any tangible change—it will not be by all primes; it might be by one or two primes—in the way in which they are investing in their relationship with those SMEs that are crucial to their business?

Kevin Craven: Broadly, you have seen some incremental improvements. Some of the primes are stepping up in terms of improved payment terms, direct account management for smaller primes, and easier routes to entry.

Q7 Lincoln Jopp: Feel free to name the ones that are doing well.

Kevin Craven: I would probably call out Lockheed Martin as one that has had a prompt payment code for a long time. There are others, such as BAE and Babcock, which are building better routes for SMEs as we speak, but, in fairness, it is patchy. Some of that relates back to the lack of longer-term certainty from the MOD in terms of 10-year programmes, and they are unable to pass that certainty on to the supply chain. The capacity of both the primes and the MOD to respond to those small businesses remains low, in my view.

Julian David: It is not that primes do not want to work with SMEs, and I am sure that Stephen and Arnab can give examples of that, but the procurement environment and the way that things are happening does push towards going through the prime rather than getting directly to the SMEs. Of course, that potentially excludes a lot of innovation.

SME spend by May 2028 is £7.5 billion, of which only £2 billion is direct and £5.5 billion is going to be indirect, and the procedure to check on this is tracking rather than committing and delivering. We have said many times that what are needed here are targets and KPIs for the people doing the procurement, and measuring progress against those, as opposed to waiting until the end of 2028 and then asking whether it happened.

Q8 Lincoln Jopp: Institutions get the behaviour that they reward.

Julian David: Yes.

Lincoln Jopp: That is why I was keen for Lockheed Martin and others to get a mention.

Julian David: The other thing that I would just point out is that the MOD has chosen to use a different definition of SME than is generally understood.

Q9 Lincoln Jopp: It is below 200 employees, is it not?

Julian David: Yes, but that is different from the Procurement Act and fair payment code. SMEs that are used to reporting in that way now have to report in another way, which does not seem to make any sense.

Q10 Emma Lewell: Good morning, all. We are coming up to about a year since the national armaments director was appointed. He assured me and this Committee that things would improve and that procurement would get better within the first year. Do you feel that it has?

Julian David: The plan and the intentions are good. We submitted to the Defence Industrial Joint Council a 30-point series of things that we thought would make a big difference here. The national armaments director agreed to adopt pretty much all of them.

What I would come back to, though, is that, if we do not have granularity behind the DIP in terms of what they are going to do and when are they going to do it at a granular level of procurement, and if we do not track performance, we are probably not going to see that. The second thing is the difficulty of addressing the gaps in capability within MOD procurement, which seems to be very challenging.

Q11 Emma Lewell: Have you heard rumours about the DIP implementation plan? Have you seen it? Do you know what it is? We do not.

Julian David: We have heard the rumours.

Q12 Emma Lewell: Are you confident that it is going to come forward? Does it exist?

Julian David: I think it is October.

Kevin Craven: We are engaging with each of the frontline commands in terms of a programme of consultation about unpacking the DIP and the implementation thereof. To be fair to the frontline commands, they too have some of the lack of certainty that is required around that implementation plan and the pace at which it is going to go. The NAD group, which is the total aggregation of departments that now report into Rupert Pearce, is some 25,000 people.

In parallel with the announcement of the DIP and implementation of the SDR, defence reform is going on. The challenge of transforming the Department while implementing a gigantic strategic shift is causing large amounts of pain within that Department, so there is still some way to go in terms of the implementation being granular, transparent and obvious to industry.

Stephen Phipson: The problem is that it is all too slow. We are constantly being told in the media that we are under threat and that 2030 is a key date. The way that things are going at the moment, if there is another delay in the spending review in terms of finding the money for the DIP, factories are not built overnight. People have to make investment plans and we have to build capability. Some of the changes in behaviour have been good, but they are minimal. They are mainly on the new capability.

If we see what is happening on autonomy—we mentioned things such as cheap one-way effectors—that is good. That is a different way of doing things with non-traditional defence suppliers, but it is tiny compared to what we need to do. The overriding sense is one of speed, pace, delivery and moving forwards quickly. That would be one message to take from this.

Q13 Emma Lewell: Since the publication of the DIP, have you seen any significant contracts being awarded?

Stephen Phipson: We have seen a few.

Julian David: Yes, and some of them are, as Stephen said, in the new areas. techUK is very keen to support that and we have had engagement and hosted members with MOD officials, but we are talking about relatively small amounts of money and relatively small programmes.

Q14 Emma Lewell: When contracts are awarded, are they too concentrated in the south-east and south-west? The Government want good growth in every postcode, and want defence to be an engine for growth in every region, but it seems to all get concentrated in the same regions, time and time again. That is my view, but you might disagree.

Stephen Phipson: We have national coverage and thousands of companies all around the UK. We do see clusters of technology. At the moment, the north-east is doing very well with technology. For example, cyber in defence is very strong in the north-east. It depends on where the existing pockets of capability are, but I would not say that there is a particular concentration in that respect. We are seeing some good effects.

We are also seeing some very interesting new stuff happening in defence with the automotive sector around the west midlands, where there is a lot of interest in using its capability, but it is early days yet. In terms of large contracts, we have not seen that.

Kevin Craven: The figures on spend per head per region that the MOD used to publish, but has either slowed down or not published, remain broadly the same. The south-west and south-east are still significant areas, and regions such as Northern Ireland and Scotland less so.

Julian David: If you have the traditional spending relationships, you will risk reinforcing that. The tech industry has a bias to certain parts of the country. There is a lot in London and the south-east, but also in Cambridge, Manchester, Newcastle and Scotland. It is entirely possible to spread it, but, if the relationships stay with the current players, that will leave the concentrations where they are.

Chair: Let us now go on to the level of detail within and the funding of the 2026 defence investment plan.

Q15 Ian Roome: We have come a long way. The SDR was published in June 2025. The DIP was going to come that autumn, and we waited and waited, and we waited even bloomin’ longer, did we not, until June 2026? Kevin, as you alluded to earlier, for industry, how does the DIP compare to the equipment plans, and does it provide enough detail and confidence to you, or would you have preferred something similar to keeping the equipment plan?

Kevin Craven: There are things in the DIP that are welcome. The focus on the industrial context and capacity is useful and helpful in terms of building the resilience that we need. The focus of the DIP is very much on the first four-year period to 2029, whereas the defence equipment plan used to focus on a granular acquisition pipeline over 10 years.

We can see that the rough shape of the DIP is around 50% to 55% of the total spend over the 10-year period, so some spend has been brought forward to improve the readiness of what we are doing, but there is not enough granularity to provide certainty to industry. Intent is not the same as a commitment that is suitable for investors in defence.

Stephen Phipson: I have been doing this for a long time, to say the least, and, as Kevin said, there is not sufficient detail. It is nothing like an equipment plan. This is not investable as it stands at the moment. It gives you a thematic direction of travel in many areas, a lot of which is welcome and is great news, and attracts a lot of new companies into the market, but the questions are then around where the contracts, the programme and the procurement are. We do not have that yet, and that is the problem.

Julian David: A good thing about the DIP is the point that Kevin made, which is that it is a more comprehensive view of what is needed, which came from the SDR, as you say, and that is very welcome. We would like to see that extended to a shift towards software-defined procurement. Stand-alone equipment purchases really do not work very well in the modern defence environment. We would also like to see, within the plan now, the granularity and specificity, and also responsibility.

Awareness of the DIP delivery plan is low. The national armaments director has confirmed that they are converting it into a more granular plan, coming in October, I believe, but does that then have the programme-level funding, the dates and the route to market? Coming back to my point, if you read through the SME action plan, it is a cautionary tale; it says “we” throughout, with no owners or KPIs, and just the one target date. We all have to wait until after 2028 to find out whether it has worked.

Arnab Dutt: To send demand signals out to the broader supply chain, we need far more information and, following on from that, a plan around regional and country-wide engagement. That will probably be the process that begins to open up opportunities for SMEs as they get ready. If they see that coming down the line, that is the real demand signal, but that is not there.

Julian David: I would also like to point out that this costs the country money, because delay in an inflationary context and, particularly in tech, an availability crisis of certain components means that what you are going to do costs more when you get around to doing it.

Q16 Ian Roome: The MOD director general for commercial and industry says that he has had a really positive response from industry to the DIP and the demand signal that it sets out. Is that the feedback that you are getting from your members?

Julian David: Has there been a really positive response to the DIP? It is a good plan, but what we now need to see is the detail and the commitments.

Kevin Craven: It is a strategic plan. It is not a detailed, granular plan as we were used to with the acquisition pipeline that was represented by the 10-year equipment plan.

Q17 Ian Roome: Would you say that it has left more questions than answers?

Kevin Craven: The short answer is no. There is a clear shift in strategic direction. We have talked about autonomy and other changes in terms of that particular—I would call out frontier AI and other things—but the lack of certainty over that 10-year period is a real problem. Headlines do not make for good investment.

Q18 Ian Roome: How damaging would it be if the next spending review failed to provide a firm, funded pathway towards 3% of GDP on defence? Would industry still invest on the strength of the current DIP?

Stephen Phipson: The answer is that it will not. It is only going to invest when there is certainty on what the programme looks like, and we have seen this for decades in defence. It has to be around a certain programme. The thematics that we have are all good and welcome.

To your earlier point, industry welcomes the new technologies and innovation, but, until we see certainty, no one is going to invest a penny. We need to see where those contracts are going to come from. We will play with all the innovation that is happening with the MOD, which is good, but we need to start building factories and capability.

Julian David: The fact that we have had certain deadlines that have either not been met or, when they have arrived, have been met at a very summary level is a learned behaviour. If you contrast that with other geographies that are spending, you do have the open question of, “Why invest here?”

Arnab Dutt: I would echo what Julian said. We have some brilliant firms, especially in some of our tech clusters, and they are finding opportunities abroad, and particularly in the Baltics and northern Europe, where those countries are getting on with it because they see the threat as imminent. If their capacity is taken up, even though they would love to be working here, we are going to lose out. By not addressing this, getting that granularity and funding it properly, the risk to us in terms of capability over the next few years is potentially very dangerous, in my opinion.

Q19 Ian Roome: Have any of your members left the UK and gone abroad?

Kevin Craven: Yes.

Q20 Ian Roome: If you had to put a percentage on it, what would that be?

Kevin Craven: It is low numbers, but there are companies that are leaving. The US and Europe represent more attractive defence markets at the moment.

Q21 Ian Roome: I will leave my questioning with the same question to each of you. If you could sort out one thing from the MOD today regarding this particular area of the DIP, what would you say that it needs to do urgently, in terms of what your members require?

Kevin Craven: I am going to say two things. The first is rapid procurement. We have a number of rapid procurement routes that work, and Commercial X has done some good work in this space. The second is resourcing the Defence Office for Small Business Growth. There are four people in that office versus the 29 that are suggested as being appropriate.

Julian David: I would echo that the contracting backlog really does need to be sorted out. Otherwise, how can we contract new stuff as well? In order to encourage investment, we have to have a delivery plan that is multi-year, granular and signals the implementation of the SDR.

Arnab Dutt: Granularity is really important, but I want to echo what Kevin said. We have fantastic exemplars of super practice and speedy procurement where barriers have been removed, and Commercial X is one of them. It has been running since 2022. If the MOD was able to use that as an exemplar across the wider field of MOD procurement, that would be a great start. It is not like reinventing the wheel. We are doing it really well. That project is incredibly successful in giving SMEs contracts, so use the exemplar.

Stephen Phipson: There are two things from my perspective. One is to echo the points about funding certainty, which is going to drive investment in terms of people being clear about when things are required and what they are going to cost.

There is a wider point that I would like to make about manufacturing in this country, which is something that we have been talking to the Chancellor about. In fact, Julian and I were with the Chancellor this morning on this very subject. We are now losing a lot of companies because it is uncompetitive to manufacture goods here. That is because of energy costs in the UK.

This year, we have had an enormous flight of businesses out of the UK. In particular, larger companies are finding it hard to allocate capital to UK capability because the maths works better if you do it in France and Germany than it does in the UK. It is very difficult to isolate that just to the MOD, but there is a wider issue about the competitiveness of manufacturing in this country, which, to be fair, the Chancellor recognised this morning as a key issue for growth.

Julian David: The number of companies saying, “We are not going to be here anymore. We are going to be somewhere else” is low, but they are shifting emphasis and investment money.

Going back to the sovereignty point, if we get into, “Buy British”, “Buy European” or “Buy US”, that is going to mean that, given the relative spends of those markets, there will be more emphasis on companies shifting their entire locus to one of those geographic entities.

Q22 Chair: Let us move on to the funding profile. It has been set out in the defence investment plan, but it sees the majority of that investment appear after 2030, so backloading. That is something that we, as a Committee, raised with the Secretary of State and the permanent secretary during our session just last week. Is there sufficient capacity within the UK defence industry to bring any of that planned investment forward, if required?

Kevin Craven: We have great underlying capacity in the UK and, as has already been mentioned, in other interested sectors, such as automotive, as we are seeing in other international competitors such as Germany, but you cannot switch on industrial capacity and production lines overnight. You need a strong demand signal over a long number of years to be able to do so, so a ramp-up towards the back end of a period is not helpful. It creates a future bottleneck where firms will compete for the necessary skills, monies and other industrial requirements at the same time, so it is not a helpful profile in any way.

Q23 Chair: Basically, what you are saying is that there is sufficient capacity to bring some of that investment forward—or are you not saying that?

Kevin Craven: With advance warning, there is sufficient capability to build the capacity that we need, but to build a brand new production line from scratch will take between two and four years, depending on what we are talking about. At the moment, we are not seeing sufficient certainty to enable that investment to take place.

Q24 Chair: Mr David, from techUK’s perspective, do we have capacity?

Julian David: The UK has a really large and capable tech industry in terms of indigenous as well as people from other countries who have invested here, and so, in one sense, there is no capacity issue. It could respond. The challenge has been that they need to have a plan that is set, and to know what they are going to invest in, particularly in terms of smaller companies.

As Kevin has said, if you do not have sight of when that capacity is potentially going to be productive, that is quite hard to do. We could do an awful lot more on some of the newer technology areas.

The other thing to watch out for is the bottlenecks that are going to happen and are already happening to components, such as memory and processors, and there will be other bottlenecks. Having a plan where people can procure ahead of time is incredibly helpful to them.

Q25 Chair: Mr Dutt, from the perspective of SMEs, is there capacity?

Arnab Dutt: We believe that there is. Adopting the dual capacity that they have done in northern Europe and the Baltic states is a really good exemplar of how you can do it, but you need time, access to finance and the right type of investment. What we worry about is the problem that we already have in the economy, which is the ability to source skilled labour. That is where the bottlenecks are going to occur. It is something that we really have to address, especially if we want to act quickly, because it is already a real problem for many small manufacturing, engineering and tech companies.

Q26 Chair: Mr Phipson, what is MakeUK’s perspective?

Stephen Phipson: We do not lack capacity at all. We certainly have it there. We have thousands of SMEs that are exporting defence technology at the moment to other nations. I can tell you from first-hand experience that they would much rather be doing that with their own MOD. We have some large sectors under pressure at the moment as well, such as automotive, where we have 600,000 people employed and we have capacity. Every single one of those is a member of MakeUK, and all of them would love to be doing much more in defence to fill some of that capacity. It is there; it is a matter of bringing it together, sending clear demand signals about where the investment is needed and funding it properly.

Q27 Chair: The message from industry is clear: “Rather than backloading, let us have a clear demand signal right here and now, and let us get on with producing”.

Let me come to the second part of my question, which is around the additional £15 billion that has been provided to defence. Is that sufficient? Does it concern you that it is reliant on efficiency savings? We had this out in the Public Accounts Committee as well when I guested there. We have all done workshops. We have all done gold, silver and bronze and all the other flavours of it. It happens in other sectors as well. It is not limited to defence.

Julian’s point about Made In Europe is not to be underestimated here. We are in a very difficult place with the Industrial Accelerator Act at the moment. The more we go out on the TV saying, “Buy British”, the more we are not going to be part of the European system, which accounts for nearly half the exports of the country. We are in a really serious place at the moment.

I have just spent two days with my counterparts across Europe doing Made in Europe. We did it in Madrid for two days, which sounds lovely, but it was Commission—

Jesse Norman: It is brutal work.

The frustration is often about buying purely American platforms without any noble UK work happening here. That sort of thing is what we are trying to get at in terms of doing more in the UK.

Q61 Jesse Norman: The other question arises is that, when the guano starts flying, the whole sector, including Europe, is going to go into some lockdown. Q63 Fred Thomas: Post DIP, which we are living in now, we are expecting the defence finance investment strategy to come forward, and pretty soon we have this investment summit, which we are all looking forward to. Kevin Craven: Private finance and capital follows viable markets. At the moment, defence here in the UK does not necessarily represent a very attractive market for investment. Longer-term certainty, which we have discussed at length this morning, is a key component of that, but, equally, it is about the attractiveness of the MOD as a customer. Those investors do not want to pay for the poor performance of the MOD as a procurer, for example. That is unhelpful.

Risk sharing is probably one of those things that is important to understand. Again, investors are not attracted to high-risk ventures. By definition, defence feels like a high-risk investment in some instances. Defence infrastructure is much lower-risk. The recent announcement on the mobile docks for the Clyde was a really good example of something that could be funded perhaps by private finance as an example.

There are a number of things, but I would also draw attention to SME financing. We have talked about the difficulties they face in procurement, but they equally face a challenge in terms of funding for working capital, bonding, insurance and that type of thing. Again, in the same way as the MOD has a challenge in terms of the capacity to address 7,000 or 8,000 suppliers, the finance industry and the capital investment industry in the UK is also wrestling with finding viable SME pathways for investment.

Q64 Fred Thomas: Are some SMEs accessing financing from high-street banks in a slightly riskier way? The context of that question is that I am briefed by a number of high-street banks that they have what they would call venture debt teams. Kevin Craven: We are working with a number of the high-street banks, though not all of them by any stretch of the imagination, that are better at supporting SMEs on that difficult journey. As many sectors are, they are attracted to defence and the possibilities of participating because globally defence spending is up 25% or 30%. It simply has not manifested here in the UK at the same speed.

Fred Thomas: I am going to come to Stephen and then I will come back to Julian to ask about that tech comment.

Stephen Phipson: I have a few comments on the financing part. We do this not only in defence but across the rest of the manufacturing sector. We can get start-up financing, and the high-street banks have a role to play in that. We still see some debanking in the defence sector, which is another subject that we might want to get on to separately, even though the larger banks have done a much better job recently. Some of the smaller banks are still very challenged by that.

The Government have done quite well, with the British Business Bank and the National Wealth Fund, on financing those SMEs. What is a big problem in this country is series B funding. This is a big problem. For series A funding, getting that start-up going, it is fine. Once they get going, they want to build a factory or do the next round of financing.

The last five deals in the manufacturing sector this year all went to the Americans—all of them. No financial institution in the UK wants to pick them up. We have a serious problem. This is something that we talk to Treasury about every week at the moment. Why is it that patient capital is not available for series B in particular? Why is it that the National Wealth Fund is in series A and will not go on to series B and help the next stage of the development of those companies? That is a whole area that needs exploring.

We are an outlier, and the American companies are taking advantage of it. Some of our high-tech businesses, not specifically in defence, recently have gone to American investors because they will take the risk at series B and we will not. There is something around that and there is still something around debanking in the defence sector that we need to address.

Q65 Fred Thomas: Just to reassure you, this Committee has heard evidence about the funding journey from series B onwards and growth-stage capital. We are aware of that.

Stephen Phipson: It is across the sector. It is not just defence; it is everywhere.

Q66 Fred Thomas: Let me come back to my earlier question, Julian. We are talking about the DIP, and I moved us on to talk about defence investment strategy and the summit that is coming up. The SDR was written in 2024-25. The DIP was written in 2025-26. We are now in 2026 and moving to 2027. Some of the movement with AI is so recent that it is unreasonable to expect that it would have been accommodated and acknowledged in a document written two years ago. How current is the DIP? Julian David: The point about the DIP and the SDR is that they did talk a lot more about outcomes. One of the things that has bedevilled Government contracting in the past is the attempt to specify inputs. They try to say, “We want this”. They do not say, “We want this capacity” or, “We want to meet this requirement”. They say, “We need this technology or this approach”. That was welcome.

We would like them to go even further and shift to software being the core of everything and equipment being brought in to work around the software core, the secure compute and the secure comms infrastructure to go with it.

The DIP in itself does not need to keep pace with which AI model is the best, and it never will. We need to have this idea of a software-enabled approach, so that things can be modified and changed. We do not want to get into a situation where we have to cut a hole into the side of a ship being built because the computers in there need to be changed and they cannot be taken out. We need to shift to everything being designed around the idea that it will be upgraded and changed and that components will be swapped in and out of an architecture. If we do that, it will be fine.

I would like to pick up on the procurement point. We find all the time, both in defence tech and dual-use tech, that there is not an appetite for long-term investment in the UK. Companies in all parts of emerging tech find a much better conversation when they talk to US investors, particularly.

Secondly, there is applied AI, which is, “What can you do with it now?” I will ask colleagues on their perspective as well, but I do not see a lot of connection between the activities of SovAI and the necessity for national security and defence. There does not seem to be any connection.
