Chair: Harriet, can you outline the different ways that the law is being used to try to stop victims and survivors speaking out?

Harriet Wistrich: Thank you very much for inviting me. The Centre for Women’s Justice is a legal charity that aims to hold the state accountable around violence against women and girls. We are very focused on the criminal justice system, and that is our speciality, but because we work with victims of rape, sexual violence, domestic abuse and so on, it often comes to our attention or we get inquiries from women who are being threatened with different types of proceedings or are fearful of being threatened by different sorts of proceedings, which is making them fearful of speaking out.

I have forwarded a few documents. I do not know if anyone has had a chance to look at them, but one of them is a very detailed FAQ document that we have produced to assist women who come to us, or often their ISVAs or those who are supporting them, to answer the sorts of questions that may arise if they are thinking of speaking out or if they have spoken out and have been threatened with legal proceedings and so on.

We are not ourselves media specialists, but we recognise that this is a feature that is quite common, particularly around gender-based violence more generally. Defamation, of course, is probably the area that people think of most. We are also very aware of the use of NDAs in a variety of proceedings.

One big piece of work we have done over a number of years is in relation to police-perpetrated domestic abuse and the issues there, particularly those who want to speak out within policing, either because they have been a victim or because they are a whistleblower. They are being made to sign NDAs, or to feel that they have to sign an NDA, which prevents them from speaking out. Of course, we see NDAs in a range of other affairs.

Again, because we are criminal justice, we are not experts in family court proceedings, but we are aware that particularly manipulative abusers will often use the family courts in different ways, including sometimes to silence victims.

One person who contacted me recently, when they heard I was giving evidence, is Liv Nervo, who has sought to raise issues around reproductive coercion. She was in a relationship with a very wealthy man who sought to silence her in a number of ways, and ultimately the family court proved to be the most successful. She asked if I could mention that.

Finally, I will talk in slightly more detail about a very recent decision that we had from the UN CEDAW Committee around a case that I have been involved with for over 12 years, which is on behalf of David de Freitas, who sits behind me. He is the father of Eleanor de Freitas, who tragically took her own life just before she was subject to a prosecution for perverting the course of justice after she reported a rape against a wealthy man. When the police decided they were not going to investigate, this man brought a private prosecution against her. Eventually, the Crown Prosecution Service was asked to stop the prosecution as an abuse of process, but unfortunately it decided to continue the prosecution.

Now, we have raised a whole series of issues that I know I will not have time to go into detail about here, but they are set out in the CEDAW report. It is the first finding of the CEDAW Committee under the optional protocol to find that the UK has violated many of the discrimination provisions it is signed up to. I would urge Members of Parliament to read that.

What they recommend is that the state party should amend its legislation around private prosecutions brought by those whom somebody makes an allegation against in relation to sexual or gender-based violence. Private prosecutions, incidentally, were an issue the Centre for Women’s Justice raised during a previous inquiry by this Justice Committee. We brought this particular case to the attention of the Committee then and raised the same points. We really think it is timely. These private prosecutions are not very common, but it is very concerning that somebody who might wish to report a case could be at risk of a private prosecution. In this case, that turned into a tragic outcome.

The second part of the case concerns what happens if a private prosecution is taken over by the Crown Prosecution Service or the state. In those circumstances, because the case started as a private prosecution, by this point the person who made the allegation of rape, who would normally have a right to anonymity and to a whole series of protections as a victim of rape, immediately loses all those protections, as happened for Eleanor.

I should add that CEDAW does not make a comment about whether or not this was a true or false allegation. It was something that was never proven one way or the other, but none the less she could not face the public shame of the proceedings against her, and very sadly, as somebody also suffering from a mental health condition, she took her own life.

I would really welcome the Committee taking a look at that decision and the recommendations made by the CEDAW Committee.

Chair: Rupert, can I ask you the same question? I know you have given evidence to the Committee on SLAPPs before, but can you also set out whether there is really an agreed definition of SLAPPs in this context?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: In terms of the framework or how SLAPPs are brought, we have an expanding retinue of laws that can be used to sue over damaging allegations, the most obvious being defamation or libel and slander. Increasingly, there are other causes of action like misuse of private information being brought over damaging allegations of sexual violence, data protection or harassment.

SLAPPs are an American concept. We have one bit of legislation in the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act, which defines a SLAPP in a very narrow, specific way. Anti-SLAPP laws have been adopted by the vast majority of US states and some of the states in Canada. There is an EU anti-SLAPP directive. The essence of what it is trying to capture is when litigation is not being brought properly for the genuine exercise of legal rights where you have a proper claim, but to threaten, intimidate or harass a target to prevent freedom of expression. The fact of the litigation and the way it is being brought is chilling free speech, stopping an allegation from coming out.

Our laws are particularly susceptible to SLAPPs, despite having very little statutory protection at the moment, because, first of all, with media law, all these causes of action are generally litigated in the High Court. They take about two to three years to get to trial. I used to say that costs could run up to £1 million; these days they can easily run into the millions.

Then, if you lose, you are susceptible to the other side’s costs, and if you win, you do not get back all your costs. For example, The Guardian successfully defended allegations against the actor Noel Clarke last year on truth and public interest grounds. It spent £6 million defending that claim. It turned out to be a true story protected by the public interest defence. I am not au fait with what it got back, but even if Mr Clarke paid some of those costs, there is always a shortfall of 20% or 30%.

There is this huge price to publishing allegations. The heart of the issue is that we have a legal system that decides the truth and legitimacy of speech in this country, which is so expensive and complex. The procedure for achieving justice is so expensive that it does not achieve justice. It lets the person with the deepest pockets grind the other side down and win.

Chair: Thank you. We will now focus on victims and survivors.

Tessa Munt: Verity, recent events have highlighted how victims and survivors can be targeted by SLAPPs. Could you explain why victims and survivors are so particularly vulnerable to SLAPPs?

Verity Nevitt: Survivors are likely quite vulnerable to SLAPPs just due to how allegations are addressed societally. Stigma and disbelief are quite prevalent, and they are rooted in misogyny, which emboldens abusers to use the law in this way. A man’s reputation and future are often seen as more valuable in comparison with a survivor who has experienced abuse. That comes into play enormously when we see SLAPPs. Men feel that they can do this because they can say that the survivor was lying, and that kind of disbelief is already present.

A huge amount of it is also that the legal merit of the case does not always come into play. They know that they can exhaust a defendant emotionally and financially far before they will ever get to trial where they deploy a defence of truth and public interest. It becomes far more about whether the defendant can withstand the process.

A key thread that runs through why facing a SLAPP as a survivor feels so difficult is that an inherent aspect of sexual violence is that, when you experience rape or sexual assault, choice is taken from you. Being subject to a SLAPP replicates those dynamics in the sense that you have no choice but to be subject to it. Either you engage in proceedings or you are forced to settle due to costs. That also means these cases are quite attractive to someone who is coercively controlling.

SLAPPs allow a flipping of the script where the perpetrator is presented as a victim of wrongdoing, and then the victim-survivor is presented as the aggressor by being made a defendant. It is an egregious reversal of reality, and it is the ultimate form of gaslighting.

I faced a SLAPP from my abuser; it felt like the truth did not matter. I went into the case thinking that the truth would prevail. At the end of it, it just felt like it did not matter at all. It did not matter that what we were saying was the truth and that we should be able to speak about that. It just became a question of costs.

Our first hearing at the High Court was quite a difficult experience, as you can imagine. I was very worried. My sister was a defendant in the case with me. Our abuser had raped her and sexually assaulted me. We were constantly worried that he was going to come around the corner at that trial.

Psychologically, you are already in a very difficult position. We had not got legal representation until the night before, and we did not have any barristers instructed at that point. It is a very scary environment to be in.

Then, as soon as the hearing had finished, we were taken aside by the solicitor who said, “You need to find £100,000 if you want to make it to the next hearing”. Obviously, that is a completely impossible thing to do as a student in London, but that is what we were facing.

We hear from survivors at the Gemini Project all the time who are facing claims of this nature, but they are in a different position. When I faced my case, I was a student at university, living in a house share and renting. I had absolutely nothing to lose, so for me there almost was not a question of fighting it, because we knew what the truth was and it was just very instinctive for us to respond in that way, but we hear from women who are very advanced in their careers or have families or a home. It is not something they can do because they stand to lose everything.

That is difficult to face, but abusers know this. They know that either someone is never going to fight this claim or, “We will exhaust them before they can properly fight it at trial”. They are very attractive for abusers who do not want anyone knowing about their wrongdoing.

Tessa Munt: Can you talk to me a little bit about the impact on your and other victims’ health and wellbeing in general?

Verity Nevitt: Facing a SLAPP may be, in some ways, worse than the rape itself. You are questioned. You have to hand over everything. It is also very exposing having to detail your trauma online in order to raise costs via crowdfunding platforms.

One of the hardest things for me was probably that. In putting together our case, I had to come across very graphic details of my sister’s rape, which is something I will probably never recover from. I am still undergoing therapy to try to heal from those memories of reading that. It made me want to end my life. That is something we hear from a lot of survivors. Our research found that 47% of survivors who face these and had responded to the survey had suicidal ideation. I am not surprised.

I wonder if it would be helpful if I read out some survivors’ testimonials and if that would give a bit of a sense of the impact of this.

Tessa Munt: Yes, please do.

Verity Nevitt: One survivor says, “Throughout the case I was constantly reminded of my abuse when writing statements, liaising with my legal team, crowdfunding for legal fees and during court appearances. It extended and exacerbated the trauma I experienced when I was sexually assaulted. All I wanted to do was to move on and to heal, but I could not get away from it. It was an inescapable, unrelenting and pervasive intrusion into my life, and I had no choice in being subjected to it”.

Another survivor says, “The terror from the threat of legal proceedings was harder to deal with than the rape itself. It is a threat with no endpoint”.

Another survivor explained that she was having to sell her home after 25 years to pay for it, and she was about to sell her car to pay the next bill.

These cases are very all-consuming. When I was fighting my case, I was taking calls in the corridor at work and rushing to get documents finalised so that my sister, who was working at a bar that was very conveniently situated round the corner from the Royal Courts of Justice, could then go and file the documents on a break.

It is not just about money; it is also the time and mental energy that you have to put into these cases. A lot of the time, we were without representation, so we were doing a lot of that ourselves. It takes over your life. I was not able to work for a period. Obviously, that has a continuing effect in the sense that I was not paying into my pension at that time, so that will follow me for the rest of my life, almost.

I do not really know how to convey how pervasive it is, but it really does take over your life. There is not a waking minute where you are not thinking about it.

Tessa Munt: Looking at your work with survivors, what sense do you have of the scale of legal threats and claims being used following disclosures of sexual violence?

Verity Nevitt: It is almost impossible to get a sense of the scale because it is so hidden. The nature of these proceedings means that it is very hidden.

What surprised us is that we always expected these sorts of cases to come when someone has made a public disclosure, but these are also used pre-emptively to prevent disclosures or if someone has made a private disclosure. A survivor also got into contact with us when they faced a threat of civil legal proceedings after reporting to the police.

Do you mind repeating the question?

Tessa Munt: I was just wondering, based on your work with survivors, about the scale. By using those testimonies, you have probably answered that question. We have a sense of that, so thank you for your evidence.

Can I move to Jennifer? I saw you responding on screen. I wondered about how the law has a chilling effect on women speaking out about sexual violence. What are the key factors that make people reluctant to tell their stories?

Jennifer Robinson: First, thank you for inviting me. I just want to reiterate my support for the statements you have heard already, particularly from Verity in sharing her story, which is common in my experience of working with women who want to speak out.

In terms of my practice, I have written a book about this called “Silenced Women” because of the frustrations I felt as a practitioner with what I was seeing in my legal practice in the post-MeToo period, where you have women who are breaking the cultural silence to speak out about gender-based violence. We saw this avalanche of litigation and legal claims being brought against them, whether defamation claims or non-disclosure agreements.

The question I get from women a lot is, “How can he sue me? It is true”. My response to them is, “The point is that you need to be able to prove it in court”, and to be able to prove it in court is incredibly expensive.

We are seeing that the mere threat of a defamation letter, for most women, would put them at risk of bankruptcy. What does free speech mean if you cannot defend it? For many women I see in my practice, and in the research that we have done around the world, the mere threat of a letter is enough to cause self-censorship.

As Verity said, we do not really know the extent of the problem because so many of these legal threat letters are sent, and they are not public. There is no register of letters, so we simply do not know how many women are self-censoring because they cannot afford to defend themselves.

To give you a couple of examples of high-profile defamation cases in the UK, there is a case that went to the Supreme Court called Stocker v. Stocker. In that case, Nicola Stocker was able to defend herself in that claim only because lawyers were willing to do it on a “no win, no fee” basis. She told us for the purposes of our book and our research that she would not have been able to defend herself. She ultimately won, but it took seven years of litigation, where she could not speak about her experience and risked bankruptcy. Fortunately, she had lawyers who were willing to defend her.

Another high-profile case in the UK is Nina Cresswell’s case. She was sued for speaking out about her abuse in the context of the MeToo movement. She was self-represented for a year and was only able to defend herself after contacting the Good Law Project and fundraising to be able to support herself to defend that case. She won on a truth and public interest defence, but it was incredibly difficult for her.

It is the sheer cost risk. We have heard some of the cost risks, and we have seen cost estimates in these cases in the millions of pounds, which most women cannot afford and, indeed, a lot of journalists cannot afford. The chilling effect is much broader than simply women themselves who want to speak out; it is also having a chilling effect on journalists and their newspapers’ willingness to report their stories, which is not in the public interest. We cannot tackle violence against women if we cannot talk about it.

As we have seen from recent events in the United Kingdom with Russell Brand, journalists’ reporting often leads to other forms of criminal accountability, so there is a public interest element to ensuring women’s right to free speech. The chilling effect of these defamation claims, and indeed other forms of silencing, whether it is privacy injunctions, non-disclosure agreements or breach of confidence proceedings, is that it is just so expensive.

I also want to recognise that, for survivors who are sued in defamation or other proceedings, having made an allegation of violence, many women experience being sued by their perpetrator as a form of continued abuse. They have left a relationship. They are no longer in this relationship, but the person can pursue them with these legal proceedings, where they become the defendant and their financial security is at risk.

Litigation is incredibly stressful, particularly for women who are being asked to relive very traumatic experiences about their lives. Of course, there is the disclosure element of that, which is their text messages and medical records. It means that their former perpetrator has access to them, contact with them through lawyers and knows where they are going to be on particular dates.

In this particular context, we need to understand the way in which civil proceedings can be used as a form of continuation of abuse and how so many women experience it that way.

This is having a huge chilling effect. We simply do not know the extent of it. We know that it is affecting women, but it is also preventing reporting. For many journalists, they will not be able to get these stories across the line unless they are working for a media organisation with very deep pockets. Even those media organisations might make a decision not to report a story because of the cost risk, and their legal budget just cannot stretch to account for that potential risk.

This is a huge issue that we need to be grappling with, because we cannot grapple with violence against women if we cannot talk about it. The point we make in our book is that, in fact, we should be treating free speech and better protecting women’s free speech as an element of our prevention strategy and of accountability in our society, since violence against women is such a widespread problem.

We need to look at legislative safeguards. The anti-SLAPP legislation that we have in the United Kingdom does not cover this context.

Chair: That is a good point at which to bring Jon’s questions in.

Jon Pearce: Thank you, Jennifer, for joining us at such a late or early hour. Taking up your point in terms of how we then protect women’s and victims’ rights to freedom of speech, can we just talk about what the legislative framework is at the moment? The only legislation we have in respect of SLAPPs covers economic crime. Can you set out the legislation around that, but then start to look at what you think we could be doing in the wider context to protect victims?

Jennifer Robinson: At the moment in the United Kingdom, we only have a very narrow anti-SLAPP protection under the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act. That only applies in respect of information where the information in question relates to economic crime, and it is where it is disclosed for purposes related to the public interest in combating economic crime.

The point that I make when I talk about this around the world and back in the UK is this: why are we privileging the protection of public interest speech on economic crimes and not for crimes against women in the context of the crisis of violence against women that we have in society?

There is no reason why anti-SLAPP legislation could not be more broadly applied. If we are willing to apply it in that context, why would we not apply it in the context of violence against women? Why are we privileging the public interest in combating economic crimes over and above combating crimes against women?

The legislation is modelled on anti-SLAPP laws around the world. There are other problems with it, but the first point to make is that it does not cover this context and we need to look at legislation that will. We need to look at anti-SLAPP legislation that covers a broad range of public interest speech, not just that of economic crimes. Certainly, in this context of violence against women, the crisis of violence against women in this country absolutely warrants it.

If you would like to look more at the terms of the legislation and how it operates, I am happy to explain that too.

Jon Pearce: It would be helpful, Jennifer, if you could look at how the current restrictive approach to SLAPPs in terms of economic crime could be applied to assist victims and whether, in effect, you could lift and shift that legislation or whether it needs a different approach.

Jennifer Robinson: This was a very particular piece of legislation. There have been various proposals and private Members’ Bills put forward, supported by the Anti-SLAPP Coalition in the United Kingdom, which has been doing excellent work on this point, to introduce a piece of legislation that would more broadly protect public interest speech. Rather than being confined to public interest speech and publication in relation to economic crimes, it would be a broader protection to protect all forms of public interest speech.

A new piece of legislation could be modelled on legislation we have seen elsewhere. I have not seen the most recent private Member’s Bill that is being proposed. It is before Parliament; I am not sure it has been published yet.

Jon Pearce: It has not.

Jennifer Robinson: If we were to look at how the current economic crime provisions work, they have basically introduced an early strike-out application, where you can seek to strike out an application where it meets the definition of a SLAPP claim and the claimant cannot show that it is more likely than not that the claim would succeed at trial. A similar test could be put in place in respect of public interest speech more broadly, and public interest speech in relation to violence against women.

Jon Pearce: Rupert, can I bring you in at this point? What are the strengths and weaknesses of an approach based on providing courts with these additional powers to strike out claims defined as SLAPPs?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: I agree with everything Jennifer has said. We are now a major outlier among western democracies in not having any general anti-SLAPP law despite, as I mentioned earlier, our laws being more susceptible to legal bullying. There is a lot of research on that.

In terms of transposing the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act, there has been only one successful application since the Act came in, and that was a case that was also struck out as being an abuse of process, with summary judgment given in any event. So far, the anti-SLAPP mechanism that has been set up only got rid of something that was already protected against.

The key issue with the way the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act SLAPP provision works is that it requires the applicants to show that the SLAPPer subjectively intended to cause distress, expense or harassment outside the ordinary course of litigation.

There are two key issues with that. First of all, it works with a hopeless case, where they are behaving so badly you can get rid of it, but any sophisticated abuser with money will just file a witness statement saying, “I did not intend to cause any distress or harm. Litigation is a combative process. I am playing within the general rules”. You cannot cross-examine them on an application like that, so it is almost an impossible hurdle to meet: to prove that the SLAPPer subjectively was trying to SLAPP you.

Also, any SLAPP law that allows distress and harassment within the ordinary course of litigation is not going to do any good because the ordinary course of litigation is too much for victims of abuse. We need our SLAPP law to look at proportionality. It is when the head of the Wagner Group sues over one small tweet rather than newspaper articles accusing him of being head of the Wagner Group, targeting journalists and victims.

Changes need to be made. If we are bringing in a general anti-SLAPP law, we cannot just adopt the approach that the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act has done without it effectively achieving very little that is not protected already. It will end up just being a fig leaf. People will say, “There is an anti-SLAPP law. This case is not a SLAPP”.

Jon Pearce: Jennifer, I can see you nodding. Is there anything you wanted to add?

Jennifer Robinson: I agree with Rupert in terms of the test that is currently in the narrow anti-SLAPP Act that we have. The intent element makes it very difficult to prove. If you look at jurisdictions outside the UK that already have anti-SLAPP laws, you do not need to prove that. The elements are simply whether the challenged claim arises from protected speech. For example, speech about violence against women is speech in the public interest and would be protected free speech. If it is directed at speech in the public interest, it would bite.

Once that applies, the second element of the test puts the burden on the claimant to show whether they have a demonstrated probability of prevailing in the claim or whether the defendant would have no defences at all. That is the test that applies in the United States, for example.

Canada has an additional element to that test where you then have a weighing exercise. It is about whether the publication or statement relates to a matter of public interest; then the burden shifts to the claimant to show that the lawsuit is meritorious and the defendant has no defences; and then the third element would be to weigh up the harm associated with the expression as opposed to the public interest in protecting that expression. It is a balancing exercise that our courts are entirely used to. In the context of defamation claims, you are balancing free speech and the right to protect your reputation.

These tests do not have a subjective intent element. It is merely about protecting public interest speech. That is a much better approach to take than what we see in the current legislation.

Sir Ashley Fox: Rupert, can I come to you first, please? Recent cases have highlighted that injunctions can be used to silence victims and survivors. What are the particular issues with the use of injunctions in this context?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: There is a very old rule that interim injunctions are generally not available in defamation; that is from Bonnard v. Perryman. Increasingly, we are seeing ways around that: bring your case in harassment; bring your case in privacy law.

The reason for the general rule is that the courts recognise that, at an interlocutory stage, it is too risky if you allow somebody to rush off to court and get a judge to stop an allegation being out there. The principle is “publish and be damned”. Speech should only be restricted after a court has been able to decide the issue of whether the allegation is true or not at trial.

Injunctions are very problematic. We are seeing them creep back in in this sphere.

Sir Ashley Fox: You say it is under the guise of breach of privacy or harassment, as opposed to defamation?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: Exactly, yes. An example that is not in the context of sexual violence or violence against women is that I represented a series of clients who had had a very bad experience with a cosmetic surgery clinic that was having issues with its regulator and pushing people into cheap operations. Mere reviews on Trustpilot were leading to this cosmetic surgery clinic suing for harassment and getting injunctions in harassment from a county court family judge, which precluded any continued criticism of this cosmetic surgery clinic.

Sir Ashley Fox: That sounds like an abuse of process.

Rupert Cowper-Coles: It was. One case has been struck out; one is still going on three years later. It is an abuse of process, yes.

Sir Ashley Fox: How prevalent are injunctions as a means of silencing victims and survivors as opposed to other methods?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: Injunctions are a growing issue. Injunctive relief is available in privacy. Our Supreme Court has decided in the case of Bloomberg LP v. ZXC that, with the fact of a police investigation, the starting point is that it is private.

A lot of the issue with survivors is defamation. Often, it is the unjustified libel letter coming through. It is the threat of that letter saying, “If you do not apologise or give an undertaking to court and commit to never saying this allegation again, you will face legal proceedings”—legal proceedings that they cannot afford to fight.

Sir Ashley Fox: It is the mere threat of legal proceedings as opposed to the application of an injunction?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: That is more of an issue, in my practical experience.

Sir Ashley Fox: That is rather difficult to prevent, is it not?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: It is quite instructive that in intellectual property law, which is a sister area of law, a bit of legislation was brought in. It is called the Intellectual Property (Unjustified Threats) Act 2017. It was brought in to prevent unjustified threats of trademark infringement or patent infringement. It was brought in specifically because they knew that the biggest companies in the world could bully smaller competitors just with the cost of IP litigation. At around the same time, intellectual property courts had brought in a cheaper, effective and quicker court called the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court because they realised there was a public policy reason why, if IP litigation costs millions of pounds, the smallest businesses lose out and can be bullied.

In an area of law in which many would say the stakes are much lower, we have recognised that you need a quicker and more effective access to justice that is a little more affordable. You need to penalise people who make threats when they should not be doing so. Effectively, unjustified threats litigation means that, if you make a threat improperly, the person you have threatened has a cause of action against you for the stress and anxiety that it has caused. I can think of no good reason why patents are protected against unjustified threats, but libel threats over allegations of sexual violence are not.

Sir Ashley Fox: Thank you. That is helpful. Jennifer, do you have anything to add specifically on the issue of injunctions?

Jennifer Robinson: I agree with Rupert that, in my experience, it is more the defamation and threat letters, but we are seeing injunctions used. For example, we saw it used against the BBC to prevent the reporting on a woman’s allegations of sexual violence when there was an ongoing police investigation. Injunctions are used in a privacy context in that development of law around protecting someone’s privacy when they are the subject of a police investigation pre-charge.

It is worth looking at those sorts of regulations. Codes of practice have a role, but they are of limited value. Sarah Russell: In terms of litigation tactics, something that I have certainly seen is where constant correspondence is used by the other party to escalate someone’s legal costs to ensure that they cannot continue to afford the defence they have had. Do you think there is a way in which we could have an ethical rule for solicitors and barristers around proportionality of correspondence, or do you think that is unworkable and too demanding?

Harriet Wistrich: I do not know if it is an ethical rule or just a procedure. A court can, when they are looking at costs, ultimately say, “This was disproportionate and you are not entitled to claim those costs”, but that is after the event and after they have scared somebody off. It is a tactic that I have certainly experienced when against somebody who has a lot of money and can just throw things at you so that you just stop it. It is another tactic that is definitely used.

Rupert Cowper-Coles: Any procedural reform to try to get a quicker, more proportionate process, which penalises anything such as document dumping or anything like that, would be really sensible.

In the context of SLAPPs, you can talk to lawyers in different jurisdictions. In the Netherlands, a libel dispute costs about €10,000 from start to finish. An investigating judge looks into it, asks a few questions, and decides it. There might be issues with how thorough and fair that process is, but, on the spectrum that we have, we are at one absolute extreme, which prevents access to justice for 99%.

Sarah Russell: The public would be really shocked by what you have said about the starting legal assumption in defamation being that a woman who raises an allegation of rape is lying until proven otherwise in a civil court.

Rupert Cowper-Coles: America does not have the presumption of falsity. We have the presumption of falsity. Any allegation that you make that is damaging will be presumed false until proven otherwise.

Harriet Wistrich: In the case that I mentioned right at the beginning, where we were talking about a private or even a public prosecution for perverting the course of justice, you lose all ability to defend what we have built safeguards around in criminal proceedings, because we recognise the particular issues that are faced due to the victim blaming and misogynistic culture that we live in.

I would invite the Committee again to consider the conclusions of CEDAW, which has raised systemic discrimination against women and the fact that we need to look at some legislation. There are some very clear suggestions and recommendations that could be implemented around that area. There are very similar safeguards that one can put in place around defamation proceedings and other sorts of proceedings.

Chair: Lawyers are supposed to have a paramount duty to the court, which is supposed to override their duty to their clients. Is that being enforced?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: There is an overriding duty to the court. There is also a duty not to take unfair advantage. When the SRA has tried to take action over this, it has got nowhere. In fact, it has, in effect, ended up facing very huge costs. It is the development of case law and authority. I am not saying that the interpretation of the law is wrong, but the way in which that duty to the court and the duty not to take unfair advantage have been interpreted has not, to my knowledge, led to any censure of a single misleading letter before action, aggressive litigation tactic or anything of that sort since the SLAPP debate began.

I have one case where a claimant’s lawyer said, “We have decided to sue you because your itchy fingers might get carried away, and I cannot trust that you are going to keep quiet”. This was the cosmetic surgery case. There were threats of imprisonment when we were in a civil context. As I said, they were acting in a way that made their own barrister say, “The threat that you are making is potentially akin to blackmail”.

Again and again, when the SRA has taken it forward, lawyers armed with KCs have said that they are simply acting in their client’s best interests as a balance of duties, but they are entitled to vigorously defend their client’s interests.

Chair: There are concerns that the Solicitors Regulation Authority does not explicitly have the powers that it needs to investigate the conduct and correspondence of solicitors who are involved in SLAPPs. As a matter of principle, should Parliament legislate to ensure that the SRA has explicit powers, including access to privileged material without client consent?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: Jennifer, do you want to go on this? I am happy to speak.

Harriet Wistrich: It is about solicitors.

Rupert Cowper-Coles: That is a good point. There is a case that I will not speak about specifically, which is looking at this. Privilege is a fundamental right, but my own view is that solicitors need to know that their correspondence could be seen by a regulator. Without that at the back of your mind, bad conduct is going to flourish.

There is a question about whether the SRA has the statutory power to see privileged material. If it does not, that is something that the Law Commission and Parliament need to look at. If we are in a situation where lawyers’ conduct cannot be checked by anybody, and you can never go behind privilege, even in cases of the worst behaviour, poor conduct will not be caught, and there will be systemic breaches of the rules that will not come to light.

Chair: Someone needs to check their homework. If it is not the SRA, then who?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: Yes, exactly.

Jon Pearce: Rupert, you raised a point about the requirements around unfair advantage. It goes back to a theme that we have repeatedly come back to: this imbalance of power. If a solicitor is taking advantage of somebody who is not represented—for example, sending letters saying, “You have 24 hours in which to sign”, where there is no requirement for legal advice and no advice to them that they should get legal advice and support—what more should the SRA be doing? You are saying that it has no teeth at the moment. And what more should we be encouraging the SRA to do to give the necessary powers to stop that practice?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: It is a very good question. The SRA police very broadbrush rules: “Do not take unfair advantage. You have to balance acting in your client’s best interests with your duty to the court”. Without something more prescriptive, the SRA, having gone wrong several times over the last few years, is not going to take action.

I have had this discussion with claimant lawyers. There is a pre-action protocol that sets out the information you should send at the outset of a claim, and that is updated from time to time. As a starting point, writing a letter in accordance with the pre-action protocol, with which there should be no issue whatsoever, is the first step before you go down civil proceedings.

Writing a letter that confuses, has the potential to mislead, puts a very short deadline or threatens prison as a consequence of a civil action misrepresents the law in fundamental ways. It is a bit disappointing that that does not cross the threshold of taking unfair advantage at the moment.

One observation is that there is a bit of inequality in terms of the SRA’s resources against some of the people with whom they are dealing. If Parliament is looking to fix the problem, there needs to be more prescriptive regulation around any threat to do with public interest speech. If you are writing a letter that is going to stop what appears to be a very serious public interest allegation from coming to light, expecting strict compliance with the pre-action protocol and high standards of regulatory conduct would be the minimum. It would be nice if five aggressive letters that mislead and terrify a person, coming in short succession, were a thing of the past.

Warinder Juss: I appreciate that, if we are dealing with pre-action matters, a court is not going to be involved. Post court action, when a court is involved, how do you find the courts behaving in terms of penalising solicitors on costs, for example, when they have acted in an unsatisfactory manner? Are courts willing to penalise solicitors whose conduct is not what it should be?

Harriet Wistrich: It can happen. At that stage, if the conduct is seen as unreasonable, you can have not only a costs order against you, but a wasted costs order. There is that power within proceedings, but the issue is that it may be too late for many people.

Warinder Juss: Would it not be available during the pre-action protocol?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: No. Before you joined, I mentioned that, in IP disputes, making unjustified threats in trademark litigation potentially gives the person who you should not be threatening a claim against you for the commercial impact that they have had. There is statute in other areas of law that protect against unwarranted, improper threats that have a bad impact on someone’s commercial interests. We just have not legislated for that in an area where unjustified threats are a much bigger problem.

Sarah Russell: From what you said, Rupert, one of the rules of the SRA is that you “only make assertions or put forward statements, representations or submissions to the court or others which are properly arguable”. Are you saying that the SRA is unable to enforce that? If so, are you saying that, effectively, outcomes-based regulation, as we have in this country, has just stopped functioning completely?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: It is a big question for me to comment on the whole regulatory framework, but it has not worked with SLAPPs at all. It has been the best intentions of the SRA to pick problematic cases, such as someone acting in furtherance of a crime, but all of it has fallen flat on its face.

Again, it comes back to a case needing to be properly arguable, but there is a presumption of falsity, and the view that is taken is, “I do not need to warrant my client’s instructions. My client says that they did not do it. My client says that it is not a fraudulent scheme. My client says this. Even as the evidence mounts up and everybody else in the world believes that there is an issue, I do not need to warrant my client’s instructions”.

There is a fundamental point here about access to justice. To me, there is a slight difference between acting for a defendant who is being prosecuted by the state and taking their instructions, and somebody who is using you to stop allegations coming to light and to prevent the dissemination of something that impacts us all. If people do not find out that people are a danger, more people are hurt. If fraudulent schemes do not come to light, people lose money in them.

This impacts everyone, but at the moment there is a focus on access to justice: “I do not need to warrant my client’s instructions. I can vigorously represent my client’s best interests, even when I strongly suspect that they are lying, because I do not know that they are lying”.

Chair: What are the risks of further legislative action to combat SLAPPs hindering legitimate civil claims? There are article 8 rights to privacy and family life. Is there a risk that, in trying to ensure that we combat the abuse on one side, we would create difficulties on the other?

Rupert Cowper-Coles: It is a really important point, because, with every attempt at reform in this area, that is the concern: not throwing the baby out with the bathwater. My view is that most anti-SLAPP legislation is trying to weed out vexatious or officious litigation that is disproportionate and not achieving something legitimate.

I have seen a Court of Appeal judge say at a conference that, “If SLAPP legislation comes in, give us something straightforward and allow us to interpret it”. Sometimes that is the best way. At the moment, a judge who really does not like the smell of a case cannot do anything about it, because there is a presumption of falsity, and so, unless the defendant can show that it is true to the standard, they need to apply the law.

A false allegation of sexual violence or something such as that is incredibly serious and damaging, which is why this is so serious and so difficult to get right, but I hope that the evidence we have collectively given today has shown that, with the way that the system is set up, anyone without the means to afford something that is pretty much unaffordable often faces a real uphill challenge in defending it.

It is a valid concern. We cannot have a piece of legislation that completely disregards article 8 rights in terms of the right to reputation and to proper recourse when you are genuinely defamed, but we need a vehicle that, as in other jurisdictions, allows the judiciary to look at all the circumstances and to weed out cases where they determine that it is abusive or is a SLAPP, and where the public interest in publication outweighs any potential infringement of article 8 rights.
