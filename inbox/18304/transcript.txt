Examination of witness

Rebecca Benneyworth.

The Chair: Welcome back to the Finance Bill Sub-Committee, where we have before us our second witness of the morning, Rebecca Benneyworth, who is a member of the Administrative Burdens Advisory Board. It sounds very impressive; you can tell us what the status of the body is. I will kick off and ask the first question after you have introduced yourself and then members can come in as they please.

Rebecca Benneyworth: I am a chartered accountant. I run a very small practice in Gloucestershire, serving very small businesses, and we look after their payroll as well as their other tax and accounting affairs. I am a member of the Administrative Burdens Advisory Board. That board is made up of a wide variety of people with business experience. We are all volunteers, and we act as a critical friend to HMRC. We look very closely at the implementation of policy and we make suggestions about potential simplification. It has become more important since the abolition of the Office of Tax Simplification, and we work closely with HMRC. We report directly to the Minister, we produce an annual report, and we do a survey of businesses about what they are finding difficult with the tax system. That is a bit about our board.

The Chair: That is very helpful. We noted that, last year in your annual report, you expressed “dismay” about how HMRC was approaching the development of the benefits in kind measure that we are looking at. Could you tell us why you are concerned and whether you think any of the issues you raised are near a solution?

Rebecca Benneyworth: We are very supportive of the policy objective behind this proposal. I remember being at a meeting of our board well over 10 years ago, when we were pushing quite hard for this to be introduced. As a result of that, HMRC introduced voluntary payrolling of benefits in kind in 2016. That means that, after the end of the tax year, somebody—for a small business, probably an external expert—produces a report for each employee who has received a benefit in kind. Alternatively, this is included in the payroll run every month and the employees are taxed as they go along. The reality is they pay some tax as they go along because HMRC estimates what the benefits will be and puts that in their notice of coding. They are paying sort of in real time but this would be more accurate; it would give more transparency; and we believed it would be an administrative simplification for employers.

So why were we dismayed when this came forward? We have had voluntary payrolling, as I have said, for about 10 years and certainly those of us on the board with direct experience in this area felt it would be the precursor to the mandatory scheme. In our practice, we encouraged our clients to go on to voluntary payrolling in 2025 and 2026 to prepare for this because we said then they will be used to the information flows, it will all be set up, and it will be a really good way to prepare for this. Unfortunately, mandatory payrolling looks nothing like voluntary payrolling. If HMRC had been able to engage with stakeholders in developing what it was going to do—

The Chair: Can you expand on that point, where you say that voluntary looks very different from mandatory?

Rebecca Benneyworth: When the schema, or the specification, of what data would be needed was released, that was the moment when our chins collectively hit the table because the volume of data and the detail that HMRC was asking for was way beyond anything that we do in voluntary payrolling.

In voluntary payrolling, if someone has a company car and the benefit in kind is £2,400, we include £200 a month in their pay as if they had had the money. They pay tax on it and all is done. The only slight flaw in voluntary payrolling is that there is still some national insurance to account for after the end of the tax year, which in future would be accounted in each month as you go along. It would all be smooth and simple.

I am not completely clear why but the data requirements and the way in which things were to be laid out was, in my view, quite extraordinary. I have a suspicion that it is to aid compliance checks rather than to work out the benefit in kind. Certainly, a lot of detail is being asked for. This is detail that, if you are unfortunate enough to pay your employees weekly, you might have to report on a weekly basis to HMRC. Both the Administrative Burdens Advisory Board and those representing the software houses that would have to build the software to allow us to do this were extremely concerned about that development and, to be fair to HMRC, those concerns were, shall we say, eventually taken on board and has resulted in our two-speed implementation. We are going to implement, from April 2027, cars, vans, fuel, and medical benefits that are, first, the most common and, secondly, probably the simplest to implement. The remaining benefits, apart from beneficial loans and accommodation, will come in in 2028. That is quite reassuring but what we were disappointed by was that the opportunity to engage with people who were going to do this and find out the simplest and most practical way to implement it had been lost.

Lord Leigh of Hurley: I am interested in understanding how disruptive and resource intensive this mandatory payrolling will be. You are in a good position to comment because—I did not realise—you run a payrolling service. In practice, do SME businesses give this work to payrolling businesses such as yours, so really it is not much of an impact for them because it is all done by a third party? What about people who do not want to use an agent and want to do it themselves? How is it going to impact them?

Rebecca Benneyworth: The impacts are quite considerable, even if companies are using an outside agent. If we take the position with form P11D, which is the annual reporting system, normally an SME, even if it usually runs payroll in-house, would usually use a tax adviser because the sorts of businesses I deal with would not normally have the tax expertise in-house to calculate the annual value of the benefits in kind. Some are straightforward and some are quite complicated. That is the as is, if you like.

In future, under mandatory payrolling, those businesses will have to establish the benefits in kind on a regular basis. Let us say we are running payroll monthly: they would have to gather the information in-house and pass it on to an external adviser or include it in the payroll if they are running payroll internally. It depends on the nature of the data they receive. You might be providing a medical benefit and you have engaged an insurance company to provide that for your employees, so you would tend to know how much was going through the bank each month to cover the employees but what you would have to do is then break that down on an employee-by-employee basis. Those premiums would change if an employee decided they wanted members of their family included on the benefit, so you would have to be alive to those changes on a monthly basis in order to report correctly. You would then have to, if necessary, do some checks to make sure that you had accurate data and then, depending on whether you were going to payroll this yourself or you were going to ask an agent to do it, you would pass that information over to the agent.

One of the issues with running payroll as an external agent is timeliness. We often run very close to the line in terms of getting data from our clients to say, “This person has been off sick or this person has done overtime”. Sometimes we are chasing up the day before a company wants to make payment to its staff to say, “Come on, we need this data”. Having more data and more complexity is quite a challenge.

I note from the updated tax information and impact note that HMRC’s belief is that there will be ongoing significant cost savings, but I am afraid that for employers that does not ring true to me because of the amount of work they will have to do. If it is a company car provided in-house, when somebody changes their car, you have to establish the list price of the vehicle and look up in the tables what the rate of tax should be. Is that being done in-house? Does the company, the SME, have the expertise to do that? If it wants to run the payroll in-house, will it have to refer to the agent to calculate the benefit in kind to understand what figure to include? Some of this will be overcome in the passage of time but, even where it is being done by an external agent, there are definitely ongoing costs for all employers.

Lord Leigh of Hurley: Can I push back a little on that? I use a payroll agency and people do not change their car that regularly, not more than once a year maximum, and the average is three years. The premiums to insurance companies are just a number that is provided. I have just pushed back a bit. For people who use a payroll agency, is it really that much extra work?

Rebecca Benneyworth: It will depend on how many employees you have. Let us take one of my clients, a two-director company. During the year they decided they would start a private medical insurance scheme. We have a premium, it needs splitting between the two people, but that is not too difficult at the outset. That premium will change annually, probably halfway through the tax year because that is how things happen. You need to remember that these people are running a business. You need to remember, “Wait a minute, I have seen on the bank that that has changed. Now I need to quickly check whether they have both gone up by the same amount or not and then pass on that information”.

Our experience of voluntary payrolling is that it gets forgotten and we get to the end of the year, as we did last March, and suddenly all sorts of things come out of the woodwork. “I forgot to tell you, we gave so-and-so a van”. “When was that?” “It was in January. Oh, sorry, we forgot”. They will get used to it, they will have to get used to it, but if you have 50, 60 or 200 employees on BUPA—there are other providers—you have to then split that down by employee. With 200 employees, it is bound to be a member of staff; children arrive, they have to up their premium, so it is an ongoing obligation.

Baroness Wheatcroft: As you pointed out in your initial statement, the Office of Tax Simplification is no longer around. This does not amount to simplification. Why do you think HMRC is asking for so much information? You suggested that it might be because of compliance. Could you elaborate on that?

Rebecca Benneyworth: In order to tax benefits through the payroll, all you really need is what the cash equivalent is this month. That might be made up of three or four benefits in kind. You might need somebody who knows about tax to calculate it, but it is just one number. If all we were being obliged to do was to include one number in the payroll, the Administrative Burdens Advisory Board would have said, “This is what we were hoping for. It is a real simplification and it gives employees the benefit of knowing that they are paying their tax as they go along and are not going to get a bill after the end of the year”. We would have been absolutely behind it.

I admit I have not yet seen the original specification, and we do not have secondary legislation yet because the Finance Bill has to go through first. I have not seen what the data requirements precisely are going to be. The original specification required each type of benefit to be broken down, so you would have to provide information about a car, fuel, medical benefits, this or that, all separately both in the month and cumulatively each month or each pay period. I can speculate only that this is to help those who want to check it. I cannot see any other reason for it. We have not probed HMRC on it because that is not something we tend to do. We will more generally push back and say, “Come on, tell us why you need this information because otherwise you should not be asking for it”.

Baroness Wheatcroft: Allied to that it seems, from the evidence we have had so far, that HMRC routinely assumes that everybody, smaller companies included, has professional advice. Do you think that that mindset is there and is it false?

Rebecca Benneyworth: I do not think HMRC actively assumes that everybody has access to professional advice. I do not think that is the case but because the people designing this are able, understand the tax system, and understand how the benefits are calculated, they forget that a small business—let us say an electrician with four staff, all of whom have a van—would not understand that. In a business larger than my clients, there is also an assumption that because the information exists within a business the people who run payroll have access to it. In a large organisation that information might be in HR, or it might be in a completely separate department that organises benefits in kind on a company-wide basis, and that information has to then flow through the business to whoever is going to use it. So there are two flawed assumptions there.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: What is the impact of this on small businesses going to be? I suspect small businesses are pretty much totally unaware that this is going to happen. When they realise what is being asked of them, how are they going to react?

Rebecca Benneyworth: At the moment there is a feeling in small businesses of being a bit under siege with various administrative requirements being placed on them. My own experience, in my practice and with my clients, is that there is more and more administrative work being offshored out into business and away from government departments. It is inevitable that small businesses will push back. My clients have very small businesses. One of my electricians’ main worry day-to-day is whether he can find another qualified electrician. That occupies a great deal of his time. Another similar-sized client just cannot find another chap to go in the workshop to repair cars. My suggestion is that he ought to train an apprentice and I could work with him to do that.

We will try, as an adviser, to shelter our clients from this but it is inevitable that we will be pushing them to keep on top of information and to keep in touch with us on a monthly basis. A lot of these clients do not want to speak to their accountant every month. They email their hours in to my payroll lady but it is going to be a little more—I am going to be pushing and probing them to get that information. They are not going to be very happy but we will try to support them with it.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: Is HMRC doing this for its own convenience?

Rebecca Benneyworth: Let me go back to our original ask in about 2014. The Administrative Burdens Advisory Board could see the benefits of doing this for HMRC, for the Exchequer, for employers and for employees, because the P11D process, after the end of the year, is a bit of a pain. If we could implement something simple in delivery, everybody would win. Certainly, that is why we lobbied for it. It came in as a voluntary. I wish voluntary uptake had been better and then perhaps we would not have mandated it. It is this idea that we have a voluntary system, it is actually working, we are now going to make that compulsory, and we are going to change it. We are going to do it completely differently and we are going to require a lot more information. That was our dismay and is still our concern; we are not dismayed now but we are concerned.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: Is it part of HMRC’s hidden agenda to try to persuade companies not to give benefits in kind?

Rebecca Benneyworth: No, I do not think so.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: You do not think it is part of that, to try to make it so difficult that small companies cease to give—for instance, your electrician gives a van to his people but only on the strict understanding that it is for the use of the business not personal use.

Rebecca Benneyworth: I cannot see why HMRC would have such an agenda. It does not make sense. It does not make sense to the Exchequer because if you do not give him a van you will have to pay him a mileage allowance, which would be tax-free, and tell him to buy his own van. It would not make sense in terms of tax take. I cannot honestly see that as an agenda.

HMRC’s agenda rightly should be everyone pays the right tax, ideally at the time they benefit from the income, whether that income is a payment of wages or the provision of an asset for private use, or whatever. That is the right agenda, and ABAB would support that.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: It is really an extension of the same problem. There are benefits that it is going to bring into this system later on because they are more complicated to calculate, particularly accommodation, overseas allowances and all the rest of those things that are extremely difficult to calculate on a monthly basis. Are we going to end up with two systems? The same company is going to have to do monthly payroll benefits and a P11D at the end because some things cannot be calculated on a monthly basis?

Rebecca Benneyworth: There is a risk. It is not just a risk; yes, that will happen. My experience of the provision of, for example, living accommodation is that it is quite unusual, and certainly smaller businesses do not do it unless it is job-related accommodation, which would be tax-free. For example, a polo chain that has a groom who needs to live in so they are there if the horses are taken ill during the night, that would not be taxable. It might be quite a small business. That would not be anything that it would have to worry about. Other businesses might well provide these benefits that are very difficult to calculate. I do not think it is going to be a vast number, but you are right. Even in 2027-28, we have cars, vans, fuel, and medical in monthly reporting and anything else that you have—for example, gymnasium membership—is on a P11D. However, I note from the guidance issued about 10 days ago that HMRC is encouraging employers to voluntarily payroll those other things in advance. Then it is two systems, is it not? If we have a system with data requirement that looks like that, and we have voluntary payrolling that looks like that, you are still running two systems. You cannot just shove everything through the same sausage machine. Yes, I agree.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: For a small company to be running two systems—

Rebecca Benneyworth: It is more admin, not less.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: It is not going to happen, is it?

Rebecca Benneyworth: No.

Baroness Bi: The purpose of this measure is to digitally transform tax compliance and yet we have heard quite a lot of commentary that the RTI system is going to struggle with the new data requirements that you think are excessive. Do you think there is a risk that digital transformation is getting ahead of the existing infrastructure? How do you think we can address that?

Rebecca Benneyworth: Clearly, policy, HMRC back-end systems, and the third bit of the triangle that is external software all run at different speeds, clearly. The likelihood that they will all coincide at the same time is a bit remote.

Obviously, HMRC struggles with legacy systems. It is trying to update and become more digital. RTI is a relatively modern HMRC system, having been around for about a decade now. I am not particularly a fan. We have some issues with RTI; they crop up. I worry that the demand for the level of data that HMRC has put in place, and we do not know what the final outcome on that is, will overburden the RTI system.

In addition, I would expect that HMRC is working with software developers to make sure that they know what they have to build but we are less than five months out. We have some new guidance but software developers have rather a lot on if they are also providing Making Tax Digital software. They have been building fast on that. We got a deferral; it was going to happen in 2026. The deferral was good. It has allowed HMRC to take on board some of our and the software sector’s concerns. The proof of the pudding will turn out some time next year or maybe after. What the Administrative Burdens Advisory Board would very strongly recommend is that HMRC looks at the costs of this and the implementation, maybe one or two years after it has been brought in, and reflects on whether it went about it in the right way. Has it produced the benefits that everybody hoped it would? Did it overestimate the cost savings? Did it underestimate the additional costs that people would have? It should do a good post-implementation review.

Baroness Bi: I can see the benefit of that, but I am worried about the commentary we have heard; for example, from Andy Scott, who said that the RTI system at the moment has “reconciliation differences to the tune of millions of pounds. That was automatically being referred to debt management”. The system is 13 years old and it is described as having, “a long-running subsistence issue”. If it is not coping with what is going through it at the moment—and it will suddenly have a lot of extra data coming through—my worry is around those reconciliation differences increasing and more people being put into debt management when they are doing the right thing. Is that something that worries you?

Rebecca Benneyworth: Yes, that is a valid concern, certainly among professionals who deal with payroll on a regular basis. We all shake our heads and get frustrated with the reconciliation differences in RTI. I think those are being addressed now. It has taken a while to break through that but those are being addressed. On some things that we come across, we just scratch our heads and say, “How on earth has that happened?” It is down to data that generally we submit being received as a different number, which is astonishing. If HMRC can work really hard to get to the bottom of it that would be great. Does the inclusion of benefits in kind through RTI make it more likely? With more data going through, it must be more likely. Separate from payrolling benefits, everyone needs to be pushing and encouraging HMRC to get to the bottom of these data issues with RTI. Yes, I would agree with your comments.

The Chair: Can I move the committee on to the correction of errors package of proposals? Could you outline to us in general what your view of these measures is and what is the motivation for them? Is it HMRC’s view that large numbers of people are not paying the right tax or paying less tax than they should, particularly in the small business sector? How do you think this will work? Will it be beneficial or not?

Rebecca Benneyworth: HMRC’s tax gap publication, which is updated every year, attributes a very significant part of the tax gap to the small business sector. Somewhere around two-thirds of the tax gap is reckoned by HMRC to be in the small business sector. It is very difficult for me, as an adviser to small businesses, to reconcile that with my experience of small businesses.

To be fair, if somebody appoints me as their adviser, we will have a letter of engagement that says, “You have to do everything right. You have to tell me the truth. Everything will be done honestly and above board; otherwise, I am not going to act for you”. My experience is probably angled towards the more compliant end of the small business market. Generally, small businesses want to pay the right amount of tax at the right time but sometimes they get confused. If they do not have an adviser, I do not know how they manage, to be frank, to get anywhere near the correct tax figure. Even if they have an adviser, it is realising that something has happened that is going to change your tax and therefore telling your adviser about it. That is actually quite a difficult area.

That sets out the backdrop for this measure. On the whole, this measure will help because, at the moment, HMRC’s approach to small business compliance would generally be to engage in a tax inquiry or a compliance check, ask for lots of information, pore over it, write to you, you write back, it writes back again, ask more questions, and so it goes on. If there is an obvious error, it seems to me logical that HMRC would contact the taxpayer or their agent and say, “This doesn’t look right. Would you look at it and correct it? We’re going to place an obligation on you now to correct it or come back with a valid explanation as to why it is wrong”.

If used properly alongside existing compliance powers, it could save HMRC tax administration time and money and small businesses’ time and money. The premise of the notice to correct is that if HMRC believes that there is an error in a document it will notify the taxpayer and the taxpayer has a stated period of time, it is not completely clear what that would be—the Institute of Chartered Accountants recommended 60 days be given—but let us say 30 days for correction. The taxpayer corrects and there is no penalty even if it was a careless inaccuracy. If they do not correct within the time, it is deemed to be a deliberate inaccuracy, which is fair enough. If you have been told there is a problem and you do nothing about it, surely you are prepared to let that problem lie, as it were, on your tax record. Generally, I feel quite positive about this measure.

Lord Newby: Taking up your point, previous witnesses have said that most small businesses do not set out to mislead HMRC on tax; they just want it sorted and, in one sense, not need to worry about it. One obvious reason for a high number of errors is the complexity of the system. I have two questions. First, from your view, do you think that HMRC could and should be doing more to explain how the system operates and how businesses should best fulfil their obligations? Secondly, and you referred to this in respect of the previous measure, do you think HMRC should be doing more to simplify things? You mentioned that the Office of Tax Simplification has been disbanded; do you think that should be restored?

Rebecca Benneyworth: The root of the problem with small businesses making mistakes about tax is twofold. Record-keeping errors contribute to it. Businesses that do not keep on top of their accounting records and roughly bung it all together in a carrier bag and take it to their accountant is always going to produce errors. By the time you have done that, you have forgotten what it was all about and cannot remember half of it. The accountant cannot make head or tail of it and it is a right mess. I would say that, outside small limited companies, the Making Tax Digital programme will improve that. I have no doubt about that. Record-keeping, and small company record-keeping, might be an area that is problematical.

Simplification is certainly something I feel very strongly about as a passionate believer in helping small businesses get their tax right. It is a really difficult area because there are trade-offs. I have been involved in panels with HMRC. I will use an example from this morning. I arrived at Kemble station. My train was 20 minutes late so I stood in the freezing cold and thought, shall I get a cup of coffee? If I had bought a cup of coffee at Kemble station, it would not be tax deductible. If on my arrival at Paddington I had bought a cup of coffee, it would be tax deductible. I remember having a conversation with another adviser and we said there are about seven different ways to view a cup of coffee within the tax system. That is not legislation; you cannot really simplify that. The only way you could do it is to say, “Everyone can have £2 a day for a cup of coffee, but no more”. Then you have an Exchequer trade-off. It is not as simple as changing legislation to make it easier, and it is something that has been embedded for a long time in our tax system. After 40 years in practice, I am still a bit defeated as to how to take some of this grit out of the system.

Back to the question, more and clearer guidance is the answer. HMRC is getting much better at guidance. There is a great tool called Tax Confident, which I did a bit of work on with HMRC. I am a tax educator as well as a practitioner. Those types of tools with a variety of presentation—a bit of video, links into manuals, flowcharts, and that sort of thing—HMRC is starting to do a lot more of that; it is a key thing to address some problems. Certainly, the Administrative Burdens Advisory Board is 100% behind these sorts of programmes.

Lord Leigh of Hurley: The background to that was that HM inspectors of taxes used to have to buy their own coffee. Therefore, they hated the idea of taxpayers having any tax-deductible coffee. My question is really to understand from you what HMRC can do. Does it understand the impact on small businesses? What more can it do to help small businesses?

Rebecca Benneyworth: HMRC is a very large organisation. There are bits of HMRC, particularly parts of the department that the Administrative Burdens Advisory Board works with quite closely, that have a good understanding of where small businesses are and what support they need. I do not think that that understanding is necessarily in the compliance guys. Might I argue that there ought to be a little more understanding in the chaps who are checking? Yes, but they have a job to do. They are probably measured on how much they generate out of an inquiry. I do not know whether or not they are, but they have a job to do.

What else can HMRC do? It should continue this programme of developing topic-specific and situation-specific guidance. “I am just starting up my business, what is it I need to know?” “I am just taking on my first employee, what is it I need to know?”

One of the problems I experience as a technical person is to look at some guidance and think it is really good but it does not tell the whole story. In the desire to simplify, bits will get left out. It is again using people such as me—I do loads and loads of voluntary work with HMRC—as tax educators to say, “We could hint at some other things they might need to consider”. More of what it is doing is probably the right answer. As I have illustrated with my coffee example, actually simplifying it is really difficult. Give people a pathway through with decision charts, diagrams and real-life examples—not examples that I look at and say, “That is a statement of the blinking obvious”. Give examples with a little difficulty—how would you resolve that?

Baroness Wheatcroft: Do you think that HMRC understands the meaning of “reckless”, and when a statement becomes reckless rather than careless? Do you think ordinary taxpayers will understand quite how important that distinction is going to be?

Rebecca Benneyworth: I do not think so; this is a bit of a new concept. It is a new but an old concept. We moved away from that type of idea in the Finance Act 2007 to “a mistake despite reasonable care”, “careless”, “deliberate” and “deliberate and concealed”. We deliberately moved away to something that was a little more objective; “reckless” is a subjective call. Does HMRC understand what “reckless” means? Some people do. Whether that will flow down into the individual and small business compliance department I am not completely clear.

Baroness Wheatcroft: If it is subjective, how can it?

Rebecca Benneyworth: It is very difficult. If you are taking on, as we know HMRC is, another 5,000 staff, and that judgment call on reckless is something that comes with experience, there are going to be at least 5,500 staff who do not have that experience. HMRC is going to have to look really hard at its training and at scrutiny by another level. Obviously, people such as me will be making representations on behalf of their clients. I hope none of my clients is reckless because I will have very serious words with them.

I would never advocate that every business has to have a paid professional adviser; it is not sustainable for businesses and there are not enough tax advisers either. Those tax advisers that there are can play their part in this. Actually, the tax and accountancy bodies have a role to play as well. It is going to be about that communication. Do I really want to be writing to my client saying, “You could go to jail?” Not really.

Baroness Wheatcroft: To what extent do you think this is all part of a government move? We have heard yet again in the Budget that it is terribly important that they make the books balance, or at least look a little better, by closing the tax gap. Are they going to go gung-ho and squeeze every penny out of people in order to close the tax gap that may or may not exist?

Rebecca Benneyworth: Is the “reckless” measure part of closing the tax gap? I guess it is. Will HMRC raise a lot of revenue as a result of it? No, I do not think so. It will act pour encourager les autres, if you will excuse my awful French accent, and you will get a few cases that are in the press. What is the difference between “deliberate” and “reckless”? I cannot say I know at the moment; I would have to have a very long think about that.

HMRC is going to focus massively on the small business sector because of its tax gap projections. I will say it again: it does not quite feel right. I cannot believe that many businesses are that bad. I do not have the experience HMRC has, and we will never know because you cannot measure what you do not have.

The Chair: No one knows how large the non-respectable sector of business is.

Rebecca Benneyworth: No. The move away from cash will make quite a difference here. People are not paying in cash; people do not carry cash. My taxi had a notice saying, “I prefer cash”. I thought, I bet you do, but I still paid by card. That will contribute to it. It is a complex question.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: The tax gap has been queried, not least by the House of Commons Library, as fictitious. Nobody knows where it comes from. HMRC does not explain in detail how it arrives at those numbers. It is politically driving changes to the tax legislation in a way that could be justified only if the tax gap could be justified itself. Do you think HMRC is open to having external people look at what the tax gap really is and how it is calculated?

Rebecca Benneyworth: I do. It would have to be handled very carefully. In the publication, there is actually quite a bit of information about how HMRC goes about calculating it. There are two ways: what it calls bottom-up and top-down. It uses a combination of those on different taxes. One of the reasons why the small business tax cap might be overstated is that some of it is from outcomes of inquiries. You will carry out a compliance check on a small business and you will record how much extra tax you have at the end of it compared with how much the business declared. You will then scale that up across the small business community. I do not know what the sample size is. If the sample size is too small, you risk an error in either direction. I would not say that 60% odd is understating it. My suspicion is, if there is error in there, it is overstated.

Some of that is quite challenging to me where, in a compliance check, you might get to a position where it is going to cost you another £1,000 to carry on arguing about something that is 200 quid’s worth of tax. You might as well just say, “Pay the man, put it to bed, and let us get it behind us”. Obviously, that will distort outcomes. It might be that that tax is not properly due but you have not been able to convince a compliance officer, you have not been able to produce enough evidence to satisfy them, or you have decided that that is it and you will pay. Obviously, that would overstate understatements of tax.

I would expect HMRC to develop policy; it is one of its strategic objectives. I would expect JP Marks to drive the tax gap figures very hard to show that HMRC is doing its job. What is, as you have said, a bit more difficult is if those figures are not right, it may be looking in the wrong place. I do not think it is looking in entirely the wrong place; probably a fair chunk of the tax gap is in the small business sector. Is it 60%? It does not feel like it. Might it be 30%? It is still worth looking at.

One of the problems in getting external scrutiny on it, such as the National Audit Office being able to go in, is that you would need some expertise. HMRC would also have to reveal some quite sensitive information about how it carries out compliance work and how it selects cases for a compliance check. It is quite difficult to handle. The NAO would probably be the only body that could get involved in that. Even so, is it going to see individual taxpayer data in looking at that? It is quite a difficult one.

The Chair: To what extent do you think the problem of companies not paying tax is a question not of deliberate avoidance but of a simple lack of knowledge? What could the Government do to improve awareness and knowledge of and education on the tax system?

Rebecca Benneyworth: I have talked a little about HMRC’s product called Tax Confident. That is an excellent product; it is aimed at the right level of knowledge, so I would want more of the same. I would be more than happy to help develop some more content on that. Should the Government be mandating things such as record keeping for small companies? A fair bit of that small business tax gap is in the company sector. There is a belief at HMRC, and I am not going to say it is entirely misplaced, that one of the big areas of difficulty is in a proprietary company where you have an owner, who is also a director, who does not really understand the difference between their money and the company’s money because the company is them. That can give rise to tax liabilities that the director would have no idea about. If that small company has a professional adviser, the professional adviser will help them keep it on the level. I would say a properly qualified professional adviser because HMRC has some data on people who help small companies with their tax who maybe do not have sufficient knowledge and experience to be able to do that, because it is an unregulated sector. Tax adviser is not a protected term. Accountant is not a protected term. You can fail at being a butcher and become an accountant tomorrow if you want to pay for a brass plate. That comes into it as well; that is problematical.

The Chair: Does the committee have any further questions?

Lord Newby: Do you think there should be a de minimis level below which HMRC should not pursue somebody for a mistake in their tax?

Rebecca Benneyworth: I do but you have a real dilemma there because if you are paid by PAYE, every penny you receive is taxed at source and there is no possibility you could have the odd 20 quid here or there and not pay tax on it. While pragmatically and practically it makes sense to say, “This notice to correct, if the amount of tax at stake is less than £30, £50, or £100, we will not worry about it”. That creates an inherent unfairness with people who do not have the luxury of a tax-free cup of coffee or whatever it is. While I can see that practically and pragmatically it makes sense, both for HMRC’s costs and the business’s costs, particularly if it has an adviser, I am a bit worried about the inherent unfairness that that might create.

Lord Newby: It would depend on the level, would it not?

Rebecca Benneyworth: Yes.
