Examination of witnesses

Dr Claire Melamed and Professor Robert Trager.

The Chair: Dr Melamed and Professor Trager, you are both very welcome. The committee is in its fifth session looking at multilateral aspects of artificial intelligence. Therefore, we very much look forward to your evidence as well. I have to say in advance that the session is being broadcast live on parliament.tv, and there is an audience out there. You will get a transcript of what you say, and any technical changes that you might make should be referred to us. Members of the committee should declare any interests before they ask a question. Just for the record, I declare that I am a senior counsellor with the Cohen Group in Washington DC.

I will ask the first question and perhaps the professor can answer it first. Maybe both of you could introduce yourselves and your background to the committee before you answer the question. Clearly, the whole issue of artificial intelligence is preoccupying the public space and inevitably the United Nations as well. There are people who say that the United Nations is simply a talking shop on this as well as other issues. Do you think that that characterisation is right and whether it is accurate or not? What has the United Nations been able to do so far in concrete terms in relation to the multilateral guardrails that might have to be put round artificial intelligence for the future?

Professor Robert Trager: Thank you, Chair, and the committee. I am very glad to be here. I am Robert Trager. I direct something called the Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative. I am a social scientist and a professor of international relations. I was a professor for many years at the University of California, Los Angeles. I have also taught at Yale and a couple of other places. I am glad to be at Oxford now. The institute I direct has both a social science and technical side. We have four labs focusing on the governance of artificial intelligence. We are now training about 22 graduate students in the governance of AI.

Getting to the question, what has the United Nations done? The United Nations began in earnest a couple of years ago with the High-Level Advisory Body on AI. To the surprise of many, it moved quickly. I thought the interim report was very good. It came out before anybody really imagined that it could. That was a diverse body. By the end, it made a series of recommendations. One of the most significant has been the scientific report, which is now part of the UN and previously had been outside it, led by Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa. That process has been very successful in identifying the latest science and risks associated with artificial intelligence in a way that has been balanced enough that all sides have agreed that it is a fair statement. We do not yet know whether that will continue, but the signs are that it will, also because the leadership has some continuity with Yoshua Bengio. That is something the UN can do.

As you say, the UN continues to discuss these issues at the global dialogue. Briefly, the UN’s strengths are universality and the legitimacy that comes with that. When it comes to statements of principle, the UN has been very successful in the past and could be in the future. We will probably get into this, but when it comes to the specifics of nuanced statements of what might be, for instance, governance best practices for artificial intelligence—the sort of thing that can make us safer—there is less of a track record at the UN for moving quickly on things like that.

The Chair: Thank you so much. Dr Melamed, thank you for coming in at short notice in place of the other witness. We are very grateful to you for calling in from Geneva.

Dr Claire Melamed: It is a real pleasure to be with you today. I am very sorry you will not have the benefit of my excellent colleague Renata Dwan’s expertise, but I will do my best. I am the vice-president for AI at the United Nations Foundation, a foundation based in the US and set up by the late Ted Turner to support the UN and its aims. We work very closely with UN leadership and different bodies to support specific activities and operations, and have an advocacy and convening role to support the broader objectives of the UN and multilateral solutions to global challenges.

Coming back to your question about how the UN has operated so far in the arena of AI governance, first I want to defend somewhat the talking shop. Bringing people around the table to talk about difficult issues should not be underestimated as an important part of solving problems. If one stops there, clearly that would not be sufficient to produce any concrete results, but that is the necessary starting point of any attempt to deal with anything as comprehensive and challenging as AI, or any of the other many challenges we confront, so for the moment let me stand up for the talking shop. As Professor Trager just said, the UN has a lot to be proud of here, building on its two key strengths. As we think through the discussion about what the role of the UN might be, it is important to remember what it is and, by extension, what it is not, and what we should and should not expect of that particular body vis-à-vis others.

As Professor Trager said, the UN has two particular sources of legitimacy here. The first is inclusivity. It is the only place where all the world’s governments sit around the table. On AI governance in particular, it has also made multi-stakeholder engagement a hallmark of a lot of what has happened. It has brought other key bodies around the table. Secondly, there is the normative framework of which the UN is custodian: the bodies on human rights and sustainable development built up over decades, none of which has been developed specifically with a view to AI but underpin the values and norms on which so much global governance rests, both inside and outside the UN. Those are two elements where the UN has specific legitimacy.

To run through some of the specific achievements, as Professor Trager said, specific architecture has been constructed around the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, whose report demonstrated the validity of this much more inclusive work. There have been lots of reports on AI over recent years. The first report of the Independent International Scientific Panel, which came out last July, was different from the others in demonstrating what happens when you put together a very broad set of academic expertise from different disciplines and different countries. You come out with something recognisably different. Of course, the Global Dialogue on AI Governance is very much a place where people come together to talk, but the conversation they have is very different and specific to sharing best practice, exchanging ideas and so on, which will be at the heart of how we manage this.

I will also point quickly to two other things. One is that the UN, even prior to this, moved quite quickly to deploy its core business and look at agreements on normative frameworks to help underpin rule-making, regulations and policies that may happen in other places on AI, in the Human Rights Council in 2019 and UNESCO in 2021. Those are examples of governments being able to come together and agree collectively on shared normative frameworks.

The other point, which is somewhat unloved but an incredibly important element of what the UN does, is that there are parts of the UN that bring together governments to agree regulatory frameworks. I am sure it escaped the notice of the world’s media, but in June a UN body meeting in Geneva adopted the first global regulatory framework on autonomous vehicles. The market for autonomous vehicles is a global one and absolutely needs some sort of common standards. It is in the interests of everybody—exporters, importers, manufacturers and governments who want to attract investment from those manufacturers—to have common frameworks. The UN was the body that brought together the US, the European Union, China and many other countries to agree a common framework on autonomous vehicles. This is one small and highly technical thing, but it is an example of where often inclusivity is the point and the forum that is needed is one that is inclusive. Parts of the UN command that legitimacy and trust to be the place where governments look to get that job done.

The Chair: Thank you. I certainly welcome your defence of the talking shop. As somebody who has addressed the General Assembly of the United Nations on two occasions, but not about my favourite subject, it has enormous merit as well.

Baroness Crawley: Looking at the UN’s agenda, what priority is it giving AI governance alongside its other major policies on climate change and peacekeeping? Where is the budget and priority going as far as AI is concerned? Separately, what influence will the new Secretary-General have on the AI agenda of the UN?

Professor Robert Trager: The current Secretary-General has been very activated by AI issues. He has wanted to make that a priority, has attended many, many meetings, for hours I understand, and scientific meetings also. Years ago, I think it was a surprise to many in the scientific community that he showed the interest he did and wanted to prioritise this. There was an ambitious agenda. It is fair to say that that agenda has been partially successful. Some states have pushed back against aspects of that agenda. Fundamentally, this technology is dual use, and states have very significant interests that are divergent. That makes it a tough place for those states that, let us say, are at the frontier of the technology and may have different interests from others to feel like this is where they want to see governance happen.

Baroness Crawley: Which states have pushed back on AI governance at the UN?

Professor Robert Trager: I cannot claim to be privy to every detail, but I think it is fair to say that in important ways the United States has pushed back, even under the previous Administration. I think there are some divergencies of interests here. To the extent that we are interested in relating current discussions also to lethal autonomous weapons discussions, which have been happening for about 15 years or so, give or take, in the UN at the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, there are some parallels to be drawn about the ways that states will allow the discussion to proceed, or not. Quickly, on the last part of your question, the new Secretary-General will have tremendous influence. The things that have happened so far have been a result of a push from the top. A new Secretary-General, whoever that is, will have tremendous influence on whether that push continues.

Dr Claire Melamed: I very much agree with Professor Trager. It is important to recognise the leadership of the current Secretary-General and his foresight in seeing that this was going to become a hugely critical global issue, perhaps when others were somewhat still less certain.

I will add a few things to what has already been said. First, though I completely agree that there are some very influential voices that are not so keen, there is clear demand from member states for a global conversation on AI and, secondly, for the UN to be the forum for at least a part of that. We saw that very clearly at the recent high-level General Assembly convened in September, where my team did a little survey of the exceptionally useful new AI-enabled UN Transcripts service. From that, one can see that two-thirds of the Heads of State who spoke—193 representatives of governments who spoke at the General Assembly— mentioned AI as a critical issue for the world to address in what was essentially an annual summary of the priorities of the world’s leaders. A very large number, over 50%, mentioned the UN as an important forum for at least part of that conversation.

The new Secretary-General is at the service of member states and there will be demand, not from everywhere but from a large number of member states, for the UN to continue to play a role. There are a number of opportunities next year for the role that the UN should play and, by extension, the areas that it should not necessarily address to be further refined, which I hope will allow for a more productive political conversation. Some things are already baked in. The Global Digital Compact, which has underpinned a lot of what has been happening on AI governance in the UN since it was agreed two years ago, will be renegotiated, starting next September. That is already baked into existing processes and something the new Secretary-General will in part be responsible for seeing through.

We also know from repeated experience that, whenever there is a major global crisis, most countries discover that the inclusivity and convening role of the UN can be very useful in helping them to align on responses. Should we see some sort of global crisis—let us hope we do not—that is at least partially caused by AI, it seems exceptionally likely to me that member states will call on the UN to play some sort of role in helping them manage that. There are lots of reasons, other than the fact that it would seem a logical choice for any new Secretary-General to see AI for what it is: an area of major global concern where the UN can play a role. There will also be demand from member states and what has already been set up in current processes for the UN to continue to work. There will be a need for evolution of that role, and perhaps we will come to that later in the session. I think it will be there. I would guess, if I am allowed to, that perhaps conflict resolution and climate may seem more urgent, but AI would be a close third there.

The Chair: How many speeches at the General Assembly were created by ChatGPT or other means?

Dr Claire Melamed: A small industry has been created among AI watchers trying to spot where leaders say the same thing. Could that possibly be because they are all using the same model to generate their speeches? I could not possibly comment.

The Chair: I know that there was a recent study done of speeches in the House of Commons which produced a remarkable number of people using these means.

Baroness Fraser of Craigmaddie: “AI” is such a buzzword, which always slightly concerns me. Everybody is talking about it. As we have already heard, it has dual use and states have different interests. Can you point to any specific areas of AI governance where you believe we are most likely to see engagement within the UN in years to come? Are there specific agendas or initiatives that could win endorsement from the major states, particularly the US and China? Are these likely to be small, technical things? Dr Melamed, you mentioned autonomous vehicles as an example. Lethal autonomous weapons could be another. Or is it likely to be broader, around the general pointing to risks? We can identify risks, but do we need to look deeper to pick off each one and deal with them one at a time?

Dr Claire Melamed: This is an absolutely huge topic. Part of the challenge in trying to get to grips with it from a politics and policy point of view is: what are we talking about when we say “AI governance”? Many things will need to be addressed across different areas of policy in different institutions. Part of a big role we can all play is to help clarify what it is we are talking about and take the issue apart somewhat. There are elements that relate to supply chains and critical minerals; there are elements that pertain to the application of AI in specific sectors, be it health or criminal justice, that perhaps will require some of their own governance instruments, as well as those that are more generic to AI. There are governance frameworks that pertain also to specific types of technology, whether that be open-weight models or models on the frontier, and the different places that you may look to regulate all of those.

Coming back to your question and the specific areas that we might look at, our first task is to take apart this issue somewhat and say: where are there areas of AI governance that satisfy two criteria—one that they are important and the other that they are tractable at the moment in our existing institutions and processes? We have already mentioned the urgency and anxiety about the use of AI in the military. I would certainly say that satisfies the first of those criteria. It is absolutely incredibly urgent and important. I think the political consensus there is a bit less clear, but nonetheless it is important to try.

Coming to your point about specific agendas, one thing we are starting to see at the moment, which is very encouraging, is the formation of coalitions around specific areas. One of the most significant things that has happened over the past few months, particularly around the UN General Assembly, is two particular groupings of middle powers coming together to align on shared agendas, shared concerns and some shared objectives for AI governance. Later this month in Barbados, that group of middle powers, which issued what was called “A Call for Control of Frontier AI Models”, will convene at a summit in Barbados co-hosted by the United Nations Secretary-General and the Prime Minister of Barbados. To my mind, this is exactly how it should work. We should see a coalition of the willing and where governments are starting to express and identify areas where consensus and action might be possible. This is not a linear matter, but in the UN at different points there will be some conversations that continue in coalitions of governments. There may be places where the UN can play a more or less useful role, but the important thing is the iteration of those two so that in a sense we can have both things: small coalitions of governments that want to act together quickly and a channel to bring those conversations into the wider and more inclusive frameworks that one can only get inside the UN.

Baroness Fraser of Craigmaddie: Professor Trager, would you agree with that, or add to it?

Professor Robert Trager: I definitely agree with what has been said. Maybe it is worth contextualising for a second where we are. One question is about what the UN can do, but another is what anyone can do. We have companies racing ahead and competing with one another, and they appear very likely soon to cross the so-called line of recursive self-improvement. We do not really know whether that will lead to models improving themselves. Recursivity is sort of the definition of an explosion, and we do not know whether it will be a small explosion or a sword of Damocles—or a mop of Damocles—hanging over our heads, but it is right there. In that urgent context, one of my colleagues at the university said recently they thought that governments need to move from traditional governing processes to crisis management, and that is the frame we should have.

In that context, what can the UN do? It is a fascinating question. I underline something that Dr Melamed said earlier: there are coalitions of states in favour of governance through the UN. That very much includes China, which has always pushed for governance that way, so there will be a push there. If we take again the discussions on lethal autonomous weapons, has there been an attempt at governance through the UN? Yes, there has, but after 15 years I do not think it could be called a success story. Given those dynamics, it is clear why that is. In a few weeks, with the Review Conference on the CCW, they will decide if they are even going to discuss lethal autonomous weapons after 15 years. It is not clear that they will answer yes to that, in which case processes may move outside the UN.

We talked about pointing to principle, and the call for control led by Finland and Norway was also mentioned. As it happens, I am just back from talking to some of those folks in Oslo yesterday. There is an ambition there. There is a possibility of doing some things through the UN. I remember the code of practice which is part of the EU AI Act now. In the EU, that is the part that directly applies to frontier companies. You could say that that was done by 12 people, with four working groups, four chairs and two vice-chairs of each, but it was really written by the vice-chairs, so eight people. I think it has had a large effect. The Nordic call now has 32 countries signed on to that. If that is moving through the UN and is done effectively, where they get leaders in fields to commit very intensively to develop a code of practice, and that then can be consensed upon and create an ecosystem where investors, insurance companies and, for that matter, states in their own domestic regulation key off of that and a mechanism for updating it quickly, you can imagine some possibilities there. We do not have much time. We have to be careful of going down a road.

To take another example quickly, the Financial Action Task Force that exists today deals with money laundering and then later terrorist financing. For a number of years, that process was tried through the UN unsuccessfully. Then France, when it had the chairpersonship of the G7, and the US together took the idea of the taskforce to the G7. It had 16 countries behind it at launch. Then it went to 48 countries, and now it is binding or at least interacting importantly with all the financial centres of the world. We see this in arms control as well. There are many cases where we see a process tried through the UN for years, it is not successful, and there is the need then for something else. There are possibilities for doing things through the UN, but the questions are: do we have time, and will it, ultimately, for a dual-use technology, prove successful?

Baroness Fraser of Craigmaddie: Can you clarify something about the code of practice from the Nordic states that you referred to? Given the pace of technological advancement, is there a risk that that is shutting the stable door after the horse has bolted?

Professor Robert Trager: Is the code of practice shutting the stable door after the horse? That is a very important question. People in the field talk about big “S” standards and little “s” standards, the big “S” being the things that run through longer processes and usually refer to process standards, whereas little “s” is more like best practices that have to evolve very quickly. We need that disjuncture. We can have something like the code of practice that will not be able to specify every detail, but it can give a statement of principles that are then worked out as to what they actually mean and evolve very quickly.

To your point, aspects of the code of practice are clearly not fit for purpose today or are just things that it does not cover. Probably the biggest is internal deployments of models. A lot of the risks today relate to the internal models that frontier companies are producing. We have all heard now about the Hugging Face incident. That was a case where, as we just talked about, internal models that had never been released to the public were doing all these things. In fact, most of the cyber incidents that we are talking about—thousands of them now, although we do not know the details—are internal models. The COP does not cover those. That is an example of where we need to extend those things. We need to have processes that are ready to extend, even at a high level, those principles.

The Chair: Can I press you a little bit on this issue? What principles should apply with any standards and regulations that we might bring in? What basic principles should apply in the case of artificial intelligence?

Professor Robert Trager: That is a complicated question. The most basic principle probably is that there should be what is often called a safety case. There should be a case made by a company that wants to develop something, deploy it internally or deploy it in the world, that this thing is in fact safe. The principle should be that the company is required to do that. Then you get into the details of what makes something safe. That is contested. Even questions such as how often it should make a safety case are evolving very quickly. Previously, when we had the model of a giant training run using lots of compute that we all heard about, and then a little bit of what is called reinforcement learning with human feedback—a little bit of stuff that they did after the giant training run about every six months—it was clear that every time you did the six-month thing you make a safety case. But the world is very different now. Internal deployments are happening all the time. Changes to models that could affect their safety properties are happening moment to moment. Exactly how we have governance of that is an open question. I think it has to involve some automated oversight of the things that are happening.

I will mention one other principle that is very important. We need to know what systems are doing. Going back to the OpenAI Hugging Face incident, it tried to falsify the record—that is the system itself; nobody asked it to—of what it had done. Actually, we do not know for sure if it was unsuccessful. We could insist that systems are provisioned so that they are hosted by a compute provider in such a way that we have at least a tamper-evident record of the things that they have done. Everything starts from that. If a system looks like it is not aligned, as they say, and is going to do things that its creators do not want, you cannot even begin to think about how you would make it aligned and fix it if you do not know the things that it is doing, either out there in the world or anywhere else.

Then we can think geopolitically. I will stop after this because I could go on about these things.

Baroness Fraser of Craigmaddie: It is fascinating.

Professor Robert Trager: Geopolitically, it is very easy to imagine a crisis like the one we saw this summer with Hugging Face, where OpenAI came out and said, “It was us”. Suppose we have something similar or maybe a little bit worse in terms of infrastructure that is targeted and the effects on people, and nobody comes out saying, “Well, it was us”. You can imagine geopolitically how that can be very fraught. Nobody knows if it was a model that was acting at someone’s behest, or a model going out and doing something on its own. Was it this state? Was it that state? It could get very fraught. Would it not be nice if we had a black box like you have in the airline industry, where some actors could come out and say cryptographically, “Actually, this wasn’t us, and we can prove that to you”? That is not a hypothetical technology. We could do things like that. How to do that in ways that are not vulnerable to hacking is a partly active area of research. That is a very doable sort of thing that we could insist on and in fact invest in.

The Chair: Dr Melamed, we noticed you nodding. Do you agree?

Dr Claire Melamed: Yes. It is a really interesting question. I would add two things. First, on that final point on the potential for embedding some of this verification and transparency inside the hardware, that is really critical. As that becomes possible technologically, it is a good example of the sort of thing that will likely happen only if key governments that make up a sufficient proportion of the market require it to happen. That is a very good example of something that, once it becomes technologically possible, needs to be mandated for it to properly become embedded in all systems.

On your question about principles and the answer about safety, I completely agree that “do no harm” is the first principle of any professional technology that one can think of. The added complication here when it comes to the conversation about AI governance and how we might translate that into practical proposals is that I do not think there is a strong consensus yet on the risks that we are trying to prevent. What do we want to be kept safe from when it comes to AI? We have had a lot of conversations talking about some sort of hypothetical future scenarios such as 10% extinction. That is in a sense one obvious risk, although very ill-defined as to exactly how that might happen, to be kept safe from. Over the summer particularly, and even just this last week with the attacks on fridges and whatever, the gap between the future and the present is closing, and we are starting to see more clearly some of those channels through which the AI risks may manifest through digital infrastructure, cybercrime and so on. One area that we need to think about to be able to answer your very important question is to arrive at some sort of stronger common view—it will not look perfect; it will look different in different contexts—on what the risks are here that we are trying to manage and what the channels are through which they are most likely to manifest themselves across different parts of our various economic, social and political systems.

The Chair: Thank you very much. That is good clarification.

Baroness Prashar: I have three related questions. First, how would you assess the UK’s involvement in the United Nations initiatives? Secondly, does the UK have a clear idea how it wishes to influence the United Nations? Thirdly, how can it best work through the system to achieve real co-operation on shared values, norms and standards—what you, Dr Melamed, described as an underlying normative framework?

Dr Claire Melamed: The UK is a very important player in the United Nations. It is one of the permanent members of the Security Council, with a veto. It has an outsized significance. I am often asked, being based in New York now, for some clues as to what the UK might be thinking, not that I in any sense speak for the UK. There is a real interest in the UK’s thinking and action here. We have not mentioned it yet, but perhaps we will come to it, but with the announcement by the Prime Minister at the General Assembly that AI will be a key part of the UK’s G20 presidency this year, that interest in what the UK may do and think in the G20, and in other related multilateral forums, has only grown. This is an important question and becoming more so.

The UK’s posture in the UN to date has been somewhat cautious. It has seen risks ahead in some of these UN discussions, not least caused by the awkwardness and difficulty of having to triangulate the views of other countries, some of which of course are the UK’s key allies and partners. There has been that difficulty. We have mentioned at several points already some of the geopolitical tensions and risks in this agenda. The inclusivity of the UN has many upsides, but the downside is that you import all those most fraught geopolitical risks and conversations inside the process when you have any kind of process within the UN. The UK has very much been alive to that real and credible danger of, in a sense, starting something at the UN where perhaps you will not necessarily always be able to control how it ends because of the way that those geopolitics might play out on all sides. The posture has been one of some caution, of “wait and see”. Apart from that sense of risks and anxieties, as a result of adopting a largely cautious posture, a clear positive and propositional agenda on the part of the UK has not necessarily been very prominent.

The UK has some real assets here. It has, most notably, the AI Security Institute. It was the first country to set up this sort of institute, but has been followed by many others, demonstrating the rightness and timeliness of the idea. It has a lot of things to build on. As someone who believes both in the UN and in the UK as forces for good in the world, I would love to see the UK take more of a leadership role, deploy its assets and the broader geopolitical stance as a bridge builder, coalition builder, and as a country that wants to uphold some of the core values of the UN, but do so in a highly pragmatic and action-focused way. That is exactly what we need here, and I really hope that the UK takes the opportunity of leadership in the G20 and the potential to take that leadership into other forums to work in a very collegiate way with existing coalitions. Not all countries have been waiting for UK leadership. They have gone ahead and built their own coalitions, but I think there would be a welcome for the UK in those coalitions.

Also, there is something of—I do not know how to put it—a gap in the political market for a country to play the role as a real leader on the responsible adoption of AI. We have this face-off between the US and China geopolitically on the frontier. Perhaps the UK and Europe are unlikely to be highly competitive with those countries on the frontier, but, even if they are not, there is a role in adopting leadership of the coalition that really shapes what AI looks like not in the future but right now as it is adopted through hundreds of thousands of millions of enterprises, government departments and so on all around the world. There is not a real lead there. Some people want that adoption debate to focus much more on the safety issues. Others want it to focus much more on questions of capacity, infrastructure and equity in adoption. These are not mutually exclusive positions. We need to have both. There is a role for the taking for a country that can bring those positions together, which are not as different as they think they are, and create a meaningful global supportive coalition around responsible adoption. I would love to see the UK use its G20 presidency to work with others who have already started on that journey and to make that the thing that we are talking about on AI governance.

Professor Robert Trager: First, I agree with Dr Melamed’s remarks, and will underline a couple of things. There is the world-leading expertise that the UK has. You really feel that when you go to meetings and there are government representatives from around the world. I have just been at such a meeting. The UK representatives are very well informed because of all the work done by, in particular, the AI Security Institute and generally by the Civil Service here over years. No other country has that level of expertise in government that the UK has. It is not even close within a government.

I see slight surprise at that so I underscore it, because it relates to the role that the UK has on the world stage. Just look at the funding of the UK AI Security Institute versus the equivalent in other places, including in the US. It is an order of magnitude difference at least. Even in the US—I have not looked at the latest numbers, so I do not want to say exactly what they are—it is at least 10 times just in terms of funding. That is important to remember when we think about what the UK can do. There is an upskilling role that is needed in terms of explaining the issues to countries around the world, and the UK is uniquely positioned for that.

I also think the G20 is key. The UK has already said it wants to use its leadership of the G20 to forward AI governance. Exactly how it will do that is the question. Very understandably, that is being worked out now, as is exactly what can be done. I highlight again the relationship to the US here, because of course that is central to whatever the UK does at the UN. Currently, in AI, one of the most hopeful features of the whole landscape, bar none, is the fact that the AI Security Institute has access to frontier US models pre-deployment, and is highly synced up with that ecosystem in the US and exercising a lot of influence that way. Especially given what I just said about its expertise, which is not replicable anywhere else, that is so important.

Professor Robert Trager: There are some distinctions to be made here. Most of the useful governance in the modern world today is not treaty based. It is soft law.
