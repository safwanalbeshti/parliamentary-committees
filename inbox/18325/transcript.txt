Examination of witnesses

Kevin Sefton, Vinay Anupindi, Lyubomira Dimitrova and Nick Tracey.

The Chair: Welcome to today’s meeting, which is the sixth oral evidence session as part of the committee’s inquiry into regulatory sandboxes. Thank you to Mr Sefton, Mr Anupindi, Ms Dimitrova and Mr Tracey for attending. Perhaps to start off you could introduce yourselves and tell us a little bit about your companies. Then we will proceed to questions.

Lyubomira Dimitrova: Hi, everyone. Good morning. I am co-founder of Relace. Relace is a regulatory technology platform and product. We are currently part of the FCA supercharged sandbox cohort 2, which runs from July until the end of the year, so we are a current participant.

Kevin Sefton: Hi, good morning. Thanks very much for having me. I originally qualified as a chartered accountant and chartered tax adviser with Arthur Andersen and have spent most of the last 30 years working in technology, generally adjacent to financial services.

I am here today as founder of Untied, which is a personal tax software product that helps people categorise transactions from open banking and other sources. They use Untied to file their self-assessment and now Making Tax Digital. We were in the fifth cohort of the FCA sandbox in 2019 and I hold senior sector roles, including on the general council of the accounting software body BASDA and with HMRC.

Vinay Anupindi: I am co-founder of Monee Financial Technologies. It is an honour, and one that I never expected, to give evidence before this esteemed House and its committee. Our story began in 2019 with the conviction that securities issued on distributed ledgers would one day be a standard for national infrastructure. In 2020, we applied to the FCA sandbox. However, we were not accepted and, in hindsight, rightly so, as the innovation was quite early.

What followed was something of an odyssey in search of a regulator and a jurisdiction. While we kept building, we found that most jurisdictions were what we call innovation theatre. We came to the UK not because it was easy, but because it was serious. We are currently in multiple sandboxes, including the Bank of England’s digital securities sandbox with the FCA, the FCA stablecoin sandbox and the Bank of England synchronisation lab. Today, we are building wholesale sterling settlement infrastructure for tokenised markets. We firmly believe that innovation does not need to come at the expense of regulation. Done well, regulation is what enables responsible innovation. I am looking forward to the discussion today.

Nick Tracey: Hi, thanks for the invite. I am CEO and co-founder of Bourn Technologies. We are developing an alternative to the business overdraft for SMEs that want to grow and we participated in the regulatory sandbox in 2025.

The Chair: Why did you go into the sandbox and how is it living up to your expectations?

Lyubomira Dimitrova: Why did we go into the sandbox? We are building regulatory technology and helping firms manage their regulatory posture and adherence, and it is a very data-heavy solution. The main reason why we wanted to go into the sandbox is to understand the regulatory regimes really intimately and in depth.

The product that we have developed is for consumer duty, which is a very recent regime. It is only three years old and a lot of the firms out there are struggling to figure out how to do it well, because it touches all parts of a company. We wanted to understand what the regulator is trying to achieve with this regime, what struggles it is seeing and how we can help. That was the main reason why we wanted to join.

The experience has been positive. There are areas that have been challenging as well. I can cover that now or pass on and come back to this later.

Kevin Sefton: The reason we took part in the sandbox was really straightforward. We needed regulation in order to run our business. At the time, open banking was coming in and there was a regime for account information service provision. At the time, you needed direct regulation from the FCA in order to do that. We had a choice of whether we went for the full regulation or for the sandbox. We had lots of discussions internally and the general feeling was that the sandbox would be the quicker way to do it.

I realise that there are lots of reasons for sandboxes, and I probably have half a dozen different types of sandbox definition as well. In this case, it was not necessarily that what we were doing was new. It was not about innovation. It was the simple need to be regulated, and we thought and hoped that the sandbox would be the quickest way to do it. I am sure that we will explore during the rest of the session whether that was actually how it transpired.

Vinay Anupindi: Similarly to Kevin, we were seeking regulation. Our innovation was at the edge of the regulatory perimeter. We were talking about issuing securities and settling it on innovative technology, which required actual regulatory guidance and perhaps even new laws made. We definitely felt that this was something that had to be done with a regulator that was serious, rather than just going out there with what many other start-ups or tech companies think of: “Let’s just move fast and break things”. This was the sort of thing where you could not break things because this had to do with the actual capital market system, so we definitely saw a need for a regulator. We searched for many years to find a jurisdiction and a regulator until we found the UK and these particular sandboxes.

Nick Tracey: For us it was similar. We knew that the product that we were developing was a regulated product, so an alternative to the business overdraft, which encompassed processing payments for businesses and potentially holding client money for those businesses in the form of e-money. We knew that regulatory permissions would be required.

I guess that fintechs have broadly two paths from concept. You can either pursue authorisation as a direct route, or seek to be an agent of an existing authorised entity. We wanted to partner with large UK-regulated banks, in order to go to market with our product proposition. We decided that direct authorisation was the more credible route to build the capabilities and controls in order to partner with those banks.

Lord Sharkey: I was struck by the written evidence provided by the Startup Coalition. I do not know whether you are members of the coalition, but it certainly made striking reading. It said some very positive things about sandboxes, but it made three criticisms. The first was that there is no published success framework and therefore no way to judge any of this, talking about sandboxes in general. It points out that the published evidence is antiquated and was caveated at the time. The chief criticism from the coalition’s written evidence is that the sandbox does not connect to authorisations and that is its central failure. Would you like to comment on that?

Lyubomira Dimitrova: The point that you mentioned around there being no success criteria or clear framework really resonates. When we applied with our project, we scoped this out ourselves. In our application we were very explicit that we were looking to develop a commercial product that, by the end of the sandbox, we would want to launch and that, as part of our sandbox participation, we wanted to bring in design partners to work with from industry.

We wanted to be in the sandbox so that we could intimately understand what the regulator wants to achieve with this regulation and how we can support the market. We heard from market participants that they are really struggling with trying to collect and ferry data from different teams. They do not have enough capacity, time and resources to focus on what appear to be the objectives of that particular regulatory framework.

We had to completely set up our own success criteria. We went to the team and, as part of our project, outlined that. At no point did we discuss what success looks like with the current team running the sandbox we are in. That was not a conversation at the beginning or throughout the programme.

The other thing that I would like to mention there is that at no point did the team that is running the sandbox have a checkpoint: “How are you guys doing? What’s the progress?” We offered to do a demo of what we are building and share insights. We were not taken up on the offer. That is challenging. How can you set out success criteria and then monitor progress? We are doing it for ourselves because we are a small team and looking to launch a commercial product.

Kevin Sefton: I participate with a lot of activities that Startup Coalition runs. It is an absolutely outstanding organisation providing something that does not exist elsewhere for smaller businesses in terms of engaging with a lot of policy areas. Although I have not contributed to this direct submission that it has made, I have seen it and buy into some of the findings.

A lot of success is assessed post hoc. Has it worked? Well, what did you set out to achieve? From our point of view, we wanted regulation. That was very clear. It provided that route and we exited the sandbox successfully. That was a fairly straightforward process, although a little bit undefined. This was seven years ago, so things are probably different today.

In terms of success, what did we want from it? There were other things that I would have hoped that it would have produced. We were in a cohort. I thought that that might give us a cohort experience. We did not get a cohort experience. Did we find financial institutions that, like some of the others giving evidence, we wanted to partner with? Did they value the sandbox experience? I am not sure that they necessarily did. I do not recall any of them saying, “Where are you with the sandbox and what’s happening with the regulation?”

Does it foster genuine collaboration and opportunities between established institutions and innovative new businesses? That is what we want to achieve. We can achieve the innovative business models, but, in order to scale that, we need to be working ideally with either someone who has an awful lot of money to invest in growing a business or, probably, some of the larger institutions. If those were the criteria, albeit not set out seven years ago before we went into the sandbox, I do not think that we met that. In terms of what its observations are saying, I would agree with it, but of course the nuance is always in the detail.

Vinay Anupindi: We have not been part of any coalition and I agree that there are some nuances. In our particular case, with the digital securities sandbox and the stablecoin sandbox, I can say that there was a very detailed published framework. The objective of the sandboxes is to get licensed and go live in terms of issuing or settling securities.

As for whether it is antiquated, our experience has not been so, because the regulators and authorities are actively building policy, in which we got to participate within the sandbox. We had very clear objectives on what would be the end of the sandbox and our exit, which was to get authorisation and be live as a firm.

To be candid, it took us about six to eight months to actually apply, given our scepticism about sandboxes, initially applying with the FCA and other jurisdictions. It took a bit of back and forth with the Bank of England and the FCA. They invited us to meet them in the FCA. We came down and then concluded that the authorities in the UK are quite serious about this and that there is a whole framework being developed, not just in terms of the sandboxes, but actual policies and laws being developed. This may be a nuanced case because of the particular sandboxes we are in, but that is our experience.

Nick Tracey: I agree with the statement that regulatory sandboxes do not connect directly to authorisation. I do not think that the sandbox is intended to be a replacement for authorisation, but we saw that the FCA innovation team that runs the sandbox was very well co-ordinated with the authorisations team and sought to limit any duplication in our effort in applying for the sandbox and then applying separately for authorisation.

We are now a fully authorised electronic money institution. There were only, I believe, 18 authorised electronic money institutions in 2025. Our authorisation came in two parts. Initially we gained restricted permissions in order to undertake regulated activity while in the sandbox. On exiting the sandbox, we submitted a variation of permissions to remove those restrictions and therefore become a fully authorised EMI.

For us, that was a straightforward and well-connected process. The total application was three months for the initial restricted permissions and an additional three months for the full or variation of permissions, so in total six months, or, end to end, including our regulatory sandbox testing, 12 months. That was probably faster than many EMIs would be able to achieve through a direct authorisation route.

Lord Hill of Oareford: You have just answered what I was going to ask, because you said that they are well co-ordinated between the sandbox and the authorisation process. Do you think that the authorisation process is expedited as a result of being in the sandbox, if you see what I mean, so you have been through the sandbox, they know you and then your authorisation period gets a bit reduced, or were you just lucky?

Nick Tracey: No, I think there is a direct connection. Testing within the regulatory sandbox had two objectives, primarily, for us. One was to technically test that the product worked and that customer feedback was positive. The other was to give us time to develop the controls and capabilities that we would need to demonstrate that we were ready to be fully authorised as an EMI.

We were very closely supervised by FCA innovation throughout the sandbox testing period. We had a monthly meeting with FCA innovation where we were able to demonstrate customer feedback and technical viability, but also the control environment that we were building internally. I am sure that, qualitatively, some of that outcome and observation, in effect from supervision, must have fed back into the authorisations process when we undertook the variation of permissions. We did not see a lot of that, but our experience was that it was well joined up. The end-to-end one-year application process felt timely.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: When applying to the sandbox, was it resource intensive and did you find that you needed to get outside advisers to help you get your application through? Was that costly as well? There is resource in cash and resource in people.

Nick Tracey: We spent around three months preparing an application to the regulatory sandbox. We were, at that stage, just a concept with three co-founders and no team. We hired a fractional compliance officer to support us with preparation of the application. It was resource intensive for a small team. We submitted probably in excess of 100 pages, so not just the application form but also supporting documents. A lot of that would have been work that we would have needed to do anyway to be a regulated entity, so I did not see that as wasted effort. All the good thinking and the preparation of policies and procedures was work that we had to do anyway. Yes, it was three months of hard work and work well spent, I think.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: It was a nice shortcut in a way.

Nick Tracey: Yes, I would say that.

Vinay Anupindi: Yes, it was resource intensive, and we believe it should be. It was intensive in terms of time and actual focus given towards the application process. At the time we were a small team, very much so, with just co-founders and a few others. We did not take any professional help. We could not afford that. We are bootstrapped still. Basically, we just went at it ourselves. If we had any questions, we asked the regulators. We read all the policies and applications, and just did it ourselves.

Kevin Sefton: We were in a very similar position to Monee in terms of doing it ourselves. Partly, that was because, if we have a compliance obligation, I tend to take the approach that I need to understand what that compliance obligation is. Therefore, the best way to do that is to write the documentation myself.

Our process may have changed since, so I will briefly walk you through it, and it is part of my written evidence as well. It was a very light-touch application to get into the sandbox itself. It was five hours’ effort to fill in a form initially, but we had already built most of the business. It then took five months, having filled that in, before we were actually approved.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: That was approval to go into the sandbox.

Kevin Sefton: Yes. I then thought, “Great, we are in the sandbox”, but it turned out that, although we were approved to be in the sandbox, we still needed to write all the documentation to get authorisation as well. We needed to go into the sandbox and to do a lot of the work to apply for registration for the firm, which was a big exercise, before we could actually start testing in the sandbox. In effect, you have been given the green light to go in, but you do not have all your bags packed in order to actually enter it. That was the big regulatory business plan, which I guess, from your point of view, was around about 100 pages. Ours is not that different.

From our point of view, this is an outstanding set of thinking that we had to go through. Having been asked to present business plans to all sorts of different investors and institutions, this is probably the only time that I have been asked for something that I would say is of a serious nature that I would expect, from a professional background, to have submitted and reviewed. That took us probably 80 to 100 hours of effort, so two solid weeks writing that.

I do not know whether I am answering the question, but we built the product, we were in the sandbox and then we were told, “You have to write this document to get registration for the firm to start the testing”. That took two or three solid weeks of additional effort to produce the document. Then we got the registration confirmed and were in the sandbox.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: Then what happened? Are they testing your technology or your authorisation worthiness?

Kevin Sefton: They are largely testing your authorisation worthiness, but we are testing the technology. We were allowed to be offering services to 200, I think, people. As a tax business, of course it is very seasonal. You do not want to start offering Christmas trees in January. We were acutely aware that we needed to make sure that we were authorised at a level in the sandbox such that we could be offering tax services in time for the 31 January deadline for self-assessment.

We were allowed to do that for a limited number of people. We realised quite quickly that it was working. Then how do we extend that to full regulation to allow us to be offering it to more people? That was the process that my colleagues described before. Quite quickly, we realised that, as a principle, this was working and we were moving very quickly to say, “How do we exit the sandbox?”

In terms of the effort that we needed to exit, it was a lot of conversation, but not a lot more documentation that was required. This is seven years ago, so I was looking through my notes in preparation. I see in my notes that I said how helpful the FCA team was during that process on the weekly calls. I apologise for the fact that I do not remember how helpful they were, but I am very glad that I documented it. If they are listening, the fact that I do not remember does not mean that I am not grateful.

Lyubomira Dimitrova: How do I follow this up? My answer here is very short. Effort is subjective. Compared to the effort these firms had to go through, ours was less. We spent most of the time thinking about the problem and scoping the project. Writing the application does not take too much time when you are clear on what you want to test and what your proposition is.

We used an adviser, not paid, just friendly, who helped us write the application. They were someone who participated in a sandbox. That was valuable because they knew the structure and what needed to be written. That was valuable. We managed to get a meeting prior to our application with the lead of our current sandbox to run through, “This is what we are thinking. This is what we would like to test. Is that interesting? Is that viable?” They gave us feedback, so that was useful as well.

Lord Eatwell: I am interested in not just what you got out of it, positive or negative, in the process, but also what the regulators got out of it. I am struck that Mr Anupindi said that he was seeking regulation at one point. He also said just now that the regulators were actively building policy, which suggests that they were learning from you. The whole point of the sandbox for the industry is that it is supposed to remove regulatory risk. You are making a lot of investment in developing new products and processes. There is a risk associated with this that the regulator will say, “Sorry, no”, and your money has just gone down a black hole.

That is the role that this is supposed to perform for you. Did you find in the process, or are you finding in the process in the case of Ms Dimitrova, that the regulators were learning and that this was a learning experience for them?

Lyubomira Dimitrova: There is a really big missed opportunity here in general. We could have better cohort experience. In our cohort right now, we had two touchpoints or opportunities to gather as a cohort and meet each other. That was at the kick-off and at a mid-point meeting. Beyond that, it is a very siloed experience. You work in your sandbox, you have a point contact that they assign to you and that is it.

There is very little opportunity to mingle with the rest of the firms. We asked to be introduced to a couple of other firms where we think we can work together to prove a point for both firms. We were not introduced. It was quicker to get an introduction through the firm or the company outside. That is a big missed opportunity.

As I mentioned, we offered to provide them feedback. Again, it is a missed opportunity to receive that. Maybe they are taking learnings and feedback, both on what is working operationally and on the technological side, but, in full honesty, I do not have evidence of that. That is not my experience.

Kevin Sefton: I am hopeful that they have learned, because things have changed since we took part. It might well be that that is based on feedback from us. A regulator learning from a sandbox is an essential part of a sandbox.

I talked about HMRC. If you will forgive me a little bit of a detour, the tax system is a highly regulated environment, one where there is a lot of opportunity for innovation, but also a lot of risk that needs to be managed. In that sort of sector, a sandbox has a role with some trusted conversations between innovators and a regulator about what innovation may do, but also, quite frankly, around the risks of that innovation. Particularly in today’s world, where AI allows you to code overnight all sorts of applications that can do things in lots of different systems, often outside regulation, an active conversation between a regulator and those innovators within that sandbox is absolutely essential.

Alongside that, there needs to be some benefit though for the innovators in the sandbox. In other words, if a company is testing a new product or idea, working closely with the regulator and then the regulator says, “This looks like a safe business model. We are going to allow it”, is there an advantage or a disadvantage for the participant that has spent that time investing not just in their business but in educating the regulator and the market in something? You do not want to find that the day that you are authorised to be conducting business is the day that everybody else is authorised as well. It is a little bit like a drug coming off patent the day that you are able to start marketing it.

Vinay Anupindi: In response, I believe that we are both learning at the same time, definitely so in our case. While the regulators are making or solidifying the parameters that they are developing and utilising the sandbox for more information and data, we at the same time were able to improve our own processes and meet the regulators’ expectations. We found that we were able to make suggestions that they took note of. We have seen this being implemented as policy as well while we were working in the sandbox.

Unlike my colleagues over here, we have not been authorised yet. We are still in the testing phase, so we do not see any expedition in terms of us getting authorised. The only advantage we see is that perhaps we are more operationally ready.

We have been engaging with the regulators not just in the sandboxes, but also in the consultation responses for policies that have been put out there. The BoE, HMT and the FCA have been very clear on what kinds of policies they are developing and where things are going. They have asked industry to give comment on that and we have done that actively. We have seen while this new regime is being developed that they have taken industry into account. Perhaps, humbly, we can say also that certain suggestions we have given have been taken into account.

It is definitely a process on both sides and this comes down to regulatory risk. We were seeking regulation because we firmly believed from the conception that this was an industry and an innovation that would require regulation and would not scale, expand and become a reality, if you will, in the marketplace without regulatory hosting. This is why we conceptualise it as something that needed to be regulatory grade, sovereign grade and, in our use case, institutional grade.

Nick Tracey: From our perspective, our experience of working with the FCA, and the FCA innovation team in particular, was that it appeared to be collaborative, genuinely curious about the innovations that we were bringing forward and keen to learn from what we were doing. Our particular innovations included application of AI, so large language models and machine learning, but also open banking, which is still maturing in terms of its uptake. The FCA, I think, has a new mandate for open banking as well. That was an area where, for example, it introduced us to the open banking specialists within the FCA to share ideas in a two-way setting. They certainly seemed interested in that.

We also noted that the FCA publishes Innovation Insights, where it includes case studies at a more general level. Throughout, from our perspective, it seemed to be interested to learn and responsive.

Lord Lilley: I apologise to the Chair and witnesses for missing some of the opening remarks. Could you explain a little more what happens when you are in a sandbox? What are you allowed or not allowed to do that you would be allowed or not allowed to do if you were outside the sandbox?

Lyubomira Dimitrova: When you are in the sandbox, you have access to datasets.

Lord Lilley: Could you elaborate on what a dataset is in this context?

Lyubomira Dimitrova: For example, you have different types of data, which are more often than not synthetic. That means created data points. If you want to test something—for example, banking transactions and processing transactions—you have synthetic data that helps you simulate and test your solution. Part of what we tested and used this data for is complaints. You have complaints data, so Ms Maria Dimitrova is writing a complaint about X, Y, Z. They have this dataset that we can use to test, for example, our technology. You have access to datasets that you can use to test your solution. That is a technological benefit; that is useful.

As part of our sandbox, we also have credit with some of the cloud providers and some coding assistance. That is also useful, especially for a start-up. It is quite beneficial.

You get mentors. For us specifically, the mentors have been one of the most valuable things. The mentors are industry people. One mentor is actually sitting at the back. They have been one of the most helpful things about this sandbox because they give you different feedback. Some are, for example, IP specialists, data protection specialists, banking executives or previous participants in sandboxes. You can utilise this resource, and that is very helpful.

They give you the environment, and then you can develop and work with your solution and test it with the datasets. One thing that we really wanted to do, and would have benefited from, is to have an expert in consumer duty working with us. That is probably one of the big disappointments for our side. Over four months, we have received two times 30-minute meetings with someone working on consumer duty. While they were amazing, they were also a junior, let us say, member of the team. They were amazing and really helpful. In thinking about how to improve this, having a point contact for the company who is expert and genuinely interested in the area would be really useful.

Lord Lilley: Mr Sefton, you mentioned having 200 customers.

Kevin Sefton: Yes, I was listening to that and I thought, “That doesn’t reflect our sandbox”. The word “sandbox” means so many different things to so many different people. It has achieved, as someone else referred to, this mythic status. Unfortunately, that means that we do not actually pin down what a sandbox is.

Understanding and separating out the different purposes—there may be overlap—is going to be helpful for regulators and something that you may be thinking about as well, whether that is testing a new business model or business technology. It may be something that everyone is thinking about across the whole industry. There may be no commercial advantage, but is this a possibility? It may be something the regulators themselves are thinking about.

From our point of view, it was simply limited regulation that allowed us to support 200 people, and then we extended that to more. The key thing is what each sandbox is intended to achieve. That one word means so many different things. It is hard from the outside, and sometimes from the inside, to know what type of sandbox you are in.

Vinay Anupindi: In our case, it was a different experience and more nuanced. We did not have any data given by the regulator because there is no data in our industry. In fact, we had to give the regulators simulated data to show them testing in our case. The sandbox provided us with a named case officer and a team.

In our particular sandbox—the stablecoin sandbox—we had an intense cadence of every week or every two weeks where we would meet our case officer and the teams in the FCA, from policy and payments to financial crime, basically testing all our procedures and putting us a little bit on notice on our architecture, what technology we are using, how we were maintaining compliance with the regulations, how we were maintaining prudential security and many other things. In our case, for the stablecoin sandbox, which we still have to exit and get authorised for, it was very interesting. As Kevin said, the concept of sandbox may be general, but each sandbox in itself may have specific criteria. How each sandbox and its participants and cohort are dealt with may be more nuanced.

The other sandbox we are in is the digital securities sandbox, which is more focused with the Bank of England. It has not been as intense. It is more of a multi-year sandbox. The intention, I believe, is to make it into a more permanent regime. With that noted, the regulators in that sandbox have been engaging with us actively. We go back and forth quite often—I would say almost multiple times in a week—but there is a longer timeframe for that sandbox than the stablecoin sandbox.

Nick Tracey: We participated in the regulatory sandbox. I guess that the consistent theme across all the sandboxes is probably innovation or enabling innovation in some form. For the regulatory sandbox, we were able to undertake regulated activity on a limited basis to test our innovation.

It is worth saying that that process was governed very closely by a test plan. In advance of being accepted, we had to develop a very detailed plan for exactly what we would do during the sandbox period. Ours had around six phases, including launching technically the product, testing the technical effectiveness of the product, starting to test that with customers in live, recording customer feedback and stress testing for scalability and resilience of the platform that we built.

There were a number of phases and it was a six-month test for us, during which we had roughly monthly engagements with FCA innovation. That enabled it to monitor our performance against the test plan. I guess that that had a dual purpose of giving confidence that the innovative product was effective and met customer needs, and, at the same time, familiarising ourselves with the FCA and vice versa, so that, if we were about to pursue a full authorisation route, it already had some awareness of us.

FCA innovation in this context is essentially providing the supervision ahead of being a fully authorised firm. In effect, you have a close, active supervision throughout the regulatory sandbox.

In fact, I would say that the digital securities sandbox is the form of waiver for us to test this out. I believe that the BoE has specifically made the sandbox so that it can give waivers within the sandbox from the CSD regulations.

The idea is that there is a controlled, regulated environment where the supervisors can observe us. We can play, test this out and make sure nobody gets hurt. Lord Griffiths of Fforestfach: As you reflect on having been in the sandbox, do you think that it has given you a competitive advantage in terms of raising money, attracting the right people, et cetera?

Nick Tracey: I feel like it has certainly given us a competitive advantage, in that we are now a fully authorised EMI and one of only 18 firms to achieve that in 2025. I believe we did that in a good amount of time, and more quickly than we might otherwise have achieved it.

Vinay Anupindi: I would answer yes and no. We have not been able to raise money yet, so I do not know whether that is an advantage there. Lord Griffiths of Fforestfach: You are saying that, post the sandbox, your regulatory relationship with the FCA and the fund that you have just mentioned was such that they knew you. Kevin Sefton: I have not done that. We have had calls from the FCA on different matters. I have always found it to be very helpful. Lyubomira Dimitrova: It was very helpful and useful. I know that I am providing a lot of constructive feedback on our experience. Lord Davies of Brixton: There were a couple of phrases that two of our witnesses produced earlier in their evidence, and I hope they both make their way into our final report. Vinay Anupindi: I will not name any jurisdictions or regulators specifically. We applied in 2020 and did not get in, which, by the way, was quite early and even pre Covid. We went to well-known jurisdictions, say in the Middle East, Asia and even the EU. We set up companies there. We sometimes got licensing or entered their sandboxes.
