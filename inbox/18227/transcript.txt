Q24 The Chair: Today, we are holding the second session of our inquiry, “Societal resilience: a national conversation”. May I ask our witnesses to introduce themselves briefly?

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: I am the chief resilience officer for the Suffolk Resilience Forum and a co-chair of the National Consortium of Societal Resilience—the NCSR, for short.

Professor Lee Miles: I am a professor of crisis and disaster management at the Bournemouth University Disaster Management Centre. I am also an associate dean of the business and law faculty there, so I am responsible for all things to do with that.

The Chair: Thank you very much. I will kick us off. The UK Public Survey of Risk Perception, Resilience and Preparedness found that 68% of respondents thought that the number of emergencies or disasters would increase in the next 10 years. Obviously, we have had the recent “Be Ready” announcement from the Government, but do you think that the public are sufficiently aware of the risks that we face in our local communities?

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: Obviously, there is the national risk register, which is public-facing, and we also have the national security strategy, so the documents are out there for those who want to look. Every local resilience forum should have a community risk register—again, that is a publicly available document—to let them know and to localise their risks. Obviously, we have that in Suffolk as well.

When we do community engagement events, those are another opportunity to engage with the public, but there is a balance to be struck. I think that society is quite anxious anyway, so I am not sure that everyone wants to know what all the risks are all the time, but I feel that, as long as they are available to them when they want to find out what they are from trusted sources, and all that is there, it is about finding a balance between not scaring the public with everything and being very transparent. It is there should they want to find it, if that answers the question.

Professor Lee Miles: It goes back to the point about how we see the position of risk. Obviously, we have the national risk register, which provides a clearer idea of how risks are formulated, but risk starts at home, in a sense. Fundamentally, it is about how the individual household or individual citizen sees their own vulnerabilities; it is about that translation.

The other important point to mention is that we have a very complicated aspect here: the mixed messaging that is going on around hazards and risks. It may be about flooding or issues to do with fire, which are often very localised and clear, as well as wider issues to do with strategic geopolitical issues, which are partly coming from the domain of the national security strategy. Those two elements do not necessarily map completely, in the eyes of the public, in the same way.

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: Sadly, yes, I think that that is accurate. That is what the data suggests.

I am happy that the figure is that high but, obviously, it is not as high as we would want it to be. In all of the social media and public-facing correspondence that we have ever had the ability to do, the message has always been, “Sign up for alerts”. It is nice to see that it is that much, but it is also disappointing to see that it is not as much as 100% of people. This is something that we will continue to push as a collective, from a resilience forum perspective, but, for me, that figure probably feels about right when we speak to people and have opportunities to engage with the public.

The Chair: To the point that Professor Miles just made, do you think that there are any particular sectors or types of risk vulnerability about which the public are particularly concerned, in terms of gaps in being prepared or thinking that something is a potentially a real risk to them?

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: From my experience of public engagement, generally, when we talk to people about risk—this is mostly face to face at community engagement events—they talk about power cuts and floods. Those seem to be their top risks. Then, when we have our show-and-tell events, with our grab packs and examples of items to put in the household, those always generate conversation. It comes down to asking, “What are you scared of? What do you think about?” It is interesting to find out what people are worried about. People tend to say, “Just power cuts, or if I have to evacuate because of weather”; that comes up as well. Generally, that is the feedback that I receive.

The Chair: Does that vary according to whether you are talking to people from a certain demographic, such as age or background?

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: We are also involved with Crucial Crew events, which are multi-agency engagement events for children in key stage 2. Children’s perception of risk is different. They seem to be more aware of those kinds of risk.

With the elderly and middle-aged demographics, it tends to come down to, “Do I need to evacuate? What do I need to do?” We remind them about flooding, and they have the ability to sign up for free flood alerts; generally, that is the engagement that we get from people. However, Covid and pandemics also come up among the public. Some people ask, “Will there be another pandemic? Will we have to have another lockdown?” Obviously, a lot of people experienced that in quite a traumatic way.

Other risks, such as animal health, do not come up so much. Obviously, we see the whole array of the risk register, but, in the public engagement with which I have been involved, it generally comes down to being able to evacuate safely and flooding.

The Chair: Professor Miles, do you want to say anything on that?

Professor Lee Miles: Basically, on your 21% figure, I would say that there is a difference between awareness and preparedness; that is an important point. The 21% may be a reflection of the fact that we are seeing greater awareness, which is partly due to the fact that we have greater perceived frequency of events; we also have better media awareness and coverage of those events and there are more clear, simplified instructions about where to go and where to look. In that sense, I am probably the opposite of you, in the sense that I would say that 21% is nowhere near where we need to be. We also have the dynamics of climate change, with more frequent weather events, which affect that aspect too. People may become aware and sign up, but the critical question is: what do they do about it? Preparedness is not necessarily at the same level, and we should not equate the 21% with activity across the piece.

The Chair: I am a child of the Cold War, so the sense of anticipation and preparedness is hard-wired into me. Do you see that in some of the work you are doing— that is, a clear stratification between age groups about the need to be prepared and what they might need to do for resilience?

Professor Lee Miles: There are a lot of competing variables, but the most important point is that it is partly about the individual vulnerability of your own household or community. If you looked within the data of that 21%, you would probably find quite a high number of areas that are already vulnerable to flooding. We have seen more movement in that space, which is good; that is important.

There is another aspect. I led the EM-PREPARE project, which was about empowering preparedness in everyday and household resilience. We found that 75% of households did not have any major measures to do with climate change at all. Something like 69% of UK households were willing to spend only up to one day on improving their preparedness.

That comes down to three things. First, with the greatest respect to the institution I am sitting in, we are living in an environment of lower trust in UK authorities. Citizens do not really trust institutions or advice in the way they used to.

The second point is that we have the economic position of our standards of living, where households and citizens are making choices; they are rational actors. Their vulnerability is related to how they perceive their level of threat or hazard, and how much of their own resources they are therefore willing to invest in it, because resilience also starts at home.

The final point is about new technologies and where people go for information. That goes back to your point about different groups using different sources of information. They may not necessarily use websites or LRFs; they may go to social media or even AI.

Q25 Baroness Tyler of Enfield: May I pursue your point about preparedness a bit further? Despite a growing feeling that there is more understanding than there was about the range of threats, there is still evidence that suggests that, as you said, many households are simply not prepared for emergencies. What do you think explains the public’s lack of engagement with preparedness? Do you think that it has anything to do, in terms of the wider geopolitical threats, with the fact that, as an island state, we are not seen as likely to be attacked?

Professor Lee Miles: Let me refer to the previous point. It is about the distance and difference between perception and real risk. For a long time, UK households and citizens did not necessarily perceive the threat of geopolitical threats; they were still there, but they were not perceived as having an impact at home. There was a distance in that, as compared to flooding or high temperatures, which were much closer to home. That is really important in how we look at this.

However, there has been a change, in the sense that, if you look at some of the data, we can perceive that geopolitical aspects are becoming more important in people’s minds. Let me give you some data. When we did the EM-PREPARE study in 2025, we found that the most common perception of risk in households was accidents at home. Only about 5% were concerned about conflict, and only 4% were concerned with terrorism. When we look at the national data, we see that that is now at about 30%, so we have started to see a movement and a linkage, but the linkages are not clear; the cascading relationships are not necessarily clear, either.

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: I agree. Other countries, such as New Zealand, often have earthquakes and tsunamis and so have higher risk profiles. They are, therefore, much more prepared. They are a great case study in terms of looking at their general levels of community resilience and their preparedness in how they work. We use them as case studies for good practice and share them through the resilience community. That is reflected across the world, such as in Japan, which has earthquake simulations because they are a real risk for people.

To go back to your comment about the older generation probably being more prepared because of the Cold War threats, remembering World War II rationing and fixing something when it breaks rather than just buying another thing, the mindset is different. There is a bit of a gap in the level of preparedness, which is why I welcome the “Be Ready” campaign to bring up our baseline as a country; to bring back that everyday discussion of resilience; and to get people prepared, so that it is normalised and not scary. Although the Cold War threat probably was quite scary back in the day, we now need to focus on a tangible, real threat: as we go into an El Niño winter, we are preparing for really bad storms and cold snaps, so let us be prepared as a country. Hopefully, that will resonate more with people.

Baroness Tyler of Enfield: I have a follow-up question. You mentioned the “Be Ready” campaign. Most people are now familiar with having three days-worth of baked beans, et cetera, but do you feel that that campaign, as it currently stands, is sufficient to prepare communities for an emergency? What next steps should the Government consider taking?

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: I am really happy about it for consistency. Three days-worth is probably a good place to start; obviously, this is very topical on social media at the moment. I have seen that, in other countries, they are saying to have seven or five days-worth. I feel that three days is a good place to start. That now gives all local resilience forums across the UK baseline standard messaging, whereas, before, it came down to capacity in local resilience forums. We are all oddly funded, and it is all a bit inconsistent, so it is really nice to bring back that consistency base level. It is a good place to start, but we could always do better; everything could always be better.

It is good timing, with the winter that we are about to go into. As I say, hopefully, that will resonate more with people. We had the storms at the weekend, and there were lots of people off power, so it is about those building blocks: having not just water and food but candles, torches, spare batteries and power banks in the house. It is about building that resilience conversation in a hopefully not-too-drastic way.

Professor Lee Miles: The obvious and fundamental point is that this is the start of a journey. I had the privilege of working in Sweden for 13 years, where a whole-of-society approach to resilience was being talked about in the 1990s. There, the links to civic society are much closer. It is partly about having a conversation around the things and items that you can immediately speak to citizens about, but it is really about mindsets. It is about developing the mindset that resilience is not just the responsibility of government or the private sector; it also involves citizens.

One of the striking features of the national survey that came out in August was that something like 82% of citizens thought that resilience was fully or entirely the responsibility of government. This means that you do not have a resilient population at all. They do not necessarily see resilience as part of their core business; that needs to be part of that mindset change.

Another point is that, in that survey, there are some very interesting data about where people are challenging the advice—particularly around the three litres of water, for example. Something like 82% of the people in the survey thought that three litres of water would probably not be enough for three days. It would probably last one day, actually, but at least they are getting into a mindset where they are challenging and understanding their own requirements; that takes us a little further down the road where we need to go.

Q26 Mr Tanmanjeet Singh Dhesi: Some of us were in the Chamber when the Security Minister made his Statement on the three-year “Be Ready” campaign, asking people to stock up fully for those three days. Professor Lee Miles: From an academic perspective, although I made the point that this is a start, it uses quite traditional mechanisms to connect with the population. Of course, the register for vulnerable people is an important element, but, as you rightly say, we are in a cost of living crisis.

When we did the EM-PREPARE study in 2025 at Bournemouth University, we found that there is a need to incentivise the population, as rational actors, because you are requiring them to make choices about what they are going to spend their own money on in relation to this. So there is a funding aspect in relation to how you are going to incentivise populations.

As one would expect as you have seen that in the news and in the media, there has been a pick-up, but my final observation is that that has been largely among parts of the population that already share the view that there is more to do on resilience. The real challenge is getting to vulnerable parts of the population, particularly where their standards of living are challenged, and that requires incentivisation. This may mean looking at the extent to which you can build resilience. For example, if people embark on resilience training or do more in their houses, to what extent might they be eligible for lower insurance premiums on their house contents insurance, so that they can share some of the costs? To what extent might you be able to use supermarkets, for example, to provide preparedness packs at discounted rates for parts of the population? Those are the things that will make the difference—where words become actions.

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: We always receive challenge, obviously, in being in a local resilience forum and working with all public sector partners. Mr Tanmanjeet Singh Dhesi: Coming back to you, Professor Miles, this is obviously a serious issue. Do you think that the Government are taking it seriously enough, or do you think that it is becoming a joke? What do you want to see happen around community engagement with the “Be Ready” campaign?

Professor Lee Miles: The important thing about the “Be Ready” campaign is turning it into a “Being Ready” campaign; that is the fundamental point. What I mean by that is that we are in a position where we are at the start of the journey: the Government have outlined the kinds of thing that citizens need to take into account in their own lifestyles. Of course, we also have the point about the frequency of events, which says that your resilience capacity could be wiped out very quickly by a series of events that take place over a summer—like the hot summer we just went through—or major flooding events, which are now happening with greater frequency. That is important.

The second element is linking all that up, as part of collaboration and connections. It is very important that citizens are linking their flood awareness and what is actually happening, weather-wise, with their level of resilience, because the two things are related at different points in time: you may need higher levels of readiness in your own house than before. Basically, we are saying that there needs to be a policy where we are making our own citizens the risk managers of their own households; that requires further information and more conversations to take place.

Becky Heginbotham-Blount: I would say that it is quite clear because local resilience forums have been around since 2004, and we have had a lot of floods, storms and other incidents—as well as Covid—where we have stood up our command-and-control co-ordination structures really effectively.
