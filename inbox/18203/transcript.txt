Examination of witness

Witness: Paula Sussex CBE, Public Service Ombudsman.

Q62 Chair: A very warm welcome to the third of our three sessions and the final session for the Child Maintenance Service inquiry. We are delighted to welcome Paula Sussex, who is the Public Service Ombudsman. I want to reflect on the complaints that you deal with in relation to the Child Maintenance Service and try to understand the patterns that you have observed and why they seem to differ in volume, in particular, from what the DWP is experiencing and reporting.

Paula Sussex: Thank you, Chair, and thank you, Committee. It is perhaps helpful to say at the start that the ombudsman comes right at the end of the process. By definition, a parent who is complaining will have been through two stages of the complaint process and the good work that our colleagues at ICE do before they come to us. None the less, as you say, Chair, it is remarkable that, although we observe that DWP says that CMS complaints have declined, ours have doubled over four years.

I am afraid I have no firm evidence as to what might be driving that, but we think that there is some element of complaints not being handled effectively, by definition, and there is that compound of, if you have been through two stages and you then go to ICE—the independent complaint examiner—if you are still not satisfied, you then come to us.

However, I should set this in the context that we are doing some wider research on the general increase in complaints across all public services. We are seeing something like a 20% to 25% increase in complaints coming to us. Our analysis is starting to show that there is generally—if I can describe it as this—greater persistence and greater distress, which is causing complainants to persist for longer. The third possible reason—which is, of course, another global reason—is the pressure of the cost of living on a lot of parents and families.

Q63 Chair: Would you say that the Child Maintenance Service is one of the services that you have the most complaints about?

Paula Sussex: No. As I am sure the Committee knows, we have a health and a government jurisdiction. Health is 80% of our work and government is only 20%. In terms of the government work, last year we received only 500 complaints, and that will be against a considerably larger number for the whole of our government work. However, CMS is about one third of the complaints that we receive about all DWP services.

Chair: That is very interesting.

Q64 Mr Peter Bedford: On those statistics, what themes are coming out in the complaints regarding the CMS?

Paula Sussex: We have recently written to the Department about a systemic investigation that I will come to in a minute. By way of making some overarching comments, as the Committee will know very well, CMS manages an unenviable combination of high-volume and highly complex processes, so it is fair to say that it is doing some very good work in the simple cases.

On the themes that we see, I would say that there are a raft of issues about better customer service. Something that we observe in other areas of DWP is a process that is designed and run around the customer rather than the policy or the regulations and, at the root of it, standing in your customer’s shoes. That is difficult to do if you are operating something that is—as I know from my own operational experience as an accounting officer. None the less, when you know that your customer base is having an extraordinarily tough time—they are not in the best state—you have to be really rigorous about how you communicate with your customers, how you keep them up to speed and how they are understanding the rules and regulations.

Q65 Mr Peter Bedford: Building on what you just said about the customer experience, why do you think there has been a fall in the number of complaints directly to the Department but there has been a significant rise in what you have received? I would surmise that that could be because there is a lack of confidence in the process in the Department to handle those complaints effectively. What is your view?

Paula Sussex: I will, of course, encourage you to ask the Department why the Department’s complaints are falling. I cannot speculate on its behalf, but we—I will turn to this at some point in this hearing—believe that there is work to be done on the Department’s complaint handling, so how it actually manages and resolves its complaints.

As for the disparity between us, I should also say that ICE is showing an increase. We and ICE show a broadly increasing trend, whereas CMS is showing a decreasing trend. That is not what we see across our health and government jurisdiction. Generally, when I am out with a mental health and community trust, as I was yesterday, those, too, are seeing a 20% to 30% increase in complaints. I would therefore speculate that there is something about the ease with which customers can complain.

Q66 Mr Peter Bedford: What impact would removing the MP filter have in cases involving the CMS?

Paula Sussex: Generally, as the Committee knows, we are supportive of the MP filter being removed in our government jurisdiction, although we always welcome the opportunity for complainants to work with their MPs. In this particular service, we see a higher number of complaints that have an MP actively engaged and involved, which we welcome. I am sure that my team sitting behind me also work quite closely with caseworkers from a number of your constituencies, as we work together. In this particular service, that might not have a huge impact but generally we would welcome it.

Q67 Mr Peter Bedford: Finally, I think you mentioned that a third of your caseload is from the CMS—or a third of the 20% that you deal with.

Paula Sussex: A third of the DWP of the 20%—and DWP is about probably 20% of government complaints.

Q68 Mr Peter Bedford: You obviously see a lot of different organisations. Which of those could the CMS learn from for their own processes?

Paula Sussex: First, although the CMS and the Department have not brought this to us, we have identified in our caseworker-to-caseworker interactions that the service modernisation programme—I am sure the Committee is aware of what it intends to cover; I think it is a multi-year programme. Having got an outline of that—again, if I go back through my own operational experience—if the Department executes on that well, if it is a funded programme and if it executes to the outcomes that we have been indicated that it will achieve, it will be in a better place.

On learning from other organisations, we see some organisations that manage communications of complex financial affairs—and these are complex. Lives are complex. Regulations are complex, sometimes unnecessarily so. We see other organisations that can handle communicating that complexity better. We certainly point to HMRC and student loans, which, I should confess, I ran for some years, manages to reflect the situation—not flawlessly but they will give a repaying individual a better breakdown of where they are in their process and their account.

Q69 Patrick Hurley: Still on complexity, you said in your evidence that the CMS performs less effectively perhaps than other organisations or than it could in complex cases. Do you think it is inherent in the work of CMS that sometimes there are just issues relating to families that cause that complexity, or is this perhaps an issue around competence or a training need for DWP?

Paula Sussex: First, I think we said in the evidence that we have identified that simple cases are dealt with better than complex cases—so perhaps more in the absolute. Again, if you go back to the perfect storm of there being a high volume—I think CMS has about 800,000, I am going to say, customers, over presumably an 18 or 19-year time period, and highly complex rules and regs that change. That is a very difficult state of affairs. Where other organisations have taken different operational decisions—for example, having a specialist or complex caseworking team—that sometimes works. We see that in HMCTS probate. However, the key thing is how you communicate with the customer as you are going through a complex set of circumstances.

To turn to, I suppose, how it feels, we the ombudsman work from the complainants’ evidence in the first instance, so we are in the privileged position of standing initially in their shoes. We hold neither for the complainant nor the organisation, as you know, because we are impartial. A very typical expression of sometimes how it feels is confusing, protracted and without support, and that is particularly so in the complex cases.

Q70 Patrick Hurley: Do you think that would be addressed by bringing in a complex case unit?

Paula Sussex: It could be. On operational flow and backlog, there are arguments for and against that, and I am sure that the CMS team has looked at it. I would probably go more to the service modernisation programme. We would very much welcome stronger engagement to understand that—that reading between the lines and getting that in are the sorts of things that we have identified would help in our cases.

Q71 John Milne: Good morning. I want to talk about how the CMS communicates with parents, and that is done in two ways. One is about basic admin. Do they answer the phone? Do they reply to an email? And deeper than that basic admin level, are their communications, letters and so on comprehensible? Do they get the information across?

Paula Sussex: I will go to the second part first if I may. Again, we find with DWP in different areas, in pensions as well—this is well on the public record—that if you look hard at the DWP service charter, you will sometimes look at the communications and say, “This isn’t measuring up well,” in terms of how clear it is, but also checking on how well it is understood.

I took the opportunity to look at DWP’s 2025-26 customer experience survey, and you can probably see that echoed in the feedback on that. We sometimes find that the communications are too boilerplate, too macro. There need to be regular updates to the customer: “What is happening with my case? Why?” And to give it a very grand word, there needs to be procedural justice: “Why have you made this calculation this way? What caused you to do this?” There is certainly work to be done there. Again, if I go back to the programme, DWP reflects on that and says that it will get better at that.

On the first part—on keeping customers up to date and up to speed—we are also not blameless in that respect, and we are working hard on it. None of us do this well enough. We and the DWP are creating an online account, which helps a bit because you have a dedicated space where you will get more of an update of how your case is progressing and why. There is certainly more work to be done there.

One thing that we have picked up anecdotally is that some of the communications assume that a paying parent intends to be delinquent from the off and that they are sometimes rather harsh from the start—they assume. Whereas, and I am sure the Department knows this, many paying parents actually want to be doing the right thing by their children.

Q72 John Milne: Yes, I have heard exactly that from some of the cases in my constituency. I am sure everyone else has, too. You have done a lot of work on the WASPI issue, which was very much about a failure to communicate. Are there any lessons to be applied from that?

Paula Sussex: Yes—I would probably go back to standing in the shoes of your customer. How could this individual receive this communication? Is it coming through the right channel at the right time? Is it being delivered empathetically? I note that they are investing in trauma-informed services, so will they be able to receive it? How do we know they have received it? What can we expect? What is a reasonable expectation of that communication?

This is about the very definition. We are talking about a receiver of communication, a customer who is at a heightened state—they are not at their best—so the effort to communicate really well and check that it has been understood needs to be more than your average day-to-day communication.

Q73 John Milne: You previously told us about a case where a parent was sent a letter threatening legal action, but it was based on incorrect information. What checks and balances can be put into the system so that it does not leap to a draconian or tough demand?

Paula Sussex: Realistically, on that volume of service and complexity, the IT was inevitably not designed for the complexity that I suspect successive years of policy regs have put in. Sometimes that is unavoidable. The important thing is that all organisations respond quickly with an apology and what they will do to fix it. Yes, you can put in various checks and balances, but we accept, of course, that mistakes will be made. It is about how you respond, show contrition—I may come later to the complaints handling—and consider the impact on the customer, not what went wrong in the process. Where you can redress, can you redress? What was the impact of this? We do not see enough focus on the complainants in the complaint process.

Q74 John Milne: I have a constituent who went through a two-year process with a mandatory review of his case. He was not told at the start that the tribunal had no power to change the way the calculation was done, so it was two years for absolutely nothing. It is bad enough that the system is like that, but not being told in the first place is even worse.

Paula Sussex: Yes.

Q75 Damien Egan: I will ask a bit more about the service modernisation programme, because there have been criticism and comments about its limited functionality and issues about uploading documents. That is the feedback I have had from constituents who have come to my surgeries feeling very distressed about it. How is the service modernisation programme progressing? How confident are you that we can get to a service that truly matches the expectations that people have? More critically, what do you need to get there?

Paula Sussex: If I can go to that last question first, we would welcome the Committee’s support in encouraging CMS. It is not that it has said no, it is not going to share the service modernisation programme, but we would find it helpful if we could get closer to the programme and its aims to follow through on its delivery.

We have heard anecdotally that the portal is not working perfectly, which I think is what you are referring to. That is not infrequently the case. This is government IT. It is not going to be flawless, as I am sure the Committee has heard before. We would happily come back or submit something to the Committee once we have a better understanding of “What is the programme? What is the process? How can you keep us up to speed with how it is going and how it is addressing things?”

The Committee will be aware that we are working much more closely with the pension side of the Department on the action plan. I am not suggesting, nor do we have the resource, that we work as closely with the Department as we are on the action plan, but I think there could be something in between so we can see what it is trying to do. That will help to give an assessment of individual redress and also of how we can support the Committee for you to oversee the success of the Department’s service. This is particularly key if the move to collect and pay kicks in at some point, because that will be a major programme for the Department, a major disruption.

Chair: Thank you. That is very useful.

Q76 Steve Darling: Thank you for coming and giving evidence. I see from your previous employment that you have been a merchant for change and transformation, and a big part of taking people on the journey is trust and ensuring that they continue to trust in the system. How do you see your role of ensuring that there is greater development of trust? Are you able to pull from stuff in your previous life or, equally, can you look at how other large organisations have developed trust with users?

Paula Sussex: That is an enormously good question and one that exercises me considerably. The backdrop to this societally—as I am sure you and the Committee are very well aware—is that trust in Government and national public services has decreased worryingly over the last few years. In fact, we surveyed this in August. We have not yet published the analysis, but 51% said that, over recent years, their trust in national Government services had declined. That is a fairly serious backdrop to be operating in.

We see no evidence that endemically customers do not trust CMS. They do not trust it for its competence when things are going wrong and things aren’t being explained. They then lose confidence and, therefore, you can say they lose trust. None the less, closer engagement—it would be interesting to hear from the Department how it does this—with its users in the way that it is working to modernise the service is the first thing that I would do in terms of building more trust in the overall organisation.

Q77 Chair: We have come to the end of our formal questions, but would you like to add to the evidence that you provided to the Committee specifically on the CMS? We were very concerned about the level of complaints, particularly given, as you rightly said, that this follows on from ICE involvement. The National Audit Office was similarly very scathing about this.

As you rightly said, this is a very emotional time for families, and I really do reflect on what you say about treating people as you are,and living in their shoes. All too often, that does not happen, and all of us need to do that as public servants—I am sure we all do with our constituents. Is there anything specifically that you would like to add to the recommendations that you have made about complaints or whatever?

Paula Sussex: Building on the point that there is a considerable increase in complaints, we cannot see a key driver, but we see a lot of people coming to us in heightened distress, challenging situations, and so on. We have opened a systemic investigation, which is about the collection of arrears, but it is not a dominant theme.

The key thing that I have not really shared with the Committee probably lies in complaints handling. They have a two-stage process, which is not uncommon, but it only works well if the first stage is about review and exchange—an informal but productive and effective stage. If that is not the case—we have seen evidence that the word “dissatisfaction” is used, which I am sure some of your constituents think means a complaint has been registered and then it hasn’t and you bounce around the system.

We think it is about really leaning into that complaints process and working on and investing in it to resolve complaints. We know that caseworkers will say, “We don’t get consistent communication or timelines from the complaints function”. I am sorry that we do not have a clear answer of what is driving that increase, except to say that we should have a look at the complaints function and how they are handling it. That is the only thing that I can logically point to.

Q78 John Milne: There are problems with all Departments, and DWP in general. But anecdotally, I think most MPs would agree that we get more issues with this Department than any other. Whether that is a fair representation or not, it involves some of the most distressing cases. They are particularly distressing because in many cases it can result in a parent being separated from a child for a very long time while some appalling bureaucratic process is going on. A year separated from a child is an infinity. It is so damaging. That time cannot be made up. We did feel last time we met the CMS that it did not fully appreciate the scale of the challenge. Do you think that the CMS does? It needs to up its game because the human consequences are particularly egregious.

Paula Sussex: It is difficult. With this particular part of DWP, we work at the operational level, and it is always important to say that there are a lot of great frontline workers working hard in both our jurisdictions. In this area, we do not engage with any senior levels, so it is difficult for me to say whether institutionally it grasps this and is putting the resource into it.

I would go back to what I said about focusing on the life of your complainant. I was told anecdotally—not by somebody from the Department—that about 15 years ago when the IT was being designed, they thought that the maximum number of change of circumstances that anybody would have would be four. That is probably not borne out in practice. If systems and regulation are designed around a set of lives that don’t exist, it makes it extremely hard.

The only thing that we have seen happening in our health jurisdiction, where your IT or the regulations mean that the situation is very complex and you cannot really speed it up, is the concept of “waiting well”—in health, they have backlogs in ADHD assessments. Waiting well is about ensure that they have a way of making contact with parents or patients and helping them cope as they go through, and possibly through some of their trauma-informed work. Some of this cannot be avoided, but you can certainly make it a better experience in the year that you mentioned, which is very painful, probably for both parents.

Q79 Chair: On complaints handling, would you be able to give relative weighting to what you are saying about the administrative issues, consistency and record keeping compared with what you are saying about the cultural issues within the service?

Paula Sussex: That is tricky, but I would probably lean towards the complaints handling. I would make the offer to CMS to put—we have been running some training across DWP, but perhaps CMS may find it helpful to lean in more to the work that is being done on the complaints handling function.

Q80 Chair: In relation to earlier questions on safeguarding across the service, we were very surprised to hear that it was not automatically flagged when there might be issues around domestic abuse, which certainly has come up in our roundtables with parents. Is that something that you highlight with complaints that have been brought to you?

Paula Sussex: We have not seen that specifically as a clear theme. However, if you and the Committee have seen that, it is probably consistent with the way in which they risk-assess their cases and, as cases come through, it is important to be able to identify where there are compounded complexities. If a case is not removed from what is sometimes referred to as—inappropriately in this circumstance—the “happy path” of straightforward processing, those complexities can be overlooked. We can certainly see elements of that here. Accordingly, if cases are not being assessed for risks of harm or other safeguarding concerns, we would recommend that the CMS addresses that. However, I do not know whether that is the case or not.

Chair: We have concluded our questions for today. Paula, thank you so much for joining us; We are very grateful.

Paula Sussex: Thank you.

Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Baroness Sherlock OBE, Simon Hunter, Duncan Gilchrist and Lucy Appleton.

Q81 Chair: Welcome to the second panel of this third and final evidence session on the Child Maintenance Service. It is a pleasure to be joined by the DWP Minister, Maeve Sherlock. Baroness Sherlock, thank you very much for joining us. You have brought your team as well: Simon Hunter, director of the CMS; Duncan Gilchrist, the deputy director responsible for policy; and then Lucy Appleton, the deputy director responsible for enforcement. Have I got that right, and would you like to make any opening remarks?

Baroness Sherlock: No, Chair. We are happy to answer any questions you have for us.

Q82 Chair: Thank you; that is very kind. I do not know if you managed to catch any of the evidence that the PHSO was able to provide to us specifically around complaints.

Baroness Sherlock: We got the heads up that there was evidence about complaints, but we were outside reading our papers nervously and encouraging each other, so I am afraid we did not get to hear the evidence.

Q83 Chair: All right. I will quickly say this before I ask my substantive question. There were concerns that, contrary to DWP data, the PHSO complaints are increasing. I think they have doubled over the last five years. There were concerns that this is contrary to DWP data that they were actually reducing.

The comments were in relation to both administrative issues around complaints handling, particularly around complex cases, but also in terms of cultural issues. Assumptions were made about paying parents, for example. The phrase that sticks in my mind—because I think it is a really good one—is about service users being treated with respect and for the complaints handler to live in those people’s shoes, to understand where they are coming from in their dealings with them. Do you want to reflect briefly on that before we go into questions?

Baroness Sherlock: I am very happy to do that, and I might ask Simon to say a word. If two sets of officials disagree on stats, I think I may let them fight it out, if you don’t mind.

There are a couple of things that are worth saying about complaints. The volume of the CMS caseload has risen quite significantly. Since 2019-20, the number of cases has gone up by about 64%. While the number of complaints is going up, relative to the caseload, that is not the case in the complaints that we see overall. Having said that, I am not being complacent. We take all complaints very seriously, and CMS regards complaints as a source of intelligence and feedback to make sure that we can learn about what happens and how we can make things better.

Two things are worth noting. On complaints about the CMS, of course, the CMS—unlike most Government services—has two customers. Even if one customer is very satisfied at the result of an action by CMS, it is entirely possible that that will leave the other customer unhappy with what is there. Secondly, I take the point you make about treating customers with respect extremely seriously. I am grateful to you for putting it in that context.

Simon, without delay, could you just give two minutes on how you take that seriously and what you do to make sure that our caseworkers listen to customers, understand their context and their lives, and respond appropriately?

Chair: Two minutes, if that is all right.

Simon Hunter: Absolutely. We kicked off a big piece of work in December last year that looked at quality as a whole. Certainly, some of the feedback that you gave us today around treating the case with respect and understanding the scenario—as the Minister said, all that information is rich information for us. We are doing a substantial piece of work around that. That also helps us in the telephony work that we do.

This is a body of work that will take time to embed in. The feedback is really important to us. Again, it is important to stress that we have very good relationships with ICE and the PHSO. The feedback that we get is used to see how we can improve what we are doing generally. As the Minister said, feedback is really important to us. We take it incredibly seriously and we work with that to see where we can be better.

Q84 Chair: Thanks very much. On perceptions of fairness—we touched on those just now—what work would you like to highlight in terms of how you are increasing confidence so that it is not just perceptions of fairness but customers experiencing actual fairness?

Baroness Sherlock: That is an excellent place to start. First, customers’ perception of fairness and trust in the CMS will be driven by their experience of using it. The job of the organisation is to make sure that the experience is one that can inspire a sense of confidence, fairness, understanding and responsiveness, so that it is not just an administrative service.

It is a challenging environment, as you know only too well. Often trust has broken down between the two parents before they ever get to use the CMS. It is therefore important that the CMS follows the rules consistently, because the two customers will have different expectations and different desires from the process, and also does it in a way that feels appropriate and responsive.

One thing we have been setting out to do is to look at how to make it really clear what is going on at every stage. If you come to the service, if you are a paying parent, how do we make it clear that all the information you have given us has been taken into account? How can you see clearly on your customer account or when the caseworker talks to you why you owe what you owe, where it came from and what you need to do as a result?

If you are a receiving parent and you are expecting money, when should you expect it? What do you do if it does not turn up? Who do you tell? How do we do that? A lot of work has gone into communication, into both the content and the expression, the formatting of information for customers, and also trying to drive some reform about making sure that we get better.

In terms of compliance, which I am sure we will come on to, performance is better than it ever has been in a difficult environment. However, the most important thing is that it this not just about that. Our job is to get money to children—in the end, the CMS is about making sure that children get the maintenance that they are entitled to—but to do it in a way that both parents understand and feel heard and seen in that process while we employ the law consistently. That is what we are setting out to do, and that has been the purpose of the reforms.

Q85 Chair: Thinking about fairness and the trust that builds or does not build, do you think there is an acceptable level of trust among customers of the service?

Baroness Sherlock: I think it is always going to be challenging, partly for the reason, as I said, that there are two customers. I have seen cases where the CMS did all the right things. I have listened to calls. I listen into them, but not to as many as Simon, who listens to a vast number of calls.

I have come to the conclusion that our job is to try to make the system as fair as it can be while recognising that child maintenance is a zero-sum game. If one parent pays more, the other one is getting more. If one parent wants more, the other one is getting less. We have to apply the law fairly. The key is to try to make sure our caseworkers are trained to understand the context in which parents are working, and a lot of work has gone into that.

A lot of work has gone into quality, assurance, customer service, consistent training, and having a programme where the managers listen over and over again to calls, where team leaders work with customers to make sure that not only are they doing the right thing, which should be taken into consideration, but that parents can understand that and that they can be appropriately sympathetic or empathetic to the circumstances while not trying to say—because you cannot tell both parents they are right if they want different things.

The final bit relating to the real source of trust is that CMS gets things wrong—I have seen a few cases where it got things wrong. With 820,000 cases a year, it is going to get things wrong. The most important thing is that when it does, it puts its hands up, says it has got it wrong and works out why it has got it wrong. It then puts it right and works out how it will make sure the system is improved for next time.

One of the things I have been really impressed with is that this is a learning organisation. I knew some of this world from the outside. It is only when you become a Minister that you get to be a bit closer up and to peer into the darker corners. This is a very learning organisation. I find that it will absolutely say, “We messed this up. This is what we have learned from that. This is how the procedures are going on”. We have been working very hard together on continuous improvement to try to get constantly better, because I think that is the way to improve trust from customers.

Q86 Liz Twist: As I understand it, some major changes are coming up. How will the Department make sure that when cases on the direct pay service move over to the single-tier system, based on collect and pay, it is done safely, without escalating the conflict between parents?

Baroness Sherlock: That is a great question. We have come to the conclusion that this is the right thing to do. At the moment with direct pay, many of the challenges we see—and some of the complaints that you as MPs see—are with indirect pay. CMS gets the information, works out what has to happen, and parent one pays parent two. The problem is if they don’t pay. We found out from research that, quite often, they do not pay either what they should or on time. We don’t know unless one or other parent tells us, so it can go quite some time until they come into the collect and pay service, by which time arrears have built up and things have gone badly wrong.

In the transition, what we will do is, first, make sure that customers get plenty of notice. The applicant, the person who applied to the CMS, will be the one we will go to first. They will be given three months’ notice. We will give really clear communications. We will help them to work out what they want to do. Are the circumstances between the parents such that they could actually make an arrangement on their own—what we call a family-based arrangement? Direct pay will have lots of parents who can get on perfectly well and do it. We could help them work out what is to be paid, give them support in making that decision, in making that arrangement, and then go off and do it.

If that isn’t going to work, they need to come into the service. Therefore, we are looking really carefully at how we transition them. We are working through different ways of doing that. How do we move cases over together? Do we consider pathfinders? We are working through all that. The key will be to do advanced communication, supporting parents who want to consider a family-based arrangement, safeguards for vulnerable customers, making sure the system is ready, not rushing into it until we know the system works, and then monitoring the effects on compliance, on customers, and particularly on any vulnerable parents. We are taking that very seriously.

Q87 Liz Twist: Two things come out of that. One is the communication that we have talked about in more general terms—you said you are looking at that carefully.

Baroness Sherlock: Very carefully.

Liz Twist: The second thing is this: are pilots going on to see how this might work or are there detailed plans of implementation?

Baroness Sherlock: We are working through whether we might do pathfinders about the process. Pilots are always quite complicated in CMS because of the way the statutory service works. However, we are looking at whether or not a pathfinder is the best way to do it. We will not move all customers over on the same day, because that just isn’t how the system works, so we are looking at what is the best way to test and to learn the best way to work that out.

Q105 Mr Peter Bedford: Turning to affordability, in previous sessions we have heard from the Department that the child maintenance calculation is designed to be affordable for paying parents, but given the history of that calculation, in terms of the 1998 thresholds, using gross income, arguably it hits those on low earnings disproportionately and often makes it unaffordable for them to pay. Could you give me your thoughts on that and why the Department is sticking by its position on this?

Baroness Sherlock: There are a couple of things embedded in that. First, let me deal with the gross income point because this comes up quite often.

There are two ways to do this. You can assess on gross income or you can assess on net income and you simply would set the rates to take account of that. I think that is often not understood. A previous system had used net income and then you would set the rates to take account of the fact that obviously it is net income. The big problem if you go for net income, as previous Administrations found, is that it just caused lots of instability and uncertainty and movement around rates, whereas gross taxable income is a clear and unambiguous basis on which to make a judgment.

On affordability, one of the benefits of the system is that the key—the biggest driver—for most paying parents is simply that you pay a percentage of your income. If your income goes down, so, too, does the percentage, and if your income goes up, so, too, does the percentage.

The reason behind that is that the whole point of CMS is that it is there to get money to kids or, crucially, to make sure that the children continue to benefit from the income of both parents even if the parents have chosen to live apart. Obviously, if they were still both living with their child and their income went up, the child would benefit from the income of both parents or, again, if they were living together and the income went down, the child would have to bear the consequences of their household income going down. Broadly speaking, it tracks that.

You make an assessment once a year and it is reviewed after a year, and in that year, the assessment will only change if the paying parent’s income has moved up or down by more than 25%. I felt when we reviewed the calculation that that was too much—that your income could rise or, say, fall by 24% and your child maintenance would not change at all until your next annual review. I have decided that I am going to reduce that to 15%. I am trying to find a sweet spot. If it is too low, even small changes in income mean that the receiving parent could find their payments moving around too quickly, but if it is too big, it is too tough on the paying parent if the income drops down. I think 15% is a good sweet spot so I made a decision to do that. I think that will help with affordability in those cases where people’s income falls. Does that help?

Q106 Mr Peter Bedford: You mentioned that the child should benefit from the income of both parents, but that has not been incorporated in the affordability calculation. Baroness Sherlock: I might ask Duncan to respond because he has done a lot of thinking about this. When we were doing the reviewing of the calculation, we did look at this. Duncan Gilchrist: Yes. Effectively, the fundamental point of the CMS—the reason we brought it in in place of the courts—was because if you are going to handle a very big caseload, you need to smooth out some of the differences; you need to pick and choose the changes that you are going to action. You are always making a balance in the regulatory framework around it, trying to get the right changes that will make the most difference to most people while not doing so many changes that will mean that the cases then become aged and you fall into the trap that the CSA did when it was first launched, which is that you cannot keep up with the workload and you end up in a situation where lots of people get no maintenance because you are not able to work the cases properly.

Q107 Mr Peter Bedford: That sounds like more of a Department problem, in that you are looking at what your capacity is rather than what the problem is.

Duncan Gilchrist: I think it is a societal problem. I mean that there is no society that I am aware of that operates a complete court-based system that looks at every case where they are getting particularly good results. In 2022, the Irish did a study of their system, which is the court-based system that we had prior to bringing in the CSA first. The majority report that came out of that review was that in order to increase the number of people getting maintenance, they needed to move to a more agency-based approach. Yes, you could pour as many people as you wanted to into the CSA and they could deal with whatever changes you wanted, but in a real world, where we have the Government having to make sensible prioritisation decisions, you cannot have a system that is as sensitive as the courts are to every single change.

Baroness Sherlock: Can I just clarify two things? I think there are two questions here about the principle of how it is structured and the operation of it.

On the principle, what I was trying to explain—probably not very well—is that the CSA was set up to do one job, and that job is to make sure that the child benefits from the incomes of both parents. By definition, the child is already benefiting from the income of the parent with whom the child is living. The parent is feeding, housing and clothing the child—providing for the child. The point of the Child Maintenance Service is that it is not primarily there to level things between the parents. The job it was given is to make sure the child benefits from both parents, based on an assumption that both parents continue to retain responsibility for the children even if they have separated and their relationship has come to an end. The job of the CMS is specifically to add into that household an appropriate amount of money from the parent who is not the primary carer of the child in order that the child benefits from both parents’ incomes.

In my experience, for a lot of families, even if—and it will be unusual—you had an extremely wealthy receiving parent and sometimes a much less wealthy paying parent, I have talked to plenty of people who would say, “I want to contribute to my child”. Baroness Sherlock: I read the complaints that come in from MPs, but I just wanted to say two quick things. One is that the cases that you see are the most complex, the most difficult. You know this from elsewhere in your postbags. The overwhelming number of cases work straightforwardly.

Duncan Gilchrist: Shall I—

Duncan Gilchrist: There are a number of reasons why it was cancelled, but perhaps we can look at low-income paying parents in particular. We asked low-income paying parents and receiving parents from relatively low incomes how they would feel about a change that, broadly speaking, brought the calculation up to date—in other words, pushed up the threshold for the flat rate and therefore the other thresholds, which would then reduce the amount that paying parents on low incomes would pay and reduce the amount that receiving parents on low incomes would receive. The feedback we got—that research has been published if the Committee wants to go over it; I will not give you the figures now—from paying parents was that a large number felt that this reduction would not really make much difference to them. Q109 Mr Peter Bedford: That was research prior to the consultation, so that was almost like a pre-consultation consultation, based on some research.

Baroness Sherlock: That was a ministerial decision, so let me take responsibility for that. We deliberately set out to get evidence because we wanted to understand the impact on paying parents and receiving parents of any changes to the system. As I said, because it is a zero-sum game every single change we make will have an effect right across the piece. If one paying parent pays less, out there somewhere is a receiving parent who is getting less money in to pay for the child. Conversely, if a receiving parent wants more maintenance, which they generally do, that means that out there a paying parent will have to pay more.

We looked across the piece at the impact of that. We also looked at the way the system works at the moment. We looked at administrative evidence. We did evidence reviews. We talked to stakeholders, and then in the round we made a decision. Was this the moment to make wholesale changes to the system in the middle of a cost of living pressure time? We did not feel the evidence for that was there. Our driver was: will this improve compliance, improve fairness and get in more money to children? They were the criteria by which I judged it. Would it do those things? The evidence did not suggest that wholesale change to the actual calculation would do those things.

Where the evidence was clear, we followed it, which is why, as I said, I have decided to reduce the income threshold from 25% down to 15%, which is something we have often been asked for. I have also often been asked why, if somebody has income that is not wages, you cannot take account of that. The aim of those two things together is that the calculation will more accurately reflect the genuine income that is being assessed—both its current level and its combination of sources. That is what we set out to do. We went where the evidence led and that is where the evidence led, which is why I made the decision.
