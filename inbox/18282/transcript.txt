Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Committee. Today we are holding a follow-up session on our inquiry into British film and high-end TV, which we published in April 2025. On our first panel today, we have Pete Johnson, the chief executive of the British Screen Forum, Claire Mundell, the managing director of Synchronicity Films, and Harriet Finney, the deputy chief executive officer of the British Film Institute. You are all very welcome. Thank you so much for joining us today, and for your patience; we had to postpone this meeting from September, so we have been on tenterhooks waiting to speak to you. Before we begin, I remind Members to declare any interests at the point that you ask questions.

I will start with you, Harriet. How would you describe the current state of the UK film and high-end TV sector? Where are we, and how does it compare with where we were when the Committee first published our report in April 2025?

Harriet Finney: Thank you, Dame Caroline, and everybody here today. I want to thank you very much for the work of the Committee and the recommendations that you published in April 2025. A number of those have been taken forward, which is fantastic. Thank you for your support, particularly around the independent film tax relief, which has come in, and the global screen fund. The amount that we now distribute to the sector from that has doubled. You also made recommendations for the BFI, and a number of those have been taken forward as well.

The timing of your Committee meeting today is particularly prescient. I am not sure whether you have yet had an opportunity to look at the third quarter statistics that were published yesterday—box office and production statistics. The good news is around box office. We have seen an extraordinary box office recovery in the UK over the past 12 months. Admissions are at around 100 million. That is back up to the level pre-covid, which is fantastic. It is fair to say that that was very much driven by two films: “The Odyssey” and “Spiderman: Brand New Day”. August saw the highest admissions in cinemas that we have ever recorded. The challenge continues to be in something identified by the Committee the last time around: distribution. The independent film tax relief is fantastic, but UK films are not cutting through at the box office. That is something that I am sure we will pick up today.

On production, the UK has an extraordinary production landscape. We are a global hub for filmmaking, but in the recent statistics we see a dip in production in the UK. The context is a global reduction in commissioning. Hollywood is commissioning less film and high-end television. The key thing is that the UK’s market share remains robust and high, but we need to think about the competitive environment around us. Other territories definitely look to the UK as an absolute model of how the production landscape can be shaped around skills, workforce, talent and, importantly, tax incentives. Whether it is in Ireland, our neighbouring territories in Europe, or Australia, we are definitely seeing a much more competitive environment. Looking ahead, potentially, there is the introduction of the US federal incentive. It is important to keep that in mind, but the stats continue to show that the domestic production market is still under pressure. We will probably talk about some of the measures we would like to see on domestic.

Claire Mundell: Just to back up what Harriet said, the critical thing remains the financing of productions and how they are put together, but I think we will come on to that later.

Pete Johnson: I would just add the broader context that, whatever the headwinds at the moment—and they are significant, as Harriet said, with increasing international competition for highly mobile production activity—the long-term forecasts for audiovisual consumption and the production of audiovisual content are still very high. Globally, we are talking about a pie that will grow. We just need to double down on our efforts to make sure that our share of it grows as well. Lots of other countries have increased their production spend. I highlight Spain, which has done so by 45% between ’22 and ’25, because they took a whole-government approach and said, “We are going to have a Brexit bonus and it will attract more inward investment in audiovisual.” They doubled down on that. We have to look continually at our competitiveness as a country. We are really good at this stuff, and Claire is an example of that. We need to make sure that we give people like Claire the tools, incentives and support that they need to continue to deliver for the UK.

Chair: Briefly, did you feel that any of the recommendations in our report that were not accepted by the Government would have had a big impact?

Pete Johnson: Personally, I think the R&D tax relief would have a significant impact. It is the bit of the production process that companies need to finance themselves, and where they take all the risk. A lot of companies struggle to raise finance anyway—particularly risk finance—and that would have been extremely helpful. Other countries allow creative endeavours to be included within their R&D tax reliefs. The Frascati definition, which is the international definition, allows it. The UK has always chosen not to. I have never quite understood why.

Gill German: We know that at the moment the Government are consulting on national lottery funding, and that the BFI is at the moment the distributor for the screen industry. I think it is fair to say that a broad benefit comes from that, which the market perhaps would not be able to provide, with things like support for young film makers. A BFI academy runs in my area and is a real asset. What could be at risk if the Government changed that model for allocation and distribution, and what part of the screen sector depends most heavily on national lottery funding?

Harriet Finney: We very much welcome the call for evidence from Government. Actually, the lottery has not been reviewed in the 30 years that it has been up and running, so it was a really good opportunity for the BFI and the sector to tell the story of the impact of the national lottery. It touches every age and stage of anybody engaging with the film industry. In terms of the areas that we feel would be the greatest loss, there are three principal things that the lottery has done incredibly successfully. One is reflecting UK lives on the screen. Obviously, we see a lot of huge blockbuster content, but national lottery and other public funding allows for small stories that would otherwise never make their way to the big screen. Just this year we have had “Rose of Nevada”, about a small Cornish fishing community, “The Incomer”, set on a remote Scottish island, and “I See Buildings Fall Like Lightning”, a story of inner-city Birmingham young people. The only way those stories will make it to the screen and allow those people to see their stories told is through the piece that national lottery funding can play.

Also, there is a huge role in talent development. You mentioned the BFI Film Academy. We have a number of talent development programmes. They can be behind the screen, but the films that we support are often fundamental in the development of British on-screen talent. You have seen Olivia Colman’s first big-screen piece, “Tyrannosaur”, which took her away from sitcoms; you have seen Josh O’Connor in “God’s Own Country” and Jessie Buckley in “Beast”. For all of them, that was their first big-screen moment and they have obviously gone on to do great things. Behind the screen is really important as well, with the skills that people learn through, say, the film academies, clusters, etc. That piece is vital and no one else is providing in that space.

Lastly, our audience funding is really significant. It reaches 2,000 cinemas, festivals and communities across the UK, providing rich and diverse programming. Our Escapes cinema programme had 220,000 free tickets, which went out to people who normally would not necessarily engage with their local cinema. It gave them the opportunity to do that. This is about audiences, talent, and British stories on screen.

Gill German: So a whole package of things is included. “Rose of Nevada” was the last film I saw at the BFI, down the road. It is an extraordinary piece, which, as you say, perhaps would not make it to cinemagoers otherwise.

Harriet Finney: Yes. On the lottery call for evidence, obviously in the process the DCMS is thinking about the policy recommendations that will come out of it. A key part of it is for us to work better with other lottery distributors, and in communities with local government and local decision makers. It is really important. I think it came up at the Committee last time that we have 2.7% of the share, or £50 million. We can meet only 10% of the applications that we receive. In some programmes it is only 3%, so we are leaving a vast number of fantastic projects unfunded at the moment.

Gill German: Thank you. Just to Claire and Pete, briefly: what kinds of film and television projects are currently most difficult to finance, and what might prevent them from attracting investment, beyond the public funding model?

Claire Mundell: Very specific British cultural stories are the hardest to fund at the moment. Typically, they are not supported by the market to the same extent as other projects. As a Scottish woman, I know that is very much the case with Scottish projects, and anything with a regional accent. Those projects end up heavily dependent on low budgets. On a low budget it is difficult to achieve the required level of quality, but we have a skills base that punches above its weight.

The crucial thing to understand is that independent producers are integral to the support of the PSB system, because we raise third-party finance and deficit finance to supplement the funding that comes from broadcasters. I am sure everyone knows that the broadcaster does not fully fund any project. Taking those specific lower-budget projects, in the £1 million to £3 million budget range, to market is incredibly difficult. In that market, money just does not exist for some projects. That is where we really depend on the BBC and UK public service broadcasters to support those sorts of stories. At the moment, those are the hardest to fund. The section in the middle has pretty much disappeared, so we either make low-budget projects or higher-budget projects supported by the streamers.

Gill German: And, of course, that talent pipeline that you mentioned earlier, because so many people come through those smaller-budget films—

Claire Mundell: In general at the moment, there are fewer opportunities across the piece. There are higher costs and stagnant or reducing commissioning tariffs, and that is leading to fewer opportunities for future talent to come through the system, and a loss of diverse British cultural stories.

Pete Johnson: Just to build on that, I highlight that independent UK film in particular has been challenged for a long time. It is very difficult to get risk capital into film projects and film companies. That is something that we have been concerned about for a long time. It is one of the engines of development of talent, which goes on to support the inward investment that forms so much of the production landscape. In TV, kids’, unscripted and, as has been noted, the mid-tier of £1 million to £3 million dramas, are the ones that tend to be British stories, often commissioned by the PSBs. We have seen spend on dramas in the £1 million to £3 million budget range, and comedy, fall by £250 million between 2023 and 2024, and it is not getting any better. Obviously, BBC funding has been cut by 25% over the last 16 years, with eight years of freezes. That has an enormous impact on the number of British stories that can be told. Increasingly, even though they are set in the UK, a lot of those stories are now filmed abroad. We are concerned about reshoring to the US, but we need to look at reshoring back to the UK productions that are set in Yorkshire and filmed in Hungary. We should bring those back and we have ideas for how to do that.

Dr Huq: Thanks for that opening picture. Following on from that, we predicted in our report that the independent film tax credit would be a game changer. This is a two-part question. Has that come to fruition, and are the smaller people getting the benefit of it? Also, it seems that a lot of the UK’s recent success has been driven by overseas investment. We have had trips to Warner and Universal—all the big names that have relocated here. Even at Ealing Studios, the world’s longest continuously working film studio, I have seen how they have remodelled to include top-notch accommodation where Hollywood stars can stay when they are making a production. Perhaps this is a Pete question: how much is this is in danger from all the inducements that Trump is offering? So, did it work and is it in peril now?

Harriet Finney: To start with, “Did it work?”, the IFTC was an incredibly welcome intervention. We will publish an impact assessment on that in 2027. We just do not have enough data at the moment to give the impact, but we can say that 400 projects have been through fund certification and we have about another 150 at interim stage. Definitely, a significant number of projects are benefiting. At an anecdotal level, we are seeing those films on screen now. A lovely example is “I Swear”. I was speaking to the film maker last week. That film could not have been made without the independent film tax relief. The commercial market just would not step up and get involved in it. There was too much swearing, and at the time the lead in it was not a household name. Obviously, subsequently, they managed with the IFTC to make the film and box offices worldwide responded really well. The film, based in Scotland, told an incredibly authentic story based around the Tourette’s community, and people would not have been able to see it without the tax relief. We know that films are getting made with that support, but, as Pete talked about, the point is that you need to crowd in other funding around it to make projects viable.

Pete Johnson: To build on that, I absolutely echo Harriet’s point about a lot of projects being made that would not have been made without it. I think we will increasingly see that come through in the future figures. Also, if we want to get the biggest possible bang for the buck that we spend on the IFTC, we need to think about other parts of the value chain as well. Incentivising more risk investment in independent film production companies would help to raise funds, get budgets and the tax relief. At the other end, there is making sure they can get seen in cinemas. An awful lot of those films do not have a significant cinema run, partly because the funding is not there to market them effectively. It is a crowded attention economy. If you do not have the big star—you cannot afford Tom Cruise in an independent British film—you must have a decent marketing spend.

I know that the Committee has been very supportive of the film distribution tax expenditure credit proposal. We were hoping that that might emerge from the Budget we have just seen. It did not, but we still believe that it would have a significant impact. It is pretty much self-funding, in terms of the additional VAT projected to come back to the Exchequer. It is projected to have a return on investment of £6.52 for every £1 of tax credit, which is a pretty good return on investment, but if people are not seeing the films they do not make money. That builds the value in subsequent windows and markets, as well. If it has had a cinema release and had that breakthrough in public consciousness, it has a higher resale value in other markets and windows. That is really important.

You mentioned the balance between inward investment and domestic production. We have been fantastically successful in attracting inward investment. That is a really good thing. An awful lot of jobs depend on it. We are really good at it. In 2025, 91% of film production in this country was inward investment, and only 7% was domestic. The trick that we need to pull off is to retain and build that inward investment—we do not want to lose any of it—but also to recognise that we need a stronger domestic economy, so that we are not so subject to the vagaries of tax credits in the US drawing back US production.

Claire Mundell: I just add that, when the tax credits were originally introduced, they were designed to give producers equity in their own productions, to increase their IP stake in a project. The tax credits are now absolutely non-negotiable. Every project requires tax credit, in independent film and increasingly in television, which is now financed like independent film. I think we need to look at tax credits in the round. There are lots of mechanisms there. You mentioned the R&D tax credit earlier. I cannot stress how crucial that is for independent production companies. The availability of development financing, which used to be fairly good, certainly among the UK broadcasters, is under severe pressure. The BBC has cut its development funding by 10%. That funding is very hard to come by. Without that, and given that it can take anything from 18 months to six or 10 years to get a project to screen, we have no pipeline to production. The tax credits are absolutely essential. We need to strengthen them and, where we can, increase them, perhaps—widening the scope of the criteria for the R&D tax credit, particularly.

Dr Huq: You mentioned unscripted, and Versa Studios, which does “Big Brother”, is in my seat. It would love to see that. Ireland has a 20% rate for unscripted shows. Would you guys go with that?

Pete Johnson: We are seeing a lot of our production go to Ireland as a result. As an industry, we are working together to look at how that package of support for the sector might be improved to help reshore some of that activity and grow domestic production in particular. We need a stronger domestic sector. That needs to run through the value chain, from raising finance to how consumers get to see the content at the other end.

Nigel Huddleston: I refer hon. Members to my entry in the Register of Members’ Financial Interests. I used to be Financial Secretary to the Treasury, so I will claim some credit for the financial incentives, but also some of the blame for not getting it 100% right.

I have a brief question for Claire that others will probably build on. You said that the tax credits are really important. My challenge is: are they really? Is it not the case that we would do quite well anyway, because we have such incredible talent both in front of and behind the camera? We have amazing facilities, studios, and so on. If those tax incentives did not exist, could we be confident that we would still have a thriving film and TV industry, because of those other incredible success factors?

Claire Mundell: No.

Nigel Huddleston: No—right, okay.

Claire Mundell: I can expand if you like.

Chair: Very quickly.

Claire Mundell: No, because the global marketplace is extremely competitive with incentives, and producers follow the money. Obviously, we see American projects come to UK cities and double USA cities in places like Glasgow and other parts of the UK. Without those incentives—no. A lot of our talent is overseas and would move overseas. We would lose our talent in fact, because they would also follow the opportunities.

Nigel Huddleston: That is a very clever answer.

Chair: Good question; excellent answer.

Mr Alaba: This question is to Harriet. We asked the BFI to look at targeted tax credits—uplifts, specifically in the £1 million to £3 million range for high-end TV. You touched on distribution challenges, global reduction and increasing international competition, and some of my colleagues touched on other aspects. You also mentioned the impact assessment that has not been done yet. I want to zero in on that. What would you need to do for the impact assessment to happen? How long would it take? What else do you think you could potentially find if it were put in place?

Harriet Finney: The impact assessment is on the impact of the independent film tax relief introduced in April 2024, so we just need enough data through to be able to finalise it. It will be published as a chapter of our screen business report, which will come out next year. Importantly, and related to all this, all the tax incentives have a return on investment. It is important to remember that they are not a subsidy for the sector; they are definitely an incentive. The last screen business report showed that for every £1 on film there was over £8 in return on investment, and for every £1 on high-end television there was over £6 of return on investment. Whenever we look at introducing tax reliefs, it is vital that we can show that there will be an economic return on them, as Pete already said in terms of the distribution tax relief.

As to the work around the HETV relief that was recommended, we worked with the high-end television group, providing data in the run-up to a previous Budget. It was not taken forward at Budget, but, as Claire has spoken really clearly to us, I think we are in a world where the paradigm is continuing to shift away from there being a clear way to fund some of that lower-budget high-end TV. We very much hope to continue to work closely with the high-end TV group as we head towards the next Budget. It will be part of the work that we are doing that Pete alluded to. There is a moment where we need to have a think about the shifting paradigm, and whether our policy and tax incentive frameworks are absolutely optimised to ensure we can keep benefiting from the international growth, and domestic growth as well.

Claire Mundell: I think the incentives provide a framework for us to compete at the highest level. The door is open for UK talent, production and projects, because we are recognised for our high level of skills around the world, but the world is very competitive. In the production community, we are concerned about the proposed US incentives on the horizon, and what they will do to inward investment in the UK. Inward investment, in turn, of course creates lots of training opportunities for existing crew members to move from a certain budget level to another one. It provides opportunities for on-screen talent. Although production companies might not retain IP in those scenarios, they provide valuable services, and that enables them to have income to develop their own projects, so they can provide domestic projects to the market. That worries the production community. We have a great foundation with the UK tax credit system, but we must not be complacent about how we could improve on it.

Pete Johnson: That skills development point is one of the tragedies of the amount of offshoring from the UK to other territories that is going on at the moment. Particularly if you look at dramas in the half a million to £1 million an hour range, which do not currently qualify for the tax reliefs at all—because there is a £1 million threshold, as the Committee will know—every time one of those gets done in Hungary, a load of Hungarian workers are trained and are getting experience, rather than British workers. That is the level at which people often get either their first role or their first role in a more senior position. They are gaining those credits, but it is Hungarian workers gaining them, not British workers, currently.

Claire Mundell: It is also about longer-term cultural relevance for our audiences, and reflecting the diversity of British life on screen, which I think is under threat. We have to protect that. The BBC in particular is at the heart of that. More certainty over the funding scenarios for the BBC, and the ways in which it has a responsibility to reflect nations’ and regions’ productions, is critical.

Pete Johnson: I do not think you can overstate how important a decent funding settlement for the BBC is for the whole sector. We all depend on having that engine at the centre of it, spreading growth throughout the whole of the UK and delivering British stories. It is absolutely vital to the health of the sector.

Chair: It was described by one of our witnesses in the BBC report that we recently did as a university of skills for the British film and TV sector. Thank you very much. Let us move on to Liz.

Liz Jarvis: Our report raised concerns about performance rights, consent and historic contracts, and concluded that current copyright, IP and data protection laws do not adequately protect performers from unauthorised voice cloning, deepfakes or the use of recorded performances to train AI systems. What progress has been made since the Committee reported in enabling the screen sector to benefit from AI while protecting copyright, creative rights in the UK and intellectual property?

Harriet Finney: I am happy to start with that one. One of your recommendations definitely fed through in terms of supporting the Government’s thinking around copyright. We are very pleased that they moved away from the opt-out option that was on the table previously. For the sector, there are two sides to this coin, as you rightly pointed out. There is the side around protection. That is absolutely key. We expect some further news from the Government on what the legislative framework that could sit around AI will look like. As to the creative, we absolutely need that kind of legislative certainty around copyright, personality rights and digital replicas to be in place, to know where we can safely innovate. That is one side of the coin.

The other side is making sure that our creators and film makers in this country have a really clear understanding of the tools that they can use and what ethical guard-rails are on those tools, and that they are allowed to experiment with a framework and guidance that are really clear. Since the report was published, the BFI has put £1.2 million of funding into innovation R&D specifically around AI. Just this morning, we announced something called “In Our Hands”, which is really practical funding of the National Film and Television School and Falmouth University to create two programmes of R&D. They will allow creators to experiment with tools and understand what does and does not work, where skills training is needed around these tools, where guidance is needed on how to use them ethically, and ultimately what sorts of guard-rails need to be put up, particularly around independent film makers who would like to experiment in this space.

Next year, the BFI will publish some guidance and guard-rails around AI usage, and we hope that that will be really helpful for the independent community and make sure that we are on a level playing field with the larger studios. Obviously, we see a lot of M&A activity in that space, so they are either buying in tools or working very closely. We do not want independent film makers not to have the agency to work confidently with AI. So some of it is guidance and guard-rails, and some of it is around the legislative framework.

Pete Johnson: Our members have engaged directly with Government and the IPA throughout this process, and we continue to do so. A lot of these questions have now been presented—gifted—to the IPO to work through with various industry players to try to work out a solution to that. I was there the last time the industry was asked to work through it together and it did not really move things forward. We are much more hopeful that this time we will come to a solution, because more certainty would be very useful in allowing people to invest in these tools and use them. The trouble is that, if you are not quite sure where the value is, what the liabilities are and all the rest of it, it retards use and investment. We are keen to see that clarified.

Liz Jarvis: Harriet, you mentioned guidance and guard-rails, but it is estimated that AI could impact up to 80% of production roles in some form by 2028. Do you think the industry is responding fast enough?

Harriet Finney: We have also done some work with CoSTAR, which is the big investment and R&D network sitting around the sector on AI and AI usage. We have done some work on that around skills and published a preliminary report. The next one will come out next year. You are absolutely right that part of this is making sure we bring the right skills into the workforce and that the people we train now are appropriately skilled, but it is also about transferable skills and upskilling those already in the industry. Some of the work we are doing in these innovation R&D labs will be about making sure that existing film makers have the agency and tools to be able to work. It is definitely something on which we need to keep a very close eye. In thinking about the competitive advantage we have in the UK, we have been really good at innovating. Our visual effects industry is absolutely extraordinary. Let us make sure our workforce is equipped for the next wave of AI.

Jo Stevens: I declare an interest as chair of the all-party parliamentary group on freelancers. Thank you very much for coming today, everybody. What do you think the new Freelance Champions should prioritise to make a tangible difference to freelancers in film and high-end TV?

Pete Johnson: The real challenge at the moment for freelancers is finding work. Despite what I said earlier about the very strong positive prospects for growth over the long term, we are definitely in a bit of a contraction at the moment. We have seen figures from the Film and TV Charity emphasising the number of people who have not found work. Of those surveyed in February of this year, 74% had considered leaving the sector largely because of the problems of finding work, and 22% had experienced sustained worklessness, which means being out of work and not having worked for more than three months in the previous 12. That is a big concern for freelancers at the moment. We had a long period of the streamer wars boom, where freelancers were never short of work. We are now in a very different environment where it is quite difficult to sustain.

I hope the Freelance Champions will be able to examine what more we might do to support freelancers. Other countries do more to support their creative freelancers. We published a report at the beginning of last year, “Make Freelancing Pay”, which looked at how six other countries used different systems to support creative freelancers. They recognise that in our sector being out of work for a period of time is a certainty, not a risk. France has had its own measures in place for over a hundred years because of that, specifically for people working in the creative sector. Ireland has a universal basic income for certain creative artists. That has just been made permanent after a three-year trial. I am not saying that any of those is the solution, but we need to worry about maintaining a freelance workforce of sufficient scale so that when we get into another upswing we are not all trying to poach each other’s workers again, as has happened in the past.

Claire Mundell: We will face a particular issue in that scenario, given the number of people leaving the industry because of the instability of it. It comes back to all the structural points we have been discussing and the framework of incentives and support that we need in the industry. The engine room of the industry is the freelance community. No television project, high-end drama or feature film is made without the freelance community, and it is having a really tough time. That is why the mechanisms in place have to be strengthened, increased and protected. We also do not want to lose the skills pipeline. In Scotland, for example, “Outlander”, which I am sure everybody knows or has heard of at least, over a decade trained hundreds of new entrants to the industry. We saw young people come in as trainee runners and leave as executive producers. We need to find a way to make that level of opportunity consistent across the board.

The industry and freelance community are coming together, but the bedrock of this exists with successful and sustainable businesses that can retain their own IP, thanks to the terms of trade, and that can have a long-term plan and have some certainty over what the commissioning opportunities are in the landscape. That is very difficult at the moment. If you do not know what the commissioning opportunities are it is impossible to plan your business. Without that, it is very hard to give any certainty to the freelance community as to what is coming down the line in a year or 18 months. This year alone in Scotland, we have seen a significant reduction in the number of projects filming in the past six months. We are seeing people leave the industry just now, but it has to be discussed structurally.

Jo Stevens: Am I right that, in summary, you do not really look at freelancers as a thing on their own; they are an integral part of the whole structure and financing of film and high-end TV?

Claire Mundell: One hundred per cent, absolutely, and their talent and experience is what makes production possible. On a low-budget drama, you might be looking at a crew of 150, but in a higher-budget drama you can have 400 to 500 people there. The loss of production opportunities is really worrying to that freelance community. If we lose their skills at either the top or bottom end, when a boom comes again we are back in the position of having to train new entrants and we would lose the benefit of the long-term planning to which so much care and attention has been given.

Jo Stevens: Thinking back to what you said earlier about the US incentives and the impact they could have, if they go ahead and that makes the UK less attractive and competitive internationally, is there a particular support mechanism or protection you would want to be available to freelancers in that scenario? Is there an obvious thing that should be put in place to support them?

Claire Mundell: To be positive about the opportunities, the UK freelance community is among the most talented, skilled and internationally recognised practitioners in the world, so we are starting from a position of strength. I come back to the framework of tax credits and incentives, but those are the biggest opportunity for us. If we can increase our incentives or have a suite of them that, as was said earlier, provides an equal system, that is our biggest protection. People will still come to the UK not just for our talent and crews but our locations, legacy and history, but we just cannot be complacent, because you can make anything look like anywhere with a bit of skilled production expertise.

Chair: Can I ask about the freelancer community? Once again, we seem to be spending far too much time this week talking about misbehaviour and, in some cases, criminal acts by gentlemen in the media abusing their power and influence. It always occurs to me that freelancers must be the most vulnerable when it comes to dealing with people of this nature.
