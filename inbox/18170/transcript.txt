Q1 Chair: I am Alex Sobel, the Chair of the Joint Committee on Human Rights. Good afternoon and welcome to the ninth meeting of the new session of Parliament of the Joint Committee on Human Rights. This is my inaugural public evidence session as Chair of the Joint Committee. The committee is made up of 12 members, drawn equally from both Houses. Our role is to examine how public policy, including legislation and practice, impacts human rights of people in the United Kingdom. You can review our recent reports, including on human rights, the regulation of AI and the Northern Ireland legacy Bill, on our website. Our current thematic inquiry is on the impact of security, safety and protest on human rights.

This is the first oral session of the inquiry on the Immigration and Asylum Bill, which is our current legislative inquiry. I would like to welcome our witnesses, Nadine Finch, former judge at the Upper Tribunal Immigration and Asylum Chamber; Imran Hussain, executive director of communications and external affairs at the Refugee Council; and Professor David Cantor, director of the Refugee Law Initiative at the School of Advanced Study at the University of London. I would also like to welcome Tony Vaughan MP, member of the House of Commons Justice Committee, who will be guesting at this evidence session.

I want to remind Members and witnesses of the sub judice resolutions. As Members will be aware, the sub judice resolutions of both Houses mean that live legal cases should not be discussed in parliamentary proceedings. Please would Members and witnesses avoid referring to any live legal cases in our discussions today?

I will ask the panel the first question. The Bill would bring into effect some of the reforms announced in the Restoring Order and Control policy statement last November. Is the Bill making major changes to the law on asylum and immigration, or are there more significant announcements in the policy statement being brought in by other means?

Professor David Cantor: A starting point for considering the Bill in the context of the policy statement is to say that it is not bringing forward many of the more important provisions that relate to community sponsorship and so on. Without that broader context and the basis in the legislation, the Bill itself stands as a rather haphazard array of different provisions relating to particular areas, such as this idea of a core protection status, the changes to the tribunal and the idea of a refugee tax. It is a bit of a mixed bag and the broader policy context that was set out last year in the statement is not given legislative grounding there.

There is a particular risk, if one looks back to the statement, in taking other countries’ models as a point of reference. If one looks at the statement and the explanatory notes for the Bill, there are a number of assertions about the rationale behind the legislation that is being brought forward, and the policy measures as well, that seem relatively unsubstantiated by reference to, for example, appeal success rates and the role of the appeal system in the broader asylum structure of the UK. On the other hand, there are also some misapprehensions about other countries’ asylum systems, in particular that of Denmark, and how they work. My colleagues, I am sure, would equally be happy to talk through where those lie in later evidence.

Nadine Finch: I would slightly disagree with David, in that the way that policy is implemented, in terms of immigration, through the statement of changes to the Immigration Rules, which is an easy and quick way to do it, is also quite a dangerous way to do it. I would prefer to see more in legislation. This Bill has only things that really have to go through the legislation, so things that are creatures of statute, such as the First-tier Tribunal.

The changes to what they require lawyers to talk about and take into account in terms of Article 8 are arguably something that has to go through statute. I am not sure about the core protection one but they obviously decided it was. We have things that they feel have to go through statute, so that is the difference.

When you talk to people, and I quite often give talks to groups of immigrants, they are very confused, because they think everything has to go through the Bill, but it does not. They will ask me what is in the Bill and I say, “You also have to look at what’s in the Immigration Rules and settlement. That’s what’s going to impact on you”. Unfortunately, when the Secretary of State and her Department talk, quite often I find it difficult to know whether she is talking about policy or prospective legislation, so it is even more difficult.

The people who are most impacted by what are gargantuan and very complicated legislation and rules are left really confused about what is happening. It is also important because, in the many things that are happening, the penalties attached to not complying in the Act are immense. It could be as much as not understanding that you have to send something into a certain office or having moved to a different place of detention so a letter does not get to you. The implications of not understanding are huge.

Imran Hussain: The first thing to say is that we have already seen big changes in the last couple of years. In 2024 the system was on the point of meltdown. Decision-making had really slowed down. Since then, decision-making has been prioritised. The speed at which cases are being processed has gone up as well. That is why we are seeing the numbers of people in hotels reducing. We have already had that in the sense of big changes.

There are changes happening, as Nadine has alluded to, outside of the Bill, the most obvious being around core protection. I think in March we saw some of the provisions kick in there about refugee status being limited to 30 months. There are big changes in the Bill and we may get on to those kinds of issues, but clearly we are concerned about what is happening around appeals. The Refugee Council is also concerned about the policy of charging refugees.

The other thing is what is missing. There is something big that is missing in the Bill and what the Government are doing outside of primary legislation, and that is about tackling the issue of poor decision-making. That is a long-standing problem in the asylum system. It is corrosive on the whole system because it has a human cost, but also it delays things and leads to greater costs in the system, whether it is accommodation costs or legal costs. We know that, when people appeal, of those who have an appeal determined about four in 10 win, but many others also win because the Home Office looks at the decision before it passes it on to the appeal authorities, sees an error and withdraws it. Our estimate is that the real success rate at appeal is closer to two-thirds.

If you look at how many people are in the appeals backlog at the moment, it is about 90,000 cases. We have estimated, of those 90,000, how many are likely to win their appeal and, for those people, what the added cost to the system is in terms of accommodation and subsistence. We are looking at around about £1 billion of savings if the people who win their cases in the current backlog had received proper decisions first time around. There are huge savings there. That is one thing that I would urge the committee to focus on or recognise. That is a big challenge in the system but it is not really being addressed.

Chair: We will next move on to a series of questions about protection status.

Q2 Baroness Hamwee: As well as statutes, rules and all the rest of it, of course we have the refugee convention. Perhaps for context I will read Article 34: “Contracting states shall as far as possible facilitate the assimilation and naturalisation of refugees. They shall in particular make every effort to expedite naturalisation proceedings and to reduce as far as possible the charges and costs of such proceedings”.

The Bill supports the move from a model in which refugees receive permanent protection, as you have said, to a model in which there is a more basic and temporary protection, in fairly neutral words. Is this new model compatible with our obligations under the refugee convention? Three people are nodding a bit.

Imran Hussain: We are worried and share the views of UNHCR and others about whether this model of core protection is consistent with Article 34. We are talking about refugee status reviews every 30 months potentially for 20 years until people get settlement. That is maybe eight reviews. We have done some estimates of the added bureaucracy of having to do these extra reviews. For 10 years, that is a million extra reviews. Is that the best use of resources for a system that is overstretched? That is a question we have.

If someone is given status for 30 months, that clearly has an impact on their stability and ability to find work and housing. We already know from Refugee Council services, which help refugees find employment, that employers even find the current five years difficult sometimes. There is a lack of certainty and sometimes they think it is not worth the risk of employing someone with five-year status. With 30-month status, it is going to be much harder for refugees to find employment, so it will be a real barrier to integration.

As we have heard, Article 34 uses language such as “shall” and “as far as possible”. It is pretty clear what the obligations on the Government are. The suggestion that such a core protection as the Government are talking about is going to help integration is really difficult to understand.

Professor David Cantor: Article 34 is not a duty to nationalise refugees, but it does have very strong positive language about the steps that states must take to facilitate integration, assimilation and naturalisation. It certainly casts against regressive steps and things that will inhibit that process of refugees becoming assimilated. The provisions as they stand on this issue seem to go directly in contrary to the obligation that we have contracted into under the refugee convention.

That is made even more difficult if you think about the context of refugees globally in today’s world, where refugee situations, because of long-standing dictatorial regimes, conflicts and so on, inevitably are protracted situations. We know that, for the last 30 years, it is nothing new. This is not a change that has suddenly come about. I think at the last counting the average refugee was at risk in refugeehood for 17 or more years, and that was 17 years and counting a couple of years ago. Reviewing refugee status every two and a half years not only inhibits integration, but also seems utterly outwith the factual context in which we have found ourselves, not suddenly in the last year or two, but over recent decades, and the world as it exists at present.

The burden administratively on the Government, as one understands that some of these provisions are looking to recoup the costs of the asylum system, will be immense. If you think of the numbers of refugees in the United Kingdom presently, even if it were not applied retrospectively but applied only going forward, you are talking about an immense amount of work for the Home Office or whoever is tasked to do this. That seems utterly contrary to many of the more sensible aspirations around trying to cut costs and manage an efficient, effective and fair system that the Government set out in their statement.

Nadine Finch: Could I answer in a slightly different way? The refugee convention makes absolutely clear what classes of people should be protected and under subsidiary protection, but it also makes clear when that protection comes to an end. There has been no attempt to marry up with that advice from the UNHCR about when it comes to an end. The 30 months is very arbitrary. Why should you review it at 30 months? What has happened to that particular asylum seeker to mean they have fallen out of the protection of the refugee convention? There seems to be no connection between that and every 30 months.

As David just said, if you look at the list of refugees and where they come from, a huge number come from places like Eritrea, Somalia and Ethiopia. I was a barrister and then a judge for many decades. The same places still produce refugees and there is no realistic view of them not producing refugees, so, again, why 30 months? The people who are caseworkers, lawyers and judges know that these people are not going home anytime soon.

As David said, the Home Office is costing itself money, time and manpower or womanpower. At one time, it was talking about places it could fast-track, such as certain clans in Somalia or certain places in the Middle East that you knew you could not return to. There are other ways of dealing with it than in this very rough way of, “Every 30 months we will churn”.

The Home Office is also not taking into account the fact that there are places where things have resolved and the vast majority of refugees have gone home. People do not necessarily want to live in Britain. They do not necessarily want to leave their homeland, culture, family and career and stay here. There seem to have been no pilot projects. There has been no research into why people go home. I find a 30 months temporary protection devoid of any real research. I cannot understand why. It is arbitrary.

I would mention in passing that the Government also confuse Ukrainians with refugees. Ukrainians are not refugees; they are here under temporary protection. If there was another situation like Ukraine, they could model themselves on the temporary protection directive of the EU and say, “We’re going to offer protection because we think this is going to be a short-term situation”. I think they thought that Ukraine would be solved. There is that possibility. In another place there may be something that they think will soon be resolved and they could offer people temporary protection, but they have not even discussed that. They have not put temporary protection and asylum together.

Baroness Hamwee: There is so much I would like to pursue.

Chair: I was going to quickly ask a follow-up. Nadine, considering what you have just said, what would your view be of, rather than having an arbitrary period, having a review when the material issue changes in the country of origin?

Nadine Finch: I think that most people would accept that. A lot of the clients I had when I was a barrister went home, and would go home as long as they had a right of appeal and access to legal aid. I am going to talk about the Afghans. A huge number of Afghans, unlike some other places, were people who were illiterate and came from rural areas. For them, that would be quite a challenge. They may not understand it. There are other places that would be much more in tune with social media and they would know exactly what was happening.

As long as people got a chance to understand what was happening and to appeal, because there may be individual circumstances around going back—for example, they could have done something to really harm somebody in a powerful position—it would be a much better way of doing it. I do not think that it would be more difficult either, and it certainly would not be more costly.

Q3 Baroness Chakrabarti: I declare a non-pecuniary interest as a council member of JUSTICE, whose legal director speaks on the next panel. Before I ask my planned question, I cannot resist following up on that point. The avenue of exploration that you have opened up as an alternative to what you see as an arbitrary period is very interesting. For example, would you feel comfortable with a statutory opportunity for the Home Secretary to trigger the potential end of refugee status, subject to appeal and so on, on the basis that there has been a change of circumstances in a particular territory?

It would not be on the basis of a particular period, but on the basis of a finding of fact by the Government, subject to safeguards and appeals, where the Government press a button and say, “We are of the view, based on the following facts, that things have changed in whichever jurisdiction and we are proposing, on a certain date, to end refugee protection, subject to appeal” or whatever. Is that something that you would be comfortable with, or would you be concerned about potential problems there too?

Nadine Finch: I would have two other asks, now you ask me. One is that with children it might be slightly different. I used to represent a huge number of unaccompanied minors. For children, especially children who have been orphaned or have lived here only during their adolescence, it is different. For children, it may not be at all fair to say, “You have to go back”, because what would they go back to? They would not be in education there. They would have no language. They might have lost language skills. Children would have to be another category.

As well as the appeal, I would want to see some expert advice in the Home Office. Having been a barrister and a judge such a long time, there used to be expert groups in the Home Office that were country-specific. They had a much better grasp of what happened. Now you just have people moved around, as far as I can see, from maybe the EU one day to deportations. They are not enabled to build up an expertise. It would work only if there were expert teams and experts called upon when needed.

Baroness Chakrabarti: This is my planned question. The relevant provisions of the Bill would allow for the introduction of a single definition of a protection claim and protection status, rather than an asylum claim or human rights claim and refugee or humanitarian protection status. Would this change affect the UK’s compliance with the refugee convention and/or human rights law?

Professor David Cantor: The point of reference for the UK’s obligations is the 1951 refugee convention, but also there are obligations that apply from human rights law that substantiate the category of humanitarian protection. The Bill as originally drafted was concerning in talking about a sole core protection status without giving further definition to that concept. There has been a move, I understand, with a recent amendment by the Government last week to introduce text in the Bill that speaks to the UK’s obligations under the refugee convention and gives a nod to humanitarian protection.

Although that language is welcome and it is right that that should appear in the Bill, rather than simply being left to regulation and politics, it should be looked at much more carefully by Parliament and this committee in terms of the way in which those provisions are phrased. Some of the provisions do not go as far, in terms of the scope of protection that is offered, as the current law stands. There may be a question as to particularly whether the provision relating to serious harm is outwith interpretation in international law of how human rights law might prevent people being sent to countries where they have a serious risk of harm. It would be worth looking very carefully at that provision again and making sure that the language is right there.

I will come back to the earlier question about cessation, which was the proposal made by Nadine. Cessation is what the refugee convention envisages. As a further ask, while cessation might very well be a useful way of approaching the kinds of issues being dealt with here, one would have to balance that by asking whether, given the length of some refugee situations, there is a point at which there is a need for thinking about settlement routes for refugees. I do not think that you can keep somebody in limbo, waiting for cessation indefinitely, so there is a balance to be had there.

On the core protection provision, clearly there is some concern about the scope of discretion given to the Secretary of State in making further regulations around this. I think that the Joint Committee would want to send a very firm message that any further regulations would need to be very much in compliance with the UK’s international obligations under human rights law and the refugee convention.

Imran Hussain: Similarly to David’s point, on the face of it, having a single protection status for refugees and humanitarian protection, with similar rights, is sensible. We did not find the original Clause 21 straightforward to understand and follow. I am aware that there is a new Clause 35 that has replaced 21. There is some uncertainty from the Refugee Council’s point of view about what the Government are looking to do here. Is the changing clause a drafting change or is it meaningful? Have the Government changed their approach? I am not sure that I have anything else to add there, I am afraid.

Nadine Finch: I am concerned about having one single definition because I do not really understand why they want to do it. It would seem to me that the entirety of refugee convention status and humanitarian protection is actually quite difficult to grasp. Putting them both together may seem simple. It may save money but it concerns me that it might be a downgrading of international protection. It might be saying, “This is a much easier way”, and in the regulations you may suddenly find some slightly different descriptions.

Baroness Chakrabarti: You are concerned that it is a way of obfuscating the actual legal obligations on the state in relation to what are currently different legal categories.

Nadine Finch: Yes.

Baroness Chakrabarti: Are you just worried about the motivation, or are you worried about the outcome?

Nadine Finch: It is the outcome, I think. I am particularly worried in the context of the abolition of the First-tier Tribunal and the lack of legal representation.

I should say that humanitarian protection is not used very much here, but it is used much more in Europe. I was part of the International Association of Refugee and Migration Judges and we did a series of guidances for the EU. The most tricky one was the qualification directive, which is like the humanitarian protection. It is a very large guidance, because it is a very difficult concept to grasp. It is when you do not fall under the classes of the refugee convention but you clearly are at risk. You might be at risk from civil war or some kind of attack within your own family. It is a much less-known protection.

Having worked on that guidance, I am worried about us just saying, “We can put them both together”, because I was surprised how different they were once I worked on it. Of course, humanitarian protection is used here for people who fall out of the refugee convention because of wrongdoings they have done. We are going to come on to other repercussions for those people. There may be a connection to that. Humanitarian protection can be a very good thing but it needs to be well defined to give people protection, because it is not as easy to understand. There is not the case law on it as there is on refugee status.

Q4 Peter Swallow: The Bill would provide that a refugee who is considered a danger to the UK would no longer be considered to be lawfully staying in the UK. Transparently, this is something that I know many of my constituents will think is a very common-sense change to make. What are the implications of this change for refugees and those who cannot be removed from the UK on human rights grounds? In other words, how do you see this being effective in practice?

Nadine Finch: I can see why, on the surface, people ask, “Why we are keeping people who might have done something wrong here?” The problem is that we have a complication of the refugee status protecting people. If you look at the case law, you cannot send them back to be on death row or to torture.

In reality, sometimes the Government have reached accommodations with the country of origin. I cannot remember the name of the case; one of you two might. There was somebody who had committed very wrong things and he was sent back, but there was an accommodation made. That is nothing you can put in legislation.

I trailed what I was going to say about “unlawfully present”, because some of the protections in the refugee convention apply only to people who are lawfully present. One of them is a right to an appeal and a due process. If you are going to say that people are unlawfully present, we must make sure that they still have a right to due process before being returned, if they are going to be returned.

Peter Swallow: Everyone in this country has a right to due process under the ECHR, do they not?

Nadine Finch: It is not as simple as that. There are huge legal aid deserts within the country. You are allowed legal aid only on very limited grounds now. For things such as this, you probably are not entitled to legal aid. If you are entitled to legal aid, you cannot necessarily get it, because if you live in many rural areas there is no legal aid provision. There is no immigration lawyer. Say you live somewhere in Northumberland. There may be nowhere nearer than Liverpool or something like that.

It sounds easy to say that people have a right. They have a right, but can they exercise it? If we are going to say that people have a right, is it a right in reality, or is it just on paper, and even if they are people we might find unpalatable?

I will tell you a small story. Years ago, I was sitting in that box you have in the House of Lords, giving people advice. Some Lib Dem Lords were talking about the Terrorism Act. Some Law Lord stood up and said that the characteristic of our democracy is that we give the worst people a right to appeal and a right to be heard. He said, “That is how I class my democracy. We give the worst kinds of terrorists a right”. That is really important, even though these are people who we might find unpalatable. It is really important for our democracy that everybody gets that right.

Professor David Cantor: I will answer the question in a slightly different, probably drier and more legal, way, which is that lawfully staying is a concept in the refugee convention. It appears that the legislation is trying to say that anybody who falls within the exclusion from the refoulement protection in Article 33(2), in other words anyone who is a danger to society, is therefore classed as not lawfully staying.

The impact of that, if that were followed through, would be that a recognised refugee who was seen as a danger would not have access to about five or six entitlements under the refugee convention that are given to refugees who are lawfully staying. They include waged employment, certain aspects of welfare and travel documents. It is not particularly clear to me why we would want to strip those particular entitlements from that class of individual. There is a question there about why this is being done and what the intended effect of that is likely to be.

The second point is that lawfully staying is a term that appears in an international treaty. In other words, it is defined by international law and has an autonomous meaning in international law. Countries cannot simply legislate domestically for whatever they consider that term to mean. Broadly speaking, the commentary and doctrine around the refugee convention sees lawfully staying not as whether you are lawful but simply as a question about whether you have been recognised as a refugee and living here for a period of time. That, in essence, is what it means. In French, it is a résidence régulière. In other words, regular residence is what the concept entails.

Peter Swallow: So that we are really clear on this, is your legal point on this that it is not possible for any given state to retract that status once given?

Professor David Cantor: My point is that the status accrues on the basis of being recognised as a refugee and having lived in the country on an ongoing basis for a period of time. We have an obligation to recognise the rights that accrue on the basis of that factual situation. If we were to legislate contrary to that, there would be a question about compliance with the refugee convention, yes.

Imran Hussain: It is the same point really. The Government seem to be conflating Article 33(2) with lawfully staying. I think that it is a point that UNHCR has made as well.

We would also question why we want to remove access to things such as employment, housing and social security from people in the country if they are going to be remaining in the country. We would also ask why the impact assessment does not say anything about the likely numbers of people affected by this proposal. That is something we feel is missing. We are not sure how many people it would be. I would suspect that it is a very small number of people, but that seems to be missing from the impact assessment.

Peter Swallow: Do you want to come back on that, Professor Cantor?

Professor David Cantor: It is unclear why this is the way to deal with the concern that one might have about a danger that an individual poses to society. The UK Government already have, if you like, undesirable but unreturnable individuals whom they deal with through a range of legislation that is already in place. What this adds is not quite clear.

Nadine Finch: Could I add something to what David said? When I read it, as somebody who practises in other law, not just immigration, I felt that they were using immigration law to deal with other things. Some people who are dangerous should be dealt with under criminal law or terrorism law. Why are they not being dealt with under those laws? Why are they suddenly trying to give the immigration law jurisdiction the burden of dealing with some of those people?

Q5 Sir Desmond Swayne: I receive representations from residents next door to where refugees have been billeted. There are very significant cultural differences and that gives rise to tensions. Salt is rubbed in the wound by the perception that everything is free for the refugees. It strikes me that those social tensions would be, at least in part, assuaged if there was a perception that the refugees were making a financial contribution to their costs. After all, it seems a reasonable ask to make, does it not?

Nadine Finch: I am not sure that it is reasonable. I have been practising for such a long time. If you turn back before 1999, refugees were dealt with through the social security system. They got 90% because it was thought that they would not be here very long and therefore they did not need winter clothes et cetera. They were spread out through the country and in ordinary housing. There were some people who did not like them being in their street, but the antipathy towards them was nothing like it is now. If you put a whole load of young men in a hotel, it is different from having a young man in your street, where you see him at the shops and get to know him.

The way that it developed is also incredibly costly. The privatisation of the immigration and asylum support system has enabled two or three huge firms such as Mitie, and I struggle to remember the others, to make a huge amount of money by acquiring hotels no longer in use, charging the Home Office a huge amount of money for them and providing very poor conditions. There is a hotel just down the road from me, which was quite a nice hotel, but it has not been maintained by the company. The food provided is really poor.

In some ways, I am going to say to you, the refugees are not being asked to pay the Home Office or the taxpayer back. They are being asked to mainly pay the privatised companies that have benefited from privatising asylum support, whether it is in a hotel, individual houses—that does not really happen now—acquired military barracks or barges, none of which maintains an Article 3 standard in my view, but all of which are really costly. It seems to me that there should be a real review of what is needed.

If you leave aside the cost of the accommodation, what is actually given to the individual refugee is minute. If they are in a hotel that has food as well, is it £9 a week they are given? If they are somewhere without food, they are given a princely sum of about £49 a week. That is probably what your constituents are thinking, that that perhaps is what they are given themselves, but what they will be charged is the huge amounts of money paid to the privatised big companies that are making money out of the Government. If your constituents ask them to repay, they would be repaying these big companies. They would not be paying the Government.

Sir Desmond Swayne: I am trying to get at the principle, rather than who is getting paid.

Nadine Finch: Let me go at it another way. If you are a person who has been raped or assaulted and you get protection, is it right to ask you to pay the hospital bill?

Sir Desmond Swayne: If you are a person who has paid gangsters very substantial sums to get you here, is it unreasonable to ask for a contribution for your keep?

Nadine Finch: I practised at a time when people came in to what was then Eurostar in Victoria and claimed asylum. It was cheaper and more manageable. If we had safe routes of entry, it would be cheaper and more controllable.

Sir Desmond Swayne: Come on. You live in a world of illusion.

Chair: This is what we are trying to get to with the inquiry, and I might pose this to Professor Cantor and then bring in Imran on it. If the Government introduce new powers to recoup the cost of asylum support, is it compatible with the refugee convention, particularly Article 29, which explicitly prohibits imposing duties, charges or taxes on refugees?

Professor David Cantor: There is a real question here. If you look at the measure in context, what are the Government asking for powers to do? The Government have not allowed people to work, and that is a decision that the Government have taken. They are not able to maintain themselves. Therefore they are forced into a situation, living often in substandard accommodation with nothing to do all day. Of course, as neighbours, no doubt, Sir Desmond, that is something that leaps to the eye. I can well understand that. These are people who have come here to seek protection. They are being asked to pay for costs for being in a position that they do not want to be in.

There is a particular unfairness here. I am not sure that it has been noted yet. As I understand it, we are asking only the genuine refugee to pay these costs. For the person who is accommodated and found not to be in need of protection, presumably they are removed and the bill is wiped clean, if you like, whereas the genuine refugee is forced into a position of paying for the fact of being found as a genuine refugee. There is a distinct unfairness there before we even get to the question of distinct legal obligations.

Before getting to those obligations, there is a real question about how this facilitates integration or assimilation. Going back to the earlier questions, do we really want to be pushing people who seek protection in our country into marginal positions where they feel like, whatever they earn, they can never quite pay off the costs of what they have come to owe to the Government, in a sense through no fault of their own, through being unable to work?

When we come to talk about the law, the position is absolutely clear. It is an unlawful provision in relation to the refugee convention. Article 29 says, “The Contracting States shall not impose upon refugees duties, charges or taxes, of any description whatsoever, other or higher than those which are or may be levied on their nationals in similar situations”. The refugee convention is absolutely crystal clear. If you look at the commentary, I do not see that there is any wriggle room whatsoever in relation to this particular provision.
