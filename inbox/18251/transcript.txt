Examination of witness

Dame Anne Owers.

The Chair: Welcome everybody to this meeting of the Justice and Home Affairs Select Committee. Following up our work on prisons, we are absolutely delighted that we have Dame Anne Owers with us today. Dame Anne, would you be kind enough to just introduce yourself and your background?

Dame Anne Owers: Thank you, Chair. I was for nine years Chief Inspector of Prisons. After that, I ran the Independent Police Complaints Commission, as it then was, and I have also been national chair of the Independent Monitoring Boards in prisons. Currently, I chair the Howard League.

Lord Tope: We were told just now we may call you Anne. I hope that is all right.

Dame Anne Owers: You may.

Lord Tope: Thank you. I notice the Chair did not; that is why I thought I would check. I have a nice simple question to start with. How do we get out of the prison capacity crisis? What do you think about a simple answer to that question? You made five recommendations a while back, and I see that the then Minister accepted the majority of them in principle. Can you tell us which ones were not accepted and how you are getting on with the ones that were accepted?

Dame Anne Owers: I will deal with those questions in turn, and I have two preliminary comments. My report points not just to one capacity crisis, but to what I called a “permacrisis”, and those crises have not stopped since I produced the report. Prisons are good at crisis management—they have to be—but if you are operating always under crisis, you are not operating at optimal capacity. Capacity, as I said in my report, is not just about space; it is about whether you have the capacity to actually deal with the issues that have brought people to prison in the first place and will bring them back if you do not do something about it.

Before I get on to my own recommendations, I think there are two rather large elephants in the room. One is the rise in prison sentences, which everyone is aware of—at least a 40% rise recently; 80% in some serious cases—so that people are not getting out of prison as quickly, whatever you do at the front end. The second reason, which I also mentioned in my report, is the importance of investing in what I called “not prison”; investing in not just the Probation Service but those other services in the community which people need if they are not to get there in the first place or not to come back. If you look at what has happened in the youth justice system, when I was inspecting there were 3,000 children in prison; now it is in the three hundreds because of the engagement of other services in the community and a reduction in youth offending. Those are the preliminary comments.

In my view, those are some of the key areas for how we get out of the crisis. On the response to my report, this committee will be aware that when you produce reports, people accept things in principle and then you look at the practice and wonder what precisely has changed. That, I think, is the case for many of the things that I recommended.

I know this committee is aware that Amber Rudd is doing a review of the Prison Service, which I think is needed, to look at it independently. Indeed, I would like the Chief Inspector of Prisons to be able to look more at the Prison Service itself instead of individual prisons. That would be helpful. I also made quite a lot of recommendations about the need to do the same with the Probation Service. We know what has happened to the Probation Service, where the capacity pressures are invisible but very real. There is also the need to involve the third sector more not just in delivery but in planning for delivery, and I have not seen any change in that.

Both David Gauke’s review and my own recommended was that there should be some independent advisory body to provide advice and validation of capacity strategies and challenges in both prisons and probation and, in particular, any proposed changes in the criminal justice system. It is always tempting to tinker with the system and to tinker with sentencing, as we have found quite recently, but there is no independent validation of what might happen as a result.

To conclude, when I started my own review, I was presented with what the Permanent Secretary in the Ministry of Justice had done before. That was to set out something she called “operation rear view”, which was to collect evidence that would show the impending result of what was happening in prisons and prison capacity, which everyone was aware of. That was very helpful to me. I would like there to be an independent body that carries out “operation forward look”, because if you are driving a car and only ever look in the rear-view mirror, you know what will happen and it keeps on happening. David Gauke also recommended that, but I do not yet see any movement on that. Having some independent body looking at the impact on capacity in both prisons and probation of any interesting new development thought plan would be incredibly helpful because it is known internally but not always known externally.

Baroness Berridge: You mentioned that the Amber Rudd review is necessary. We have a list of quite a number of reviews that have happened in the last five or 10 years. How do you distinguish that review?

Dame Anne Owers: It is looking independently at what is happening in the Prison Service as a whole. As I say, the chief inspector can look at individual prisons. There is not at the moment—and there should be—a regular capacity for looking at what is happening in prisons. What is happening to staffing? What is happening to buildings? All those things are very important.

But I think your point is a wider one that Governments are very good at commissioning independent reviews, independent inspections and independent all kinds of things. The advantage of independence is that you can say what should be happening rather than what can happen right now. The proof of the pudding is whether anything changes.

Baroness Bertin: Good morning. Can we carry on a little from the first question about how effective the systems in place for prisoners leaving prisons are—for example, tagging, housing and health? They are three quite meaty subjects, which I realise you cannot answer in full. If we could begin on tagging because—as you will be very aware—victims of domestic abuse are terrified by the early release scheme and the fact that tagging does not seem to be, as far as we can tell, being put in place in an entirely proper manner.

Dame Anne Owers: I am not an expert on tagging, so I am not going to venture too many opinions, except to say that I know the Institute for Government has had a look at it, has it not? There are some serious issues about compliance, fitting tags, unfitting tags and so on. Of course, it also adds to the pressure on the police service and so on. That is evident. The other problem with tagging though is that it is enforcement, not support. What people leaving prison very often need is the services that are very stretched outside: housing, mental health services, drug and alcohol services, and employment—all those things. That is the crucial thing. Just investing in prisons or indeed just in any sort of enforcement is, I used to say, a bit like just investing in accident and emergency services in the health service—you are picking up what is going wrong elsewhere. You are not actually engaged in prevention, support and making people healthier and less likely to commit crimes.

Baroness Bertin: Could I just follow up on that, particularly around housing and health? How far away do you think we are from the place you put forward that would help stop people going back into prison? Could you put an estimate on how far away we are from getting to a better place on that?

Dame Anne Owers: Quite a long way away. Two-thirds of those who are homeless will end up back in prison; that is just one statistic. I could bore you with statistics for a long while on this. But we know that more of the people in prison will have used drugs and more of them will have mental health issues. We know that community services are very stretched. It is evident the criminal justice system acts as a sort of emergency mental health service, both in terms of policing and in terms of prisons. Without investment in “not prison”, I think we will be back in the same circle.

Lord Hogan-Howe: Just quickly on tagging, I agree entirely with everything you have said up to now, but there is now sobriety tagging, which helps to reduce some of the cause of some offending for some people. There is also geofencing, which helps reassure people such as domestic violence victims that people will not be entering an area in which they live, for example. It is not just the present system which is inefficient; it seems to me that the future has not been mapped out.

Dame Anne Owers: Yes, I take your point. I am not saying that tagging is ineffective—and certainly it can provide some assurance to people—but unless you deal with the underlying causes it is the same as prison. You are not in the end going to make the community safer.

Baroness Buscombe: Thank you. I have to say, Dame Anne, that we are really pleased that you have come here today because some of us have been debating this for over 20 years and there has been no change, just a different approach. The latest one, of course, is to try and sort the cost, time and delay through the Courts and Tribunals Bill. That is supposed to have a positive effect on prison capacity and the prison estate. We would love to have your thoughts on that and on what changes you would like to see made to the Bill.

Dame Anne Owers: I have to confess that I have not looked at the Bill in detail. You are right that there will be an immediate effect on both prisons and probation. The impact assessment for the Bill indicates that there will be a quick pressure consequence for both prisons and probation from the Courts and Tribunals Bill and that it will increase probation costs. The premise of the Bill of faster justice is a good thing in itself. It is a good thing for victims and actually a good thing for offenders too. What I saw in prisons a lot was prisoners serving almost the whole of their sentence on remand, which is not good for anybody because it means that obviously they cannot be put into offending behaviour programmes because they have not offended. They are not obliged to go to work or education, and prisons often cannot make the space for them to do that. In my report, I found that sometimes people are released from court without even any probation supervision because they had already served every part of their sentence before they had even been sentenced. Faster justice is, in principle, a good thing, but I go back to the point that it then requires support for prisons to be able to do what they are supposed to do.

Baroness Buscombe: Do you have confidence that that will happen? We know the probation system is in crisis and has been for years.

Dame Anne Owers: Absolutely. I wish I had more confidence but, as I said earlier, there has been a capacity crisis in probation for a very long time, not helped by extensive reorganisations and re-reorganisations, and difficulties with staffing and difficulties in all areas. The system is not working as it should.

Lord Bach: This is a hugely wide question, and I am going to try to ask about one aspect of it. What are the major challenges that you see in our prison system? I am almost slightly embarrassed to ask you that question. I would like just to direct your mind a bit towards your experience of the issue around prison officers. We have debated this and reported on it quite a lot recently, and there seems to be a real problem in terms of prison officers. I wonder whether you have any solutions.

Dame Anne Owers: Ah, solutions. I agree with you and, yes, if you were to ask me about the major challenges, I think we would probably be here until at least lunchtime. I do not propose to subject you to that. Yes, there is a real problem with staffing. There is a problem in both prisons and probation with the numbers and levels of experience of staff who are dealing with some very difficult and problematic people—and some have a lot of experience of prisons. Some 31% of the main prison officer grade have been there less than three years and only 25% more than 10 years. If you look back to 2010, when I stopped inspecting prisons, only 13% of prison officers had been there under three years, and 56% had been there more than 10 years.

Just being there for a long time does not necessarily make you a great prison officer, but it does mean that you have experience. The thing that struck me when I went around prisons was the inexperience and youth on the landings. There are not enough experienced prison officers around to warn those who are sometimes only 18 or 19 years old about being strong in the right places and not homing in on the wrong things at the wrong times. There is inexperience, inconsistent practice and limited capacity. I also have always thought that we do not invest enough in training our prison staff or governors. They are doing an incredibly difficult job and there is insufficient investment in both early and continuous training.

Lord Bach: Can I just have one quick supplementary? If I remember rightly, it looks like our allies—other countries when we look internationally—kind of professionalise prison officers and make it a worthier profession in the eyes of the public than we perhaps do here sometimes. Is there something in that? Is part of it an attempt to really make it sound like and be a better job, and make people prouder of it than they are? People are not particularly appreciative of prison officers. Is there anything in that at all?

Dame Anne Owers: There is something in it, but perhaps going down the Norwegian route of two years’ academic training before you become a prison officer is probably not it. We definitely need more training, but the second bit of your question about respect for the job is also important.

Lord Bach: Exactly.

Dame Anne Owers: People do not know what goes on in prison. People see police officers and the very difficult job that they are doing, but people do not see prison officers and what happens behind those walls. We need more investment in staff generally, and in in-service training and all those things. There are projects starting but, in my experience, projects start and sometimes sort of fade away.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: This committee has called for a greater focus on meaningful, purposeful activity in prisons. Indeed, the chief inspector has lamented over many decades the lack of meaningful, purposeful activity. Why do you think that is? What, in your view, is the best way to achieve it? Is it a matter of resource or political priority or is it just in the too-difficult box? How do you see that? The failure to realise the potential of activity in prison to help people avoid offending when they get out has been a sustained picture, has it not?

Dame Anne Owers: That is absolutely true. It is partly a function of what we were talking about earlier, which is that the sheer number of people in prison limits the budget. Look at the education budget, for example; the Prison Service will say that the education budget has not been cut. Well, no, but the number of prisoners has risen and teachers’ salaries have also risen, so there is an effective 30% to 40% cut in prison education. That is crucial because you are often dealing with people who have avoided or been avoided by education for most of their young lives and you have an opportunity there to do something.

The other thing I would say about prison education is that vocational training has taken a real dive. If you have people who have, as I say, been avoiding classrooms for most of their lives, sitting them down in a classroom is not necessarily the best way of learning. We are looking at vocational training in education generally, linking training to what people might want to do. I remember visiting Aylesbury prison a long time ago: Nissan had invested in a beautifully constructed car workshop, so that kids who outside prison had been taking cars were fascinated by them, were learning how to fix them and were really enthused by that. It is that kind of thing—I focused a bit on education but generally purposeful activity.

If you look at the surveys from the prison inspectorate, 34% of people are locked up for 22 hours a day. They are going to be locked up waiting for the next drone drop and watching television. You have the opportunity of literally a captive audience, and you ought to be doing something with them.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Do you think resource constraints are a major factor? Are prison staff now more inured to locking people up all day than they used to be? When I visited prisons—when I knew you—that was something that officers really wanted to avoid and yet it is very commonplace now.

Dame Anne Owers: I think it is the combined impact of inexperienced staff, the Covid lockdowns and—let us be honest—some difficult-to-reach prisoners. The combination of all those things has made a different feel in prisons than there used to be. When I used to inspect prisons, in a bad prison officers had a saying, “Happiness is door-shaped”. In other words, everything was all right as long as the prisoners were behind their doors. That was unusual, rare and frowned upon, but prisoners locked up for 22 hours a day has now become normalised in some places.

So I agree. Prisons are much less purposeful and much less active places than they used to be, although they were by no means perfect when I was inspecting them. If you look at the assessments of the Chief Inspector of Prisons and purposeful activity, from memory I think there was only one closed prison, which he said was good.

The Chair: Could I just interrupt? Can you tell us, and you may not know, whether the rate of reoffending was lower when you were doing your inspections, when there was more purposeful activity, than it is currently?

Dame Anne Owers: I cannot answer that question, I am afraid. I could go and have a look. I do not know.

The Chair: It would be very helpful if we knew that, because it would help make the case we are trying to make for more purposeful activity.

Dame Anne Owers: To some extent, the rate of reoffending depends on the number of short-sentence prisoners—there are now fewer prisoners serving short sentences—as those are your revolving-door prisoners. They will revolve until a certain point, when a light goes on and they become too old or stop doing it. It depends a bit on the composition of the prison population, as well as what is happening in prisons.

The Chair: That is a very good point.

Lord Dubs: Good morning, Anne. In our report, we talked a lot about the power of governors and how much autonomy they have. Indeed, we felt, and I quote from our report, “Governors are hindered by an over-complicated and confusing management structure, excessive bureaucracy” et cetera. What do you think?

Dame Anne Owers: I agree in principle and so does the Prison Governors Association, unsurprisingly. Governors often feel that their hands are tied in terms of the things that they want to do, even the things they want to get for the prison. Everything is very centralised. I know that probation is looking at devolution to local areas and that would also fit in with governors getting more involved in what happens outside. However, if you are going to give more autonomy and decision-making to governors then, like with prison staff, you need to invest in training. This is a very difficult job: you are running something that has to provide everything that a small town would; it is a very complex environment with some very complex people. More devolution to governors would be helpful, combined with more training and, I hope, less movement of governors.

I noticed in the Prison Inspectorate report, and the IMB report, that one prison had had three governors in a year. The staff know that they just need to sit around and wait for the next one. You have to stay long enough to manage the consequences of the changes that you want to put in place.

The Chair: Just before we move on to Lord Anderson, should the increased autonomy for governors—noting your point about training, support and so on—include greater involvement in the appointment of prison staff?

Dame Anne Owers: It is certainly something that prison governors have said they would like to be more involved in. I can see the point: in other institutions, such as schools, you get to choose the people who work for you. There also needs to be a different kind of interviewing for prison staff, because an awful lot of prison staff drop out very early on. People genuinely do not realise what they are getting into and an experienced governor would probably be able to ask the right questions.

The Chair: Our next question is quite lengthy, but I will leave Lord Anderson to take us through it.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: The question is about oversight. This committee found last year that the recommendations of the chief inspector are often disregarded, even if, as I think you were hinting, they may have been accepted in theory. It also found that the capacity for the independent monitoring boards to deliver robust and consistent oversight had diminished. I wondered whether you had any thoughts on those conclusions. Do you agree with them? What do you think perhaps ought to be done to remedy them, if you do agree?

If that was not enough, can I add a final curveball—something that this committee has been thinking about in a slightly different context? Do you think there is still any place for Crown immunity in our Prison Service?

Dame Anne Owers: There has always been a tension in oversight between effectiveness and independence. If you are going to be independent, you will not be recommending things that the service can immediately do or does not want to do sometimes. You would not be independent if all you were doing was saying how things should be and how things are. For that reason, Chief Inspectors of Prisons have always come from outside the prison service and have always been genuinely independent. You are not tied to what the system can currently do, which creates a tension, because you sometimes recommend things that cannot happen immediately.

However, there ought to be a rather more effective mechanism of testing what actually has been done in relation to inspection reports. You talked about the number of reviews and so on. Some sort of formal reporting of what has actually happened, whether to this House or the other place, would be incredibly helpful. That is one of the reasons why my review talks about some sort of body looking forward at what is happening. All inspectorates do, if they do it well, is to tell you what is happening now and what the consequences of your actions have been. They tell you what is happening at the time, not what might happen.

There are some genuine difficulties with independent monitoring boards. Recruitment to a voluntary, unpaid role will always be difficult. The advantage of independent monitoring boards is that they are there all the time; they really ought to be a very effective complement to the inspectorate, because they are permanent. They can tell you what the trajectory of a prison is as opposed to taking a deep dive at one particular moment. Having people there on the ground feeds in very well to occasional inspecting. The IMB’s most recent annual report is a really good description of what is happening just now in the prison system. Working together, those two bits of inspection can do all that inspection can do, which is to tell you what is actually happening.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: What about Crown immunity?

Dame Anne Owers: I am surprised by a couple of things from looking at Crown immunity. I am surprised, for example, that the police and the NHS do not have Crown immunity. I am also surprised that private sector prisons do not. It seems rather strange to the point of being really difficult to defend that, for those lucky enough to be in a private prison, it has to comply with the Crown Premises Fire Safety Inspectorate. In a public sector prison, they can say, “Well, we might do something about it, but we have not done anything about it yet”.

I noticed there were nine step away notices recently, including two in a young offender institution. I know this committee has taken a lot of interest in fire safety, which is one of the huge risks, but there are others such as legionella and radon. These are potentially very risky places, so I struggle to see why there should still be Crown immunity. With other public sector bodies, there are sort of reduced fines—you do not reduce the capacity of the body to be able to act by enforcing, but it concentrates the mind wonderfully.

The Chair: The large number of reviews that have taken place recently about one aspect or another of prisons have already been referred to. We have also had annual reports from the Chief Inspector of Prisons and the probation reports. All of this is happening, but I think you implied that not a lot has changed. I can also tell you that, sadly—our committee adviser just looked up the figures—in fact, there has been no change in the return rate of prisoners over the last 30-odd years. Apart from a slight blip during Covid, it has remained remarkably consistent.

What is your view about there being all these reports and yet everything remaining much the same? What can we do to give reports from the chief inspector and from committees like this and others more teeth to get some changes made?

Dame Anne Owers: I wish I had a concise answer to this, and I am not sure I do. Reviews almost always follow a disaster rather than preventing it. The trick is to try to make sure that those preventive mechanisms exist. As I said in answer to the previous question, I do not think you can have an enforcement role because otherwise you could not point to what is good and necessary rather than what is possible. Certainly there could be greater accountability to the Prison Service for not saying, “In principle, we accept this” when, in practice, nothing has happened, as I said.

There are lots of good organisations in the voluntary sector which point to things that go wrong. I am very tired of hearing that lessons have been learned. I wish they had, but I do not think you would pass the GCSE if that was the way you learned lessons. As I said, there should be a more active role for the chief inspector in the Prison Service and reporting to Parliament on recommendations and what has happened. Sometimes these things take time, but it simply is not enough.

The Chair: Can I just take you back to the question Lord Anderson raised earlier about Crown immunity? Do you believe—and I think this is what you implied in your answer—that, if we removed Crown immunity from public prisons, it would help to increase accountability?

Dame Anne Owers: Inevitably it would. As I say, I think it concentrates the mind wonderfully. If it did not, we would not have a criminal justice system. There cannot be no penalty whatever for things that are unsafe and potentially catastrophic. As and when a catastrophe happens, as we found in other areas of public life, everyone wrings their hands and says, “Why did not we know? Why did not we do something about it beforehand?” This is a way of doing something about it beforehand.

The Chair: We have a few minutes left and I know that one or two colleagues wish to come in.

Baroness Berridge: You mentioned earlier about planning and the third sector being involved in planning. Just as background, years ago I used to be involved in a charity called Kainos that merged with Langley House Trust because we could not manage the big procurement that the MoJ needed. What is happening to the third sector’s involvement in rehabilitation, in terms of its capacity and involvement in our prison estate?

Dame Anne Owers: It has not been easy. One of the things that I found a bit disappointing in the questions that Amber Rudd’s review was asking was that there was no mention at all of the third sector. But I understand that she has reached out to third sector organisations and is doing something with them. It tends to be, “Oh, yes, and”, whereas the third sector can be influential, because it has those outside links that prison staff and sometimes probation staff do not have. They provide that really essential link. I used to be very depressed when I went into prisons: I would very often commend a particularly good and effective model that was being used, usually with third-sector involvement, and go back next time and it had gone, because there were not the resources or they could not get the staff for it. We have got to think of the third sector as more than an add-on—“You can do this for us”—and actually learn from what the third sector knows and can do well. They have got to be a genuine partner. Partnerships are talked about a lot, but they very often mean, “We talk. You do.”

The Chair: I call Lord Empey. Then we will have Baroness Buscombe and then, I am afraid, we will have to bring it to a close.

Lord Empey: Good morning. At the very beginning of your answers you referred to our lengths of sentencing. That is of course a political decision, basically. It would be nice to know the evidence between long and short and so on, but the point is: if you have longer sentences and a high rate of reoffending, and if you then shorten the sentences and the high rate of reoffending is not curbed, in fact you are not actually solving anything. What is your assessment then of what we do about the sentencing? We seem to be a bit of an outlier in western countries. I am just wondering whether it is simply a political reaction: “Lock them up, throw away the key”, and all that sort of thing? What assessment have you made of that? Would shorter sentences in fact make any difference to the number of people in prison?

Dame Anne Owers: Perhaps I could answer that in two different ways. One is that, actually, short sentences really do not work but, in order not to have them, you have to invest in what I call “not prison”. The idea is that there will not be short sentences and that things will be dealt with by the Probation Service, but that is no good. You are going to have to do a heck of a lot of investment, because those are the people with the problems that we just talked about that need dealing with.

In terms of sentencing, one of the things that I am intrigued by is the notion of early release, which is the way that we have dealt with increasing sentence lengths and prison capacity. I wish that we would not talk about early release and that we could talk about conditional release, because it has always been the case that most people will not serve in prison the sentence of the court that they were given. Way back in the 19th century, we had tickets of leave. We used to have remission for good behaviour immediately after the Second World War. Quintin Hogg—Lord Hailsham, as he then was not—and Roy Jenkins joined together on the 1967 Criminal Justice Bill, which I was intrigued by. It said that prisoners should serve a third of their sentence in prison, a third of their sentence on parole—having just created the Parole Board—and a third of the sentence remitted. Does that sound awfully familiar? It sounds exactly like David Gauke nearly 70 years later.

I wish we could then talk about a structured sentence, the structure critically depending on doing something effective in prison and doing something effective in the community. We vary between people spending half of their sentence in prison, 40% in prison and a third in prison. For the public, it just looks as if it is completely random and—as it is—being driven by capacity and not by effectively dealing with people. We could have a system up front that said, “This is the effective way of dealing with people, and some of it will be in prison and some of it will be in the community”. I do not think this constant moving around does anything for public confidence.

Baroness Buscombe: I shall be very quick. You talked about the enormous advantage of trying to focus on prevention. Some of the future feels a bit bleak and possibly catastrophic. In our report, we did have quite a lot of evidence which showed that there is a huge increase in violence within prisons—that being a reason why people are banged up for 22 hours at a time, because it is just too tough on the staff—violence against staff, and drones with drugs coming in. Do we need to be a bit more brutal in explaining to the Government just what is happening in our prisons? As well, there are those who are due to be deported on release being released because the Home Office has not got around to organising their papers, for example. The public, in other words, need to hear a response from Government on some of these tough areas.

Dame Anne Owers: You are right about safety. Prisons are less safe than they used to be; that is certainly the case. That has two aspects. One is violence in prison. Assaults have been increasing, although they seem to have plateaued recently. The other is suicide and self-harm. Self-harm rates have rocketed in recent years; they have always been high in women’s prisons—higher than they should be. Safety takes in both of those aspects—the safety of prisoners and staff from each other, but also safety from yourself—given overcrowding and just dealing with today. The MoJ has done an interesting study which shows that, in prisons that have more purposeful activity, violence goes down. Who knew? Who has ever dealt with adolescents, teenagers, and young men and women? It all hangs together. If there are drugs in the prison also, that is going result in debts, and debts lead to violence.

The Chair: All that is a rather depressing note to end on, although I did note the suggestion that we be brutal with the Government, having talked about violence in prison. I am not quite sure about that. It has been an enormously helpful session based on your very many years of experience in the field, for which many thanks for all you have done. On behalf of the entire committee, thank you very much indeed. Order, order. The public evidence session is now concluded.
