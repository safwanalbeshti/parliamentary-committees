Examination of witnesses

Ian Lawrence and Ben Cockburn.

The Chair: Good morning, everybody. Welcome to this meeting of the Justice and Home Affairs Committee, which is doing a follow-up on our earlier work on electronic monitoring. We are absolutely delighted to have our two guests before us. Before we go any further, I would be grateful if you could both introduce yourselves.

Ian Lawrence: Thank you, Lord Chair. I am the general secretary of Napo, which is the trade union representing probation and family court staff.

Ben Cockburn: I am the national chair of Napo. I am a probation officer. Mine is an elected role from within union membership. Thanks.

The Chair: Thank you very much. I understand you are both happy to be called Ben and Ian. Well, you might be Ben, you could be Ian, but not both Ben and Ian. We will kick off with a question from Lord Moraes.

Lord Moraes: Good morning to you both and thank you for your time this morning. As both of you know, two weeks ago HM Inspectorate of Probation published its thematic report on electronic monitoring. What is your brief response to that report?

Ian Lawrence: I will start and Ben will want to have some input as well. Nothing in this report, and indeed nothing in previous reports we have seen, such as the NAO report into electronic monitoring or previous reports done by the inspectorate of probation, has given us any surprises. It depressingly paints a consistent picture of poor service delivery and mixed impact in how it operates and the effect on staff, and indeed clients.

Ben Cockburn: We support significant aspects of the report. We agree that we are weak on governance of where HMPPS is and on accountability, as well as how the systems are co-ordinated across agencies. As a practitioner, more importantly for me it speaks to the issue of what electronic monitoring is actually for and what we are using it for.

I think that confusion goes right the way through the system. We see that with practitioners as well because we are really not clear on what we are using this for. Is it rehabilitation? Is it some sort of public protection? Is it a control mechanism or is it a mix of all of those things?

Lord Moraes: I do not have a supplementary because I think the rest of the questions go more deeply into that.

The Chair: Thank you very much. In that case, Baroness Hughes.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Good morning. We understand that just under 12% of the people being supervised by the Probation Service at the moment have been fitted with a tag. How do you describe what proportion of your workload, as opposed to the percentage of individuals, is taken up by supervising people on tags? Arising from that, what are the major challenges in supervising these offenders?

Ben Cockburn: The issue we have at the minute is that things are extremely uncertain post-October 2026, with the introduction of those provisions of the Sentencing Act. The old understandings may be shifting quite rapidly as electronic monitoring is expanded massively through the system. All of those workload assumptions are kind of falling by the wayside now and we are trying to get to grips with exactly what we are trying to deal with.

Those sorts of large-scale changes are really important for us because the way the system is set up at the minute is that lots of work defaults to probation practitioners. Notifications about risk, updates, variations of licences—all of those things come back to individual probation officers. If you massively expand the provision of electronic monitoring, by definition that workload also massively expands.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Do I take that to mean that any glitch in the system, any delay in fitting the tag, any uncertainty about the location of an offender or any concern about the victims, all comes back to the probation officer to assure or satisfy themselves and follow up?

Ben Cockburn: Yes. That is the policy now and we expect it to continue. The issue is that if a probation practitioner was putting together a plan to manage risk in the community, and a key part of that risk management plan is about any form of electronic monitoring, when that is not available it is down to the individual probation practitioner, because of the way HMPPS has set this up, to come up with alternatives as best they can. That might be increased reporting. It might be reaching out to police colleagues to ask them if they can do checks, which is increasingly difficult given the organisational staffing there. What this comes down to is that it falls to us to come up with the alternatives and all too often, unfortunately, that falls to individual practitioners and their managers.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Accepting that the situation is going to change in ways that you are not yet fully clear about because of the uncertainty, would you say that the workload involved with supervising people on tags now is disproportionate to that with other offenders? Do you expect that disproportionality to get worse?

Ben Cockburn: I expect some sort of disproportionality to get worse. It is difficult because every individual we supervise comes with a range of needs. There might be somebody who does not have electronic monitoring as a feature of their probation but they have another range of needs that makes them more complex. As a general view, if you are adding anything as electronic monitoring, it falls back to probation to deal with it if the provision is not there as it is meant to be. Even if it is there as it is meant to be, that is still an additional workload for us.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Finally, by when and by whom would you expect the uncertainty to be clarified—certainly the implications for the Probation Service—with the massive expansion in electronic monitoring that is coming down the line?

Ben Cockburn: I would have hoped that, as a responsible agency, HMPPS would have had that in place before October when this changed. Every day that goes past, where practitioners are left with uncertainty, is just a day too long. This should have been arranged well before now and making sure that the provision is there in almost every case. That it is not there fundamentally falls on the head of HMPPS, for me.

The Chair: Can I pick up on some of the details? We will come on later to talk about Serco and its delivery and relationship with the police and so on. We will come to all of that later. I want to be clear: one of the concerns that this committee has had in the past is that where the Probation Service is responsible for somebody who has been released from prison on licence and they breach that licence, very often the response of the probation officer has, basically, been to send them back to prison quickly because that saves them having to sort out all the details. Putting people back in prison has been the easy way of getting a problem out of the way. You might dispute that, but that is the evidence we have received. My question then is: on the responsibility of the Probation Service for monitoring those on tagging, is there a similar issue that we should be aware of?

Ben Cockburn: The biggest issue that we have is that we are dealing with an external agency, one where Napo’s policy is that it should be insourced. We struggle with similar issues with communication. We are trying to communicate a lot of information out there and duplicating work that is already available on probation systems, whereas with an insourced model that would be available already to the electronic monitoring provider, because they would be part of the Probation Service under our policy.

The Chair: We will pick that up again a little later on when we come to discuss Serco and so on. We will move to Lord Tope.

Lord Tope: The MoJ is currently conducting a pilot at six prisons involving tagging on release, as you obviously know. What is your involvement with this pilot and what are your members saying about it?

Ian Lawrence: Thanks, my Lord. Very little, to be quite honest.

Lord Tope: Your involvement is very little?

Ian Lawrence: Our involvement is very little.

Lord Tope: Your members are saying very little?

Ian Lawrence: Yes, because we have pilot schemes running. We are waiting for the full evaluation reports to be published. We would have welcomed the opportunity to see those in advance. We have not yet.

Lord Tope: Is there a reason why you are not yet involved?

Ian Lawrence: We will find out what the reason is. We were told about this literally back last week, Ben?

Ben Cockburn: Yes.

Ian Lawrence: We were told that this announcement was due and in mid-December there would be a further rollout. We have had very little opportunity to analyse that and certainly no data yet for us to look at in detail.

Ben Cockburn: The install at source model potentially solves one issue by fitting a physical tag on to a person. It does not necessarily solve it where a unit also has to be fitted at an address, because a visit still has to be made to that address. It will be interesting from the pilot to see what proportion of monitoring units are not being fitted and why. Without it, it renders the whole purpose pointless because you have a tag that cannot be monitored. That is one thing we are looking for when pilots report and do evaluation studies.

Lord Tope: Do you expect that you will have a greater involvement as it develops with the opportunity to make your comments about it?

Ben Cockburn: It is primarily something that we will see happening rather than being involved in. It will take place at the prison and prison staff will be facilitating that for the electronic monitoring service. We will see it happening but we will not necessarily have any role in doing it.

Ian Lawrence: I want to add that we, as trade unionists, are also cognisant of the cost to the taxpayer. It is not just about our members but what the impact on the taxpayer is. If this scheme reduces costs and creates some effectiveness, great. As Ben has said, it is not so straightforward as just fitting a tag and that is it.

The Chair: In the generality, and bearing in mind what you have just said, what is your relationship with the MoJ?

Ian Lawrence: The MoJ relationship is conducted via senior HMPPS leadership. We do come into contact with senior MoJ officials from time to time. It is fair to say that, as far as Napo is concerned, our relationship is not on a very good footing at the moment. Back in the summer, our national executive committee passed a motion of no confidence in HMPPS senior leadership over pay and workloads. Sadly, we are at a place where we are now balloting our members for the prospect of industrial action on both those issues, with the result expected at the back end of this week. We still maintain contact. We still seek to engage constructively where we can. It does not mean we are taking the ball home, but the fact that we have reached this stage is an indication that things are not quite good.

The Chair: Thank you. I just wanted you to have the opportunity to put that on record.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: This is a general question, but I am perplexed by the number of your implied criticisms of HMPPS, MoJ and so on. Where do your Chief Probation Officer and her senior managers sit in the realm of governance, alongside HMPPS and MoJ? What contact and discussions do you have with her and her colleagues about these issues?

Ben Cockburn: We have regular meetings with the Chief Probation Officer. We also have systems through the trade union recognition and consultation methods to have regular meetings with them, so we raise issues as we go. I know that tagging is one that has been raised previously. When there were some high-profile media stories about tagging—I think it was last year—we were involved in discussions with them about trying to push for our members’ interests in not taking on additional work, because of the failures of tagging companies. On where that lies, as far as I am aware, that should probably come from HMPPS but I do not think that the Chief Probation Officer holds direct responsibility for the electronic monitoring contracts—it lies elsewhere in HMPPS.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Presumably you would take to the Chief Probation Officer the issue that you have raised that you and your members were not involved, for instance, in the pilot and do not know what the evaluation and all of that shows. Does she have some leverage?

Ben Cockburn: Or elsewhere within HMPPS, we would take it to people direct. We have done that and said that when the evaluation reports and pilots are done, if this is to be rolled out more widely, we would expect to see that.

Ian Lawrence: It is worth adding that it is often the prison-focused initiatives are those we get to hear about, rather than ones that we are intrinsically involved in. There could be legitimate reasons for that. However, we need to be on the same page on the big issues so that we are aware of what is coming down the track and, ultimately, our members should be aware as well. I have nothing to add to Ben’s analysis of the issue, but we would like to have seen a much greater collective approach on this issue.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: As a comment—you may have something to say about it—but you say “prison-focused”. Obviously, the tagging starts with somebody being released from prison but once they are out, it is the Probation Service’s responsibility, so it is not really just prison-focused, is it?

Ian Lawrence: Sorry, if I may clarify, it was the installation at source programme and that aspect of it.

Ben Cockburn: The more general view is that the policy of Napo now is to have electronic monitoring insourced in the Probation Service and we see the link. If you are going to increase expansion to the way it is now, which looks massively different from the way it did 25 or 30 years ago, we think that it has to form a central part of the Probation Service.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Thank you. That is clear.

The Chair: We will pick that up in a bit more detail shortly.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: In March, the Government announced £100 million to fund what they called “the expansion of tagging”. What is your view of that uplift and how do you think that money would best be spent?

Ian Lawrence: Ben has alluded to the fact that we think the whole programme should be insourced. On a wider point to your question, where is the money for this? The question our members pose to us regularly is: why is that money not reaching the front face in a way that makes a difference to workloads and encourages people, first, to make a career in probation and, secondly, to stay in probation?

The attrition rates in the Probation Service, because of existing workloads—let alone the pressures that we are talking about here—are in a crisis state. It is not unusual for probation delivery units to have a 30% deficit in their staffing. People are reporting serious mental health issues when they are ill, with the highest proportion of sickness absence through mental health anywhere in the Civil Service, so everything is not rosy. Unless there is an investment of serious cash in pay and workloads, we will see that attrition worsen and members put under more and more pressure. There has to be a more transparent use of the money in a way that we feel confident will make a difference.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: As far as you can judge from the Government’s press release, they seem to have in mind that this money would be used to fund a presumption that all prisoners will be tagged on release, a pilot to tag domestic abusers, a pilot to tag convicted burglars and thieves. I think what you are saying to us is “We can’t run before we can walk” and that this money needs to be urgently directed towards improving the pay and conditions of your members.

Ben Cockburn: There is very little purpose to tagging somebody if there is no probation officer to do anything with that information. If you have vacancy rates as high as we do—we have probation officer rates across the country as one in five probation officers missing and the loss of experienced staff. In some probation offices, the most experienced member of staff there has been qualified for a year or less. That is a massive issue.

I think the wider issue around this expansion of tagging, though, is: what are we using tagging for? The key part is there is a press release and what are we using this for? Is this again about rehabilitation? Is it about control? Is it about some sort of replication of a prison system within the community? My personal view is that that is a crazy idea because those two things are very different.

The issue we keep coming back to, though, is that there is investment in technology—almost a fetishisation of technology—in the Ministry of Justice and all the things that it can bring, which we are not necessarily against. We just think it is sometimes overstated and missing the point: that, essentially, probation practice is about building human relationships with people and you need people there to be able to do that. All the technology in the world is not going to do that for you.

Lord Bach: As I understand it, the £100 million to support tagging expansion is coming out of the £700 million that the Government have offered for probation during the period up to 2029. Are you surprised that it comes out of the £700 million? I think I know the answer to this one: are you happy that it comes out of that? What could the possible justification be for it to come out of what some people would say is a pretty small amount anyway?

Ben Cockburn: And an amount that certainly does not replicate the amount of money that has been taken out of the Probation Service in the preceding years and out of the reach of HMPPS as well. The issue again is that we cannot get significant clarity on what that £700 million will be spent on. Obviously our focus is on recruiting and, especially, retaining staff. If this is being announced in the middle of the spending review period, I imagine it will be paid from different budgets. I think it is for HMPPS to be clear: if it is not spending this £100 million on something, because it is being put towards expanding tagging, what is not going to be funded as a result? We cannot get answers to that. When we have asked HMPPS previously, as part of pay negotiations, what it is planning to spend that £700 million increase on, we have not received answers about exactly what that is.

Ian Lawrence: On a wider point you will know, my Lord, that the Probation Service was part-privatised some 13 years ago—a disastrous social experiment of the highest order. That was reversed even by the Administration that conducted it, which was welcome. But at the time, and consistently since, we have asked where the investment is to support that welcome move and it has not come. At a time when suddenly there is money available, I do not think it is unreasonable for us to argue strongly, on behalf of our members, that that must be put into their pay and ways of reducing their workloads, so that we can sustain and build the service again. This is not just a trade union coming here asking for more money. We have a deeply felt conviction that we need to rebuild the Probation Service and the last Administration—and to an extent, this one—have not reacted in the way we want.

Lord Hogan-Howe: Good morning. We have recently been told that Serco has consistently met its contractual requirements on electronic monitoring since March 2026, so this year. Are probation staff working effectively with Serco? Given reports about the inconsistencies with tagging, are the contractual requirements therefore up to scratch?

Ben Cockburn: The issue remains that as long as Serco is a separate agency, there will be issues with communication. We see that repeatedly with the duplication of information being communicated. For instance, Serco will not necessarily communicate breaches of tagging to a particular officer. It will go to a mailbox, it is then sent to one person and you can see straightaway how those lines of communication might break down.

On whether Serco is meeting its contractual requirements, that is reflected in the HMIP report and the previous National Audit Office report and what they say about whether that is happening. Certainly from members’ points of view, there is an ongoing frustration around tags not being fitted, visits not being done; that has consistently come back from members right through several years and over different contracts. That is probably where members are at on whether Serco is meeting its contractual requirements.

Lord Hogan-Howe: Ian, you shook your head when I said about the March figures being an improvement.

Ian Lawrence: I allude to what Ben has just said. I see Mr Jones has joined us as well and you will be hearing from him later. His reports point out, as I touched on earlier, the sheer waste of money to the taxpayer as a result of Serco’s non-performance. Equipment can be sent to a venue and never fitted because the person is not there, along with the problems we have had in picking up accurate monitoring and data.

The other aspect that does not get a lot of attention is: what happens to that equipment once the period of use is spent? Reports suggest it is just languishing in the ether somewhere. There are an awful lot of questions to be raised about public procurement and the use of taxpayers’ money, and we care strongly about that.

Lord Hogan-Howe: As a final question on this, Ben, you mentioned a really important point earlier: a lack of strategy about what this thing is intended for—the thing that is already there—which is therefore amplified by having a huge rollout without a strategy. That is quite worrying. Serco must have written something down that said, “This is what is intended”, but your feeling is it is not working.

Ben Cockburn: Thinking back to when I trained—it was 2001 to 2003, so it is a lifetime ago—we had a really clear understanding of using electronic monitoring and curfews at that point for particular reasons. You were breaking up associations, restricting the ability to commit criminal activities, doing it towards rehabilitative work and all those sorts of things. We are not seeing that currently. We are seeing the idea of it is being used almost to justify the early release scheme and put extra licence conditions in place, as opposed to seeing a more positive impact that we know is there.

Research tells us that if electronic monitoring is used properly and selectively, it can bring results in reducing reoffending, but the thing that surprised me was that I do not remember seeing the Government actually promoting that research when they did this. They have not said, “Research shows that there are fewer victims and improvements in people’s behaviour on probation because we are using electronic monitoring judiciously”. Instead, the approach has been, “Just throw everything at the wall and hope it sticks”. That is quite concerning to a probation practitioner who wants to be led by the evidence that is there.

Lord Hogan-Howe: A system that is being run by email still, really.

Ben Cockburn: Yes, as opposed to somebody sitting in the same office as you and discussing what the risks are with them, before they go out and visit the property. That is preferable to sending an email off detailing the risk and hoping that the person who is doing a visit at 11 pm, to somewhere they have never been before, reads that email properly and is happy with the contents.

The Chair: In our earlier report, this committee suggested that the Government should consider either the introduction of more competition into the provision of the tagging service or should seriously consider bringing it in-house. My understanding is that your union is very much in favour of the latter. Could you quickly answer the simple question: why do you believe bringing it in-house would remove some of the problems that we already have with the Serco contract, for example the KPI that says it is about attempting to fit a tag rather than succeeding in fitting one and so on? Those are the same problems that the Probation Service would have if it were brought in-house, are they not?

Ian Lawrence: Ben will cover the technicalities but on a principled position, we have long argued that insourcing contractors of this nature is the right approach. This ties in with our overall campaign to see a more joined-up criminal justice system in any event. If this were introduced, there would be improved management and accountability, and I think—for the reasons that Ben will talk about—a better performance between practitioners and the operatives.

Ben Cockburn: Not to speak to particular KPIs, but the important thing is that they are about improved communication, and that is the real benefit here. It is also about accountability. If there was a level of accountability, especially if there was a greater level of local democratic control over probation services, is that not again what we want to see, to move away from HMPPS to a system more potentially in line with youth justice—

The Chair: I am sorry to interrupt. We looked at this in some detail, and I just want to ask a very specific question. You can probably guess what our view as a committee has generally been. We are fairly supportive of what you are proposing, but we need to look into some of the things that are stated as the benefits. If the Probation Service takes it all over, you say there will be “improved communication”. However, surely there will still be the problem that a central control system, now run by the Probation Service, identifies that somebody with a tag on is where they should not be, for example. That is notified to the relevant probation officer who may be off sick, not on duty, on holiday or whatever. That is causing a problem with communication at the moment from Serco to the Probation Service. Would that not still apply? How do you get round it in a way that Serco could not get round it by doing a deal with you?

Ben Cockburn: Well, you get round it by having the electronic monitoring operatives located in the exact same office as the probation staff who are supervising the people involved. You do it that way, so you are not relying on email exchanges. You would do it in exactly the same way as we run unpaid work, for instance. Where unpaid work supervisors come in, you can talk to people on unpaid work about what the issues are with the people who are coming on to the groups. If somebody comes on with a particular history, you can discuss all that. That is the key thing. The assumption there is that we retain the same model of flawed email exchanges and third-party ways of doing things. If we move to a truly local model, that is the key.

The Chair: Thank you. That is very helpful, but you also raise the issue of better communication across the whole criminal justice system. We will move on to that with Baroness Buscombe.

Baroness Buscombe: Thank you, Ian and Ben. I come to a critical link between the police and the Probation Service. Whichever of you would like to begin, how effectively do you feel our police and probation are working together in managing electronic monitoring and what improvements could there be? We said in our report that we noted that national guidance was limited on how you should work together and that there are different systems, processes and fieldwork for the forces. It varied considerably, making it very difficult for yourselves and the police.

Ben Cockburn: I think we are working as effectively as we can, given some of the barriers that are in place—barriers because we do not necessarily cover the same areas geographically. We also do not have the same systems in place and there are significant issues in exchanging that information. I have to be honest: I struggle to see a way around that with the different roles that the police and probation have, and the different levels of access to information that the police have compared to the Probation Service. It is an ongoing issue. Again, the improvements we would be looking at are in trying to increase communication and moving to some sort of a common platform. On how to exchange communication, I am not sure if we are ever going to get there with the very different security requirements that the police would have, as opposed to the Probation Service.

Ian Lawrence: Thank you very much for your question. We are not here, obviously, to look at the police side of the operation. But I have read the report, and I cannot help noticing that it is far from effective within the police force in terms of monitoring, management and joined-up communication systems. That is not necessarily their fault. They too are under severe pressure, by way of the early release scheme and the EM requirements that follow. A more holistic approach is definitely required.

Baroness Buscombe: Could one suggest, therefore, that having fewer separate forces could make a difference? I find it staggering that the most basic communication is so mixed and different, depending on which police force you are talking to. Do you see hope in that reducing the number of forces should help improve communications and working together?

Ben Cockburn: It is not necessarily about the reduction in forces but, if that were not to take place, there would definitely need to be improvements made in the standards of information and an agreement between the police and probation services about what standard of information was exchanged and in what time could people expect to receive responses. If there was not that sort of reduction, there would need to be at least that level of agreement on what each side could expect from each other.

The Chair: Perhaps not surprisingly, in view of his work in this area, Lord Hogan-Howe wishes to intervene.

Lord Hogan-Howe: If I may declare an interest, I have just done a review for the Home Secretary about the number of forces. One of the things that crosses over for probation is that you describe a system in which the Probation Service struggles to get the information it needs through an email. The police get it one step removed. There is geofencing on tags and that might indicate a rapist is near to where a rape has just happened—not two days ago or two weeks ago. There are domestic violence victims who have geofencing around where they live.

I am going to be careful about this, but there is a danger that everybody treats new electronic things as a burden, not a gift, and therefore there is no new vision that says, “We need to change the way we do things together”. Is there that joint approach with police, the Home Office and the Ministry of Justice to get the most out of this thing, rather than seeing it as a real pain that, for good reason, is just not working?

Ian Lawrence: That goes back to the issue of people having very valuable conversations. Clearly, government departments and the Civil Service are not having those discussions at the appropriate level, and it is difficult. Everybody is vying for resources and has their own priorities, but the bigger picture has to be taken into account. We are talking about one single issue here, electronic monitoring and the ways it could be improved for the benefits it will bring to society. There is a huge wider problem of interdepartmental in-communication, let me put it that way. That has to be rectified.

The Chair: Thank you. Time is very tight, so I will ask colleagues for fairly brief questions and fairly brief answers if we can.

Lord Dubs: We understand that restriction and exclusion zones have been recently introduced. How do you think they are working in practice? I will add my supplementary: what are the processes associated with them and are they working?

Ben Cockburn: I think that the issue of restriction zones in particular was novel. As a probation practitioner, I have some concerns over restriction zones. We seem to be moving away from trying to keep people away from a particular area to confining people in a particular area. I can think of repeated examples from people I have worked with where it is not necessarily a good thing for people to go back to the place that they have always been—where they were raised and offended—and all those kinds of issues. There are some challenges with how we implement restriction zones.

The biggest issue, from lots of the publicity that came out before these changes were made, was the idea that we might be mis-selling what exclusion zones and restriction zones can do. If somebody breaches them, a probation worker is not going to find out until, potentially, the day after or a day after that, so we are reacting to information as opposed to watching things happen in real time. That is part of my concern about how these are being sold, especially to victims—if I can put it that way—about how effective they are. I worry that victims are getting a very false sense of security about what technology can bring people. With the best will in the world, probation staff do not work 24/7. Those involved in what they call sentence management tend to work Monday to Friday from about 9 am to about 5 pm, and occasionally a couple of hours later in the night. If an electronic monitoring breach is after that, we do not find out there and then. We find out a day or two later.

The Chair: On that, can I be absolutely clear—and then I will bring in Baroness Berridge—that with your description of bringing it in-house at a local level, you still have the problem of there not being probation officers on duty in large numbers, for instance at nighttime and weekends.

Ben Cockburn: We would have a system in approved premises where we use out-of-hours recall systems. It would be down to HMPPS to have a discussion with people, such as the trade unions, to talk about varying terms and conditions of service to make sure we have probation staff on duty at different times of day to match that. That is a discussion for after insourcing has taken place but it is definitely a possibility. In approved premises and for out-of-hours recall—

The Chair: I just want to be clear that the benefits of in-housing still have problems unless additional funding is made available, effectively. Yes.

I have one question about capacity and one about resilience. I think you mentioned having one in five vacancies and the mental health issues of your staff. What is overall capacity actually running at in the system? You may need to write to us but you have people off on sick leave or compassionate leave and there are holidays, obviously. I think that is really important and relates to resilience.

Your members are very front line, but we have heard about hacking. We have heard about the power grid being vulnerable. This may be for Mr Jones rather than you, but what is the confidence for the public? If this system goes down, are we thinking about resilience in this system? Are your members concerned about that at all?

Ben Cockburn: We have no way of accurately discussing what the capacity of the Probation Service is because in late September, the employer withdrew a workload measurement tool a matter of days before the changes took place from the Sentencing Act. Even our own employer cannot tell us what it thinks an acceptable level of workload is, which is one of the main drivers towards the industrial action that we are balloting for currently.

Baroness Berridge: Can I just clarify? When you say “workload”, is that a word meaning workload per person or about how many people are on duty within the system?

Ben Cockburn: It is about the number of cases that people are supervising at any one time.

Ben Cockburn: We have the figures on staffing, but we will have to write to you to confirm those, as well as the people who are on probation. We have seen that increase to more than a quarter of a million recently.

The Chair: I think we have received some fairly detailed statistics about the figure.

Lord Empey: Good morning. What impact is the early release scheme having on your service?

Ben Cockburn: Devastating. It is a devastating impact. We have seen a run-up that has been—I think it is diplomatic to call it—chaotic and poorly planned. We have seen a massive, once-in-a-generation change to sentencing rolled out for a service that is in crisis. Learning and training have been incredibly poorly rolled out, to the point where staff are trying to deal with excessive workloads and coming to terms with hundreds of pages of guidance documents that we have all seen, which in some cases are being rewritten as political decisions have been taken over the summer. Even now, there are things being rolled out. All that is on top of a workload that for staff has simply been unsustainable for a number of years. I am not going to call it a perfect storm, but it is just an awful situation that is deteriorating and deteriorating.

Lord Empey: You mentioned retention issues in an earlier answer. What is the rate of churn and has it accelerated since the summer?

Ian Lawrence: You will hear other politicians talking about the 1,300 probation staff they are going to recruit this year. We obviously welcome and support that, but they do not talk about the equal or sometimes higher number of staff who leave the service. You cannot just pick up a probation officer off the supermarket shelf. It can take up to two years for them to be up and running in a fully effective way, because of the complexities of their work and public protection issues.

It goes back to the issues we have talked about before. The early release scheme you have alluded to is not the first. The previous Administration ran two more, with equally chaotic results in places. We also come at this by saying that there are too many people in our prisons. There are too many women in the prison estate—probably the highest number in Europe—and that is appalling. No Governments have got to grips with this. I can understand why they have enacted the early release schemes. In principle, anything that relieves the prison population is good, but it is no good if we cannot do the things that Ben and I have been talking about and our members cannot react in the way that the public wants.
