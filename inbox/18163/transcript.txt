Witnesses: Professor Jenny Gibson, Neil Leitch, and Richard Waite.

Q81 Chair: Good morning and welcome to this oral evidence session of the Education Select Committee. This morning, we are taking evidence on our inquiry into AI and edtech, thinking about the role and the impact of AI and edtech on the early years phase of education.

I thank our witnesses very much to for being here with us this morning. Can I ask each of you to introduce yourselves to the Committee, please, starting with Neil Leitch?

Neil Leitch: I am Neil Leitch, the chief executive of an organisation called the Early Years Alliance. We represent around 18,000 members and supporters, predominantly nursery groups and pre-schools, but some child minders as well.

The one thing that I would say is fairly unique about us as a membership organisation is that we also operate 28 settings exclusively in areas of deprivation, so I would like to think that we witness at first hand some of the wonders and the challenges of early years.

Professor Gibson: Hi everyone, I am Jenny Gibson, Professor of Neurodiversity and Developmental Psychology at the University of Cambridge. My expertise is in early childhood, particularly focusing on early language and communication development and children’s social and emotional wellbeing.

Richard Waite: My name is Richard Waite. I am an early years digital pedagogy and digital safeguarding consultant. My background is that I have been NNEB trained since 1986, so I have just under 40 years’ experience in early years, and I have gone from working in settings as management to support office for a large nursery group, and now I support nurseries as an adviser.

Q82 Chair: Thank you all very much indeed. I am going to begin our questioning this morning. First of all, could you describe the overall impact that AI and educational technology is currently having on the early years sector? Neil, I might start with you if that is okay.

Neil Leitch: From our perspective, particularly post-pandemic, we started to see more and more children arrive in our settings with social and emotional development delays. When you speak to providers and managers, they particularly quote AI and edtech as being one of the problems that they have. What is interesting is that, still to this day, we experience that. To put it bluntly, I wish I had £1 for every manager that has said to me that they had to spend their day pacifying staff who had been kicked, bitten, hair pulled, and so on, by children who are not socially and emotionally developed.

I will be very quick with this, but to put it into a very practical position, from January through to September we have recorded 43 accidents of staff in our 28 nurseries; 19 of those incidents related to children biting, kicking, throwing objects, scratching, pulling hair of staff, and so on. That is not even child on child. That is on adults. I would say that the big concern we have is basically child development, and children’s ability to socialise because of AI, edtech and, dare I say, sedentary lifestyles.

Chair: Thank you. Professor Gibson?

Professor Gibson: We have done some research in our research centre recently looking at the impact of AI-enabled toys. This is where there is a generative AI chatbot embedded in something that is cuddly looking, designed for children and marketed to under-fives. We were quite concerned about these kinds of developments, and we did some research that looked at the interactions of these toys with young children, and we sought parents’ views and educator views as well.

Particularly for the early years sector, people working as nursery practitioners or others in the sector told us that they were quite concerned about impacts of generative AI. They felt they did not have the knowledge and skills they needed, and they did not necessarily know where to turn for support or advice. On the educator side, there is a lot of uncertainty and quite a bit of worry.

On the parent and child side of things, we are seeing that it is ubiquitous. There is so much quick development in this area. From what we saw in this very initial study, very little research has been done on the impacts of generative AI on young children’s development that sits outside the tech development sector. We lack evidence of what impacts on child development. From the observational work that we have done so far, we did see that generative AI toy interactions were not particularly well calibrated to what we know is good and important for young children’s development. We have big social change happening and at the moment we do not necessarily have the evidence to know what exactly the impact is on children.

Chair: Thank you very much. Richard, what are you seeing at the moment?

Richard Waite: At the moment—thinking about day-to-day and boots on the ground—among the staff concerns that Neil has spoken about, it is the struggle with staffing, recruitment and getting the right type of staffing, these kinds of issues. Technology in early years has already been seen as an issue because most early years people, if you say the word “technology”, they immediately jump to the conclusion that you are going to talk about screen time.

If I am trying to deliver a workshop on technology, nine out of 10 people will not come to my workshop because they do not realise what I am actually talking about. They will think I am going to talk to them about tablets or whatever, so that is an issue and a big concern because screen time is across the media, social media, parent groups all talking about it.

It puts pressures on nurseries because they are thinking about that and now AI has impacted more. It has added to the mix. You not only have screen time, but you also have AI. Because of recent events in early years—even looking at cyber-security—there is this massive cloud of information that nurseries were just not prepared for. A nursery manager or an educator does not go in having knowledge about AI, cyber-security and screen time. When a manager is coming into a nursery at 7.30 in the morning and they have staff that have called in sick, the chef is not turning up—all these issues coming in—the last thing they are probably going to think about is AI, cyber-security and screen time.

Q83 Chair: Thank you very much. You have talked about the influence of AI from outside the setting. What types of educational technology are currently being used in early years settings? Is there any framework for that in terms of decision making and procurement in the early years, or any evidence base that underpins it that you are aware of? Again, I will start with Neil.

Neil Leitch: I am not sure there is a framework. It is very much an “introduce and test later” sort of approach that we are seeing at this point in time.

Within early years settings, I would say that it is used at the moment predominantly for administration, and it is fair to say that many educators value that. The problem that we have is it does not articulate any nuance whatsoever in terms of sensitivity. We are starting to see generated reports for example, that are very clinical. They do not basically take into account the nuance of a child’s personal attributes, and so on.

The other thing that we are starting to see is a plethora of AI generated correspondence from parents, whether that be complaints or otherwise. It has just meant a massive overload for early years educators. The response that they give, unlike maybe 10 years ago, which was to sit down and talk with a parent, now becomes a legal document predominantly generated by AI. That is where I would say it is generally being used.

That is not to say that we have not used it particularly within our settings to support speech and language, for example. There are some good products, but there are some awful products as well. I find it really interesting—to give an example—that if you were developing a piece of medical equipment for a hospital, it would be assumed that you would understand the consequences of that machine failing or the clinical steps that need to be taken subsequently. However, when it comes to edtech, for example, nothing. We just take it for granted that it is in the best interests of a child.

There are some very good manufacturers and producers of edtech equipment, so this is not about everybody, but we are also seeing a plethora of poorly constructed equipment being produced with no consideration for a child’s social and emotional development. That is a big concern, so what we are seeing is educators reluctant to use kit at this time. To take Richard’s point, their support and training is almost non-existent. They are very, very fearful. When you are fearful, you avoid. That is what has happening at this particular point in time.

Chair: Thank you. Professor Gibson?

Professor Gibson: I do not know of any frameworks in place around edtech governance. Obviously, though, we do have a lot of regulation around child protection and child support. I do think that early years’ educators know what is good for children in terms of social and emotional development because of the professional training that many early years sector people have.

We perhaps need to do a bit of confidence building that actually you know what is good for children in terms of their social development, the things that you might do with them, and then apply that to technology to say, “Is this supporting the human back and forth interactions that children need to develop and grow?” I think early years educators do have that set of expertise, and I think support for them to translate that into practice with edtech is interesting.

I have seen technology mainly applied for planning sessions and things like that. From my observation, it is more educator facing than child facing. I have seen things like Alexa being used in nurseries, and I think it is interesting that, because of the ubiquitous nature of these kinds of technologies, it is not always easy to disentangle whether or not this has a generative AI component. I think in some cases, people are using AI unawares.

Richard Waite: I think technology as a whole in the sector because people have become scared of the idea that it is screen time. When I worked in the last chain, I was supporting 310 nurseries and I was looking at integrating technology tools into nursery settings, from coding devices to digital microscopes, all these sorts of things. This is where there is a gap in the understanding that there are non-digital as well as digital tech resources.

The word “edtech” may work in schools, but I think in early years I prefer the term “digital pedagogy” because if you put “pedagogy” in there, sometimes there is more of an interest in, “Okay, digital pedagogy is to help with that”, because then we are making sure the pedagogy is there. “Edtech” does not really tell you that there is learning from such resources.

I support and work with a few companies that produce some very good non-digital plus digital tools, but due to the lack of knowledge and the fear in settings, their choice is, “We just will not bother. We will not get it in because the hassle of having to learn it and just figuring it all out, it is just too much for the sector at the moment,” so most of the time they are staying away.

Digital pedagogy was introduced in the “Birth to 5 Matters: non‑statutory guidance for the EYFS”, 2nd edition. I was actually on the working group for that. There was a need to put that in there because, as Neil said earlier, you cannot put the genie back in the bottle. We are in a digital age. Children are exposed to technology every day, from washing machines to microwaves and mobile phones. We cannot put it back, so we have to better prepare them for the future that they are facing.

Q84 Chair: Thank you. Finally from me, before I pass on to other members: do you think that the Government have fully grasped the scale of the impacts of this technology on the early years sector, and what further action do you think the Government should take to support the sector in navigating these very significant changes that you have all articulated really well? I will start with Neil.

Neil Leitch: I do not think they have. I find it really interesting that, when we are looking at safeguarding, if there is a physical connection, the Government are always very quick to respond. I understand that, whether that is about safer sleeping practices, eating, and so on. However, there is nothing when it comes to the emotional safeguarding of children that is in place.

I come back to the point, Chair, that our staff are greatly concerned that there is no support whatsoever. They don’t know how to deal with AI. They don’t know how to deal with the consequences when things go wrong. For example, I am amazed and appalled—this is not necessarily just in early years, but it moves into maybe further education—at the number of educators who don’t understand the pressures that children are being placed under from external influences. Their response, predominantly in the school environment, has been to exclude whatever it happens to be rather than understand. They are almost ignorant of what has led to this.

I am appalled by people who constantly say our children are less resilient; they are living in a different world. The Government need to ensure that educators are supported in not just understanding IT, but understanding children who are on the end of it, because that is not the case as it stands.

Professor Gibson: My view is that there could be more understanding and integration across sectors. The distinction between health and education in the early years is somewhat arbitrary. Services that support children and babies from a very young age, including social services, health visiting and so on, are part of that early education landscape because children are learning, and the home learning environment is very important and that might sit outside traditional educational settings.

It is important to encourage joint working and thinking, and I guess cross-sector responsibility as well. Top-down government regulation is not going to solve this. It is about taking some ownership as well as being part of those professions, and parents and families have all got a part to play.

Thinking about government regulation, I do think psychological safety should be an important part of product safety standards. I think there needs to be more exploration of that, particularly thinking about toys that are coming in with generative AI capabilities. One of the biggest risks there is the friendship-affirming nature of interactions that generative AI has, which might be acting at a time when children are particularly developmentally vulnerable, so I think product regulation with a view to safety is important in this early years picture.

The other thing I want to say is that I am glad we are here talking about the early years. It is often missing from these conversations, so I think keeping this sector part of conversations about educational technology is really important too.

Richard Waite: I want to share a survey Tapestry that did this year, which found that 46% of workplaces are using AI, 31% have AI guidance, but only 21% of settings have training. Training and CPD is the big thing for me; it has been a big thing for a long time. I have been in and out of nurseries for over three decades. I see the lack of support in terms of training and development for educators: we have apprentices coming in who are supposedly going to be our future educators who have had no training in their apprenticeship on AI, edtech or digital pedagogy, or cyber-security. So where do they get it from?

If they are doing their level twos, level threes or their apprenticeships and none of those three things is being discussed, they are being thrown into the lion’s den, basically, of all this with information overload. Where is the support? A 30-minute webinar or a staff meeting at the end of the day, when you are absolutely drained from running around after however many hundreds of children? I think they need some space; something needs to be done to allow educators to get proper support and training, and for the leaders as well. Managers and owners also need training.

Chair: Thank you very much. I am going to go to Peter Swallow next.

Q85 Peter Swallow: Professor Gibson, in your earlier answers, you identified your concerns about young people and young children’s learning and development. Could you set out in a little bit more detail what specifically you are concerned about? Is it their communication and language skills, their social and emotional development? What specific risks are there to young children’s development from AI and edtech?

Professor Gibson: Thanks for the question. One of the things that parents seemed excited about in our research is that, unlike some perceptions of screen time as being quite passive edtech, generative AI gives this possibility of a back-and-forth conversation. We have done a lot of work over the years in developmental psychology showing that those back-and-forth interactions are really the essence of young children’s development. Those early social relationships and bonds are the basis of later language and cognitive development, so they are absolutely critical.

What is worrying, when you are replacing a human in those interactive relationships, is that you are potentially missing that sensitivity. If you have, let’s say, a generative AI toy that will always tell you that you are right or tell you that it is a friend and you can tell it secrets, it is really quite worrying in terms of what the disruption in those reciprocal human relationships could be.

That is not to say that it would be impossible to produce a product that was more positive in terms of those relationships and interactions, but at the moment I think they are not at the heart of this development. What if we had products that supported perhaps bringing the adult into those interactions? We have seen some research—for example, from the University of Sheffield—where digital parenting interventions can really support parents in that back and forth with their child and supports their early language development. If we had AI products that could support that in a sensitive way, I would be really positive about it, but at the moment, we are not seeing products that are well calibrated to the social need that children have.

Q86 Peter Swallow: Without wanting to put words into your mouth, if I can summarise, the advantages that we might see in communication and language development come at a potential cost to social and emotional development; is that essentially what you are saying?

Professor Gibson: I think it would be premature to talk about any advantages yet. I have not seen a product that really convinces me of that, but potentially yes.

Q87 Peter Swallow: Mr Leitch, you mentioned that you were very concerned about children’s emotional development as well. What safeguards would you like to see put in place to make sure that we are protecting very young children from these risks?

Neil Leitch: I think that if we have product development and we have a body that regulates that product development, then exactly the same should apply for edtech and AI. Those organisations should be accountable to a learned body that understands the impact of their product. That would be at least a starting point.

I also think it is really important to understand that our educators are not completely led by AI as well. If I could just give a very quick example—it goes back a couple of years, but please bear with me. We had a mother come into one of our nurseries to collect her child, around 4 pm, and she was inebriated. She told the manager that that morning her partner of many, many years had walked out and told her that he had two children by another woman.

The manager recognised that this was not the normal practice of this particular woman. I took that scenario last night and I typed it into AI and said, “What should the educator do?” It came back with the most insensitive, inhumane process that individuals should follow, including involving the police and other agencies, and so on. What the manager did was she took the child, she took the mother, she went home with them in the evening and she sat there until 6 o’clock in the morning, until the mother came round basically, because she knew it was a one-off incident.

My concern is that if we follow AI and technology to the letter or if educators do, and they lose the human touch. Product development should also understand that element when it is being designed.

Q88 Peter Swallow: Thank you. Mr Waite, you mentioned that you have seen some really concerning uses of edtech and edtech tools and also some really exciting ones. What do you see as the greatest potential for these technologies to support positive development and improve outcomes?

Richard Waite: For early years, I think a bit like screen time, where there is a limit, I think we absolutely have to have a limit on it. To be fair, I do not particularly want to see AI generated toys in nursery settings, because the value of our educators and what they deliver to the children and that personal human contact is the most essential thing. I do not want children in nurseries sitting there talking to a cuddly teddy bear telling them that they are great.

I think the parents at home need a bit of support in terms of understanding if they are buying these things that they need to really check. Do not buy something from an online catalogue, basically, if you have not properly checked it out. There are some good resources out there that I have looked at, but few and far between, so I do not want that.

On technology, I support the forest school approach. I support all types of nurseries. I still believe in the three I’s—intent, implementation, and impact. What is the intent? If you are bringing a resource—and it could be sand, water or Play-Doh—why are you bringing that into a learning experience today? Educators need to think about that first. How are you going to deliver it? Twenty children around a Bee-Bot is not going to happen, is it? With a Bee-Bot only one person can use it at any one time. Therefore, how do you implement it?

Groups of children in an enabling environment—what is the environment to introduce these things? Then the impact is what is left—the observations, the assessment, and the next steps for the child. My view is do not bring edtech into—I do not even like edtech, sorry—but do not bring digital resources into nursery just for the sake of it, just because it looks good on your brochure or whatever. What are you doing with it?

When I deliver learning experiences, I use the STEAM approach: science, technology, engineering, arts, and maths. It makes the educator think about what the resource is. Think about the education first and then how the resource supports it. Technology is a tool, just like sand, water, and Play-Doh, as far as I am concerned. Once it is treated and respected in that way and not seen as something scary, it can have real benefits.

Q89 Peter Swallow: When members of this Committee visited Estonia, and early years settings in Estonia, we saw exactly that. We saw children being introduced to technology through hands-on play-based activities, often without screens, and with practitioners actively guiding the learning, including one-to-one interactions. That seems to me exactly what you are talking about, but it also seems to me that you are suggesting that in many settings in the UK we are very far from that at the moment. Is that fair?

Richard Waite: I think we are really far away from it. We are really far away, because we are still at a phase where people are scared of it. People are worried about bringing it in and about what the consequences might be. They are seeing things on the news. They are seeing things on social media.

The Government have brought out screen time guidance for parents. They have also brought out screen time guidance for settings. Yet last week BBC news was again going on about screens. Sometimes it is almost like parent bashing and when the parents are being bashed, they are coming in and bashing the educators, saying things like, “We don’t want this in here. We have just seen it on the news. It is really bad.” I think this is the issue. We have to change the narrative. The narrative needs to change, and I think we need help from the Government to change that narrative.

Q90 Peter Swallow: Mr Leitch, are your members looking at what is going on in Estonia and elsewhere and learning from how they are applying technology with a pedagogical underpinning in early year settings?

Neil Leitch: I would suggest that they are not. I would suggest, alongside Richard’s comments, that at the moment fear overtakes everything else. Obviously screen time is incredibly prominent, and so on, so they are avoiding learning elsewhere and even the use of technology beyond administration. As I have alluded to previously, it is used within administration but I think they are incredibly fearful about engagement with children.

One interesting thing came up in speaking to Laurence Taylor, who is the counter terrorism commissioner. We were talking about why it was that early years educators are reluctant. He said it is because there is a plethora of AI and technology that is absolutely awful. He quoted, for example that it is legal in this country for a child to play a game that simulates the shootings in Christchurch. We can do that. I think that speaks volumes about the protection that we have for children at this time. Therefore educators are nervous, nervous, nervous.

Q91 Peter Swallow: Professor Gibson, a final question from me. The reception baseline assessment can involve young children interacting with digital technology shortly after starting school, but, as we have heard, there is this very real fear, including, as Mr Waite alluded to, coming from parents who are concerned about the amount of edtech and technology in the classroom, sometimes seeming to counter all of the very real concerns that we have been talking about this morning. How should the Government balance the educational benefits of using technology in assessment with these concerns about limiting the use of screen time, limiting the exposure to harmful forms of technology?

Professor Gibson: A great question. I do not know of any evidence to support what that balance should be. I think you can think about general principles of what we want children to learn—those skills of critical appraisal, human connection and what has been called 21st century skills—working in teams, collaborating, and of course not being frightened of technology and being able to critically appraise it.

I do think it is important that children are exposed to technologies and they have those conversations about when, how, and how much and does it have a mind and what can it do and what can it not do. It has to be part of a broader approach to pedagogy that involves dialogue and helping children and also educators to build that critical skill.

In international examples, in Hong Kong they have an interesting implementation of AI literacy through games and engagement with these kinds of things associated with pedagogical goals around the technology itself. I think we absolutely do need that tech to be there, but as Richard said it needs to be used in that intentional way.

Peter Swallow: Thank you very much.

Q92 Chair: Can I ask a supplementary question, particularly on the reception baseline test?

I have heard examples in recent weeks of a parent saying to me, “I told my son to say, ‘I am not going to do it because we don’t do screens in our house’”. Another parent said, “My child just does not know how to do anything on a screen because I have kept them away from it.” Do you think there is a contradiction at the moment between the Government’s purposeful introduction of screen-based baseline testing in reception and the messages that parents and educators in the early years are receiving about children’s interaction with screens?

Professor Gibson: I do not think so. It is important that technology is a part of our processes. Maybe my colleagues might disagree with me on that, but I do not think it is unreasonable. Those data are really important for understanding educational added value and the trajectories of young children going through, and understanding educational achievement in this country. That is really important, so that is the value of that data.

We also have to think about the counterfactual in terms of getting children to perhaps sit pen and paper tests or activities and the feasibility of that, so I would be cautious. The message around screen time is very hard to nuance, because people tend to see it as an all‑or‑nothing issue, but when it’s interactive and used purposefully, certain technologies do have a place in the early years and can play a positive role.

Of course, if there are children who perhaps have not been exposed to technologies, that is a pedagogical question for teachers and local authorities administering those tests. We might want to think about how we support settings and support children to take it, but I certainly would not be throwing it out yet.

Neil Leitch: Despite the fact that I said that, particularly throughout the pandemic, when parents were trying to hold down jobs and also support their children and so on, it should be no surprise that they reached for technology, screens, and so on. What I think has started to happen is that the messaging—not just from the Government but from elsewhere—has started to make the parents feel like they are awful in many instances. They have almost gone from a binary position of, “Yes, you can access,” to, “No, you can’t,” and that is incredibly unhelpful. We should learn to understand the pressures.

I spoke earlier about the pressures on young children living in a different world. That is exactly the same for parents. I was asked by the chief executive of one of our largest supermarkets a little while ago—a few years ago, actually, to be fair—if I would talk to their annual conference on early years, and I said, “What about?” He said, “How we can get more parents, mums predominantly, back into the working environment?” I said that it was odd, but that I would speak to them. I spoke to 42 parents—all mums. Every single one of them said that they were rushed back to work. They felt under pressure. They talked about how they were the ones getting up at 5.30, 6 o’clock in the morning, making the packed lunches for the children or whatever it happened to be, doing a full day’s job, coming back in the evening and still preparing the meal. They said they would spend an hour, perhaps no more, with their child, predominantly in the bath, and they would buy them more sweets and more toys.

That is a long-winded way of saying that we really have to understand the pressures on parents. As well as supporting children within early years settings, it is probably more important that we support parents at home to understand the benefits and the risks, because it is here. But they are under pressure.

Chair: Thank you. In the interests of time, I am going to move us on to Mark now.

Q93 Mark Sewards: A couple of questions from me about inclusion and closing development gaps with AI and edtech. Understandably, you will know that we focused a lot in our previous inquiries on SEND and improving SEND provision. Obviously, it is a big focus for the DFE as well. There is very limited evidence out there that states that AI and edtech can be used to close these development gaps, but what written evidence we have been able to find is quite optimistic about it. How optimistic should we be that we can use AI and edtech to close development gaps, particularly for children with SEND or additional learning needs? What sort of opportunities should we be looking for to make sure we take advantage of this? I am happy to start with anyone, but I was drawn to Professor Gibson.

Professor Gibson: I think there is a lot of hype out there, to be honest, and I think the evidence is yet to catch up with it. At some point I started to get quite frustrated. I would go to conferences and they would say, “Oh, we are really worried about AI, except for autistic children,” based on nothing more than an intuition quite often that this might be helpful. Personally, I would want to see a lot more in terms of well-constructed trials of products that are devised in collaboration with children and families affected by SEND.

What I am optimistic about—if we fantasise a bit more—is profiling and developments if we are perhaps having conversations around the case that provision should be needs led and not diagnosis led. However, it is quite hard to get a nuanced perspective on individual needs, particularly with waiting times being what they are and the complexity of integrating information from different sources for children with additional needs.

I think that AI tools can help with profiling and understanding children’s needs, so I am quite optimistic from that point of view, but I would stress that collaborative bit with members of the community, particularly parents and children, being consulted and integrated into part of this development. Q94 Mark Sewards: Mr Leitch, is there any cause to be optimistic that we can improve inclusion in early years?

Neil Leitch: I need to be hanging on to hope. I feel we should because it is an unknown position at the moment that perhaps there is hope. I would say this. I do not think it is AI and edtech that is driving SEND inclusion. It is, frankly, policy; it is the Government’s prioritising of SEND that is driving it, as well as Ofsted looking at it from an inspection position. It is much more of a defensive position for why there is inclusion rather than edtech driving that.

I was in China with the Department for Business and Trade just a few weeks ago, and I saw some really good edtech products that, if used correctly, if monitored, would be supportive of children who have additional needs.

Q95 Mark Sewards: Any that come to mind right now? Any that we should be aware of?

Neil Leitch: They didn’t brand them. It was all very secretive to say the least, but they impressed me. Children with an educator would go through a routine and, effectively, it would identify, “You are pushing this child way beyond what they are capable of at this moment in time.” Or alternatively, “Why are you wasting this child’s time having to go through routines when actually they want to get on to the next bit? They are curious, they are adventurous,” and so on. I suppose that is where I would hold the hope.

Q96 Mark Sewards: Thank you very much. Mr Waite, is there anything in your working experience that would show some optimism?

Richard Waite: The term “assistive technology” has been around for a long time and there are companies and resources around. I am thinking about large push buttons for children who cannot use a keyboard, a special mouse, a trackball mouse on that side of helping certain children with special educational needs.

My experience is of a child in my nursery who had profound difficulties. When we spoke to the physios and all the people who supported him at a TAC—team around the child—meeting, it was said that he would get good support if he had a tablet in the nursery setting.
