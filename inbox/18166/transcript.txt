Q207 Chair: Welcome to this morning’s session of the Energy Security and Net Zero Committee. We are talking about energy resilience, a very topical discussion given today’s announcement. Welcome to Minister Michael Shanks and to Dan Micklethwaite from the Department.

Minister, you have a big announcement today. I know you have a statement to give in the House later, but we will undoubtedly be pursuing it here because of the topic being absolutely relevant to what you have announced. Can you tell us to start with how today’s announcement to approve Jackdaw will contribute to the UK’s energy resilience?

Michael Shanks: Good morning, Chair. It is good to be with you again on a really important topic. What we are announcing today is that the Energy Secretary has given her agreement and the NSTA has given its consent to the Jackdaw project to proceed.

It is a gas project in the North sea, which is an already licensed field, and Members will recall it was consented previously by the previous Government. That ended up in the courts, and we have been responding to that since and have come to the decision today. It is important for a number of reasons. First, it does strengthen our energy security. It is important for me to say we have a diverse range of supplies of gas at the moment from the continental shelf on our own side, from Norway and from other imports, but undoubtedly a project like this helps strengthen that energy security. Of course because the court allowed the project to continue its development while awaiting consent, it will be able to bring that gas on to the system in the UK this winter, which is important.

We estimate that it will make up 6% of our gas at its peak. So it is important from that point of view, but we have also—

Q208 Chair: Can I just interrupt you there? It will, at peak, be 6% of the entire gas demand for the UK. Have we got that right?

Michael Shanks: Yes, so 5% of domestic production, and around 6% at its peak over the lifespan of 11 years. That is what we estimate from the project.

Q209 Chair: Six per cent of production, not of demand?

Michael Shanks: It is expected to increase domestic gas production by around 6%, which is what I said in my initial answer, but you are right to clarify it. At its peak it will be 5% of domestic gas production. It is estimated to have a lifespan of around 11 years.

Q210 Chair: How much of our demand will that comprise?

Michael Shanks: Do you mean of this particular project?

Q211 Chair: How much of UK gas use will be produced—will be contributed to—by Jackdaw at peak?

Michael Shanks: I do not have the number that way round, but our domestic production at the moment is 42% of overall. Overall, 42% of our gas supply comes from UK continental shelves, so it will contribute to that. I don’t have the figure—the reverse—for Jackdaw.

Q212 Chair: So will the 6% figure increase that from 42%, or will it maintain it at around the 42% level?

Michael Shanks: Well, it will increase production.

Chair: It will increase that.

Michael Shanks: Yes.

Q213 Chair: So production will go from 42% to 48%—is that a reasonable assumption to make?

Michael Shanks: No, because clearly the fields are changing at all times, so we will see fields that will reduce. Of course, the exact numbers change all the time, so the live numbers change depending on what is extracted at any given moment.

Chair: Thank you for engaging with the figures.

Michael Shanks: We are doing the numbers early on.

Chair: Yes, you might find some of those questions reappear during the day, I dare say.

Can you tell us how the energy wealth fund will operate? In the press release, there is a reference to it contributing to the energy transition, for example.

Michael Shanks: Partly that is about recognising that we are really clear that the long-term future of this country does not lie in oil and gas. Although gas remains important in the short term, we have to start building much more quickly the infrastructure that gets us away from fossil fuels. That is absolutely central to our long-term resilience as a country.

What we have announced today is that we will set up an energy wealth fund, which is about funding the transition. It will be capitalised initially with £2 billion up front and will be added to with revenues from the Jackdaw field as those come in. However, it is about funding the infrastructure that we need to get us off fossil fuels.

What we have already been doing as a Government to date is bringing in private investment alongside public investment through the National Wealth Fund and Great British Energy. This is about increasing that and making sure that we are in a position where, although we need gas in the short term, we are building our way out of that need for gas in the long term.

Q214 Chair: One of the concerns raised with us, and I know with you, has been around the impact on communities and on workers—over a lot of years, in fact. Is part of the role of this fund to invest in those communities most affected? How will that operate?

Michael Shanks: We do two things on that. We have always said the transition has to bring the communities with us. The workers who are powering the country right now in the North sea, and who have done for 60 years, do an incredibly important job, but they are also the workers who will help us build the projects of the future. It is in our own interest to make sure the transition puts them right at the heart of it. We are doing that through the North sea future plan.

I think I have spoken to this Committee before about the North sea jobs service, which will be in place early next year to support workers into new careers, but this is also a really important part of that. The more we invest in the infrastructure here in Britain, the more we can keep the jobs and crucially the supply chain, which is where most of the jobs in oil and gas are. We can keep those in the UK, keep workers employed and keep investment in communities, but also build the infrastructure we need for the future.

Q215 Chair: Thank you. Before I move over to Melanie Onn, how will this announcement improve the national security elements of our energy supply and energy security?

Michael Shanks: I want to say first of all that clearly domestic production from the UK continental shelf is important in an uncertain world. If we can get it from the North sea, that does provide benefits to us. However, I also want to be really clear that in the long-term picture, it is relatively marginal overall. We will not stop being a net importer of fossil fuels because of this decision.

This is the argument I have made previously. Our long-term trend for more than two decades now has been to import more. That is not going to change. Our long-term answer to that security and resilience point is to break away from fossil fuels so that we have energy sovereignty here in the UK. That is going to come from renewables and from nuclear. It is not going to come from oil and gas.

Q216 Melanie Onn: Good morning, Minister. I was listening intently to the Secretary of State on the “Today” programme. The first thing that she said in response to Nick Robinson’s question was, “This will not bring down bills.” I wonder why, when the cost of living is such a critical issue for this Government, constituents up and down the country should support this decision if it doesn’t help them and it doesn’t help towards decarbonisation.

Michael Shanks: We have been very clear that new oil and gas does not take a penny off bills and any argument that suggests it does is simply wrong. We trade as an international commodity what comes off the North sea and we pay the price. We are always going to be a price taker, which is why we are saying that our long-term future does not lie in oil and gas.

But it does do other things. I think we need to be really clear that the vast majority of British homes are heated by gas, so while we have to electrify the economy and move away from gas for home heating, that is not going to happen overnight. Therefore, our supply does matter and if we can get it from the continental shelf that is important—that is the judgment the Energy Secretary has made on this particular project. It also is important for jobs and it is important as a source of revenue, which we are going to fund into the transition.

I am not going to cut across anything the Chancellor might say about wider support for consumers, but I would say that it is important that we invest now in building the things that get us off fossil fuels in the long term. People’s bills have gone up because of our exposure to fossil fuels. We have to do that piece that breaks us from that, but this is really important for our short-term energy security.

Q217 Chair: Thanks very much. Just a question or two on energy security. We have heard reports of recent attacks, or potential attacks, on the UK’s energy infrastructure. What steps have you been taking as a Department to increase awareness and resilience within the system following the reports of what we have seen from Russian submarines and, indeed, cyber threats as well?

Michael Shanks: The most important responsibility I have as Energy Minister is the resilience and security of the system. I will ask Dan to add to some of this.

The steps that we have been taking are, first, to continue to look at the Government’s risk assessment to make sure we have those risks fully up to date. There is a public version of that risk register. There is a version kept at a classified level to make sure we are really considering each of those risks in turn. We will be bringing forward an energy resilience strategy later this year, which picks up on many of the points that have been raised by this Committee and others to make sure that we continue to have as robust a system as possible.

There is legislation going through Parliament at the moment on cyber-security, which continues to be a really important risk that we are looking at. Across Government, in each Department that has a responsibility for security, there is a very active conversation about making sure we are as resilient as possible and we are also preparing the public so that they have resilience in this situation as well. Dan might want to add more.

Dan Micklethwaite: Yes, that is absolutely right. We do ongoing monitoring of risks as well as the structural, cyclical approach to that through the national security risk assessment. We do that on a tactical basis as well with our security partners and energy partners.

There is already a strong regulatory framework in place for cyber-resilience. That is going to be strengthened through the cyber-security and resilience Bill, and we have an energy cyber-security strategy about going further and faster on that as well. Again, we talk tactically to the industry about that on specific information as and when we have it.

We have quite strong resilience plans already for a wide range of disruption scenarios that are, if you like, cause-agnostic—we have those already. As the Minister said on the energy resilience strategy, we will take that further and think about how we can set clear energy resilience policy statements across a range of areas consistent with our cyber-security plans, our critical energy plans and our physical plans.

Chair: We look forward to reading the energy resilience strategy when it is published.

Q218 Torcuil Crichton: I know the session is mainly about energy resilience, but part of that, obviously, is today’s Jackdaw decision, which I welcome as a practical step in energy security, but also as a signal that we are in the North sea today, tomorrow and for many years to come as that base is wound down. I think, as the Chair said, the most important part of this announcement today is the £2 billion fund to help the transition from one industry to another.

Can you give us any more clarity on that and on how you will be engaging with the Scottish Government, for example? Will this fund be restricted to the north-east? We know that jobs and supply chains are not just in Aberdeen—they are all across the UK—so how will this £2 billion be felt in communities like mine and in others?

Michael Shanks: It is really important. The first thing I would say is that we shouldn’t think about this as just £2 billion for the transition. This is on top of what we have capitalised into Great British Energy, which is entirely about driving forward the transition but with the Government owning a stake in those projects, and the National Wealth Fund, which has clean energy as one of its driving missions. This is £2 billion on top of that existing investment and it is to make sure that we capitalise investment into clean energy projects that are creating jobs and actually retaining jobs and retaining the supply chain. The vast majority of the oil and gas jobs are in the supply chain. We need those jobs for the clean energy sectors we have to build.

Decisions about what that will be spent on will be made on the basis of individual projects, but it is fair to assume that given the number of projects that are already in Scotland and the potential for additional projects—the pipeline of offshore wind and other technologies is much greater in Scotland, and the supply chains are in Scotland—the vast majority of this will probably end up being spent in Scotland.

The benefits of this—I think you have made this point before—are in every single postcode across the country in supply chain jobs and in manufacturing. It ties into the wider Government strategy around re-industrialisation, not just investing in projects, but making sure those projects are built in Britain with the jobs that go with it.

Q219 Torcuil Crichton: I see the First Minister of Scotland, John Swinney, has welcomed this through gritted teeth, saying that it provides some certainty in the north-east. Have you had any chat with the Scottish Government or spoken to them about how this £2 billion might benefit Scotland?

Michael Shanks: I haven’t specifically yet, as we have only announced it today. Certainty is one thing I could not say about the SNP’s energy strategy. But we do need to move at pace on things like consenting, which is the responsibility of the Scottish Government, to make sure we are delivering the projects that the people of Scotland expect us to build together.

Q220 Torcuil Crichton: Let’s move on to the wider theme of this session, which is meant to be about resilience and how the energy infrastructure of the country is protected. Apart from what you have said to the Chair, what further steps do you need to take to future-proof the energy system from cyber-attacks and physical attacks?

Michael Shanks: There are a number of things. One of the things I did not say before that I think is important is I am now chairing a group of senior chief executives in industry because the Government, of course, operate none of this infrastructure directly. It is in the hands of individual organisations and we have to make sure that resilience, particularly cyber-resilience, is taken seriously in every single part of every single organisation.

One of the things we have been driving that forward on is making sure that we have the industry-led technical knowledge that is necessary, so that people have sufficient training at all levels of organisations. We are looking at whether we need to review legislation and whether we have the right powers to be able to do more in that space, and we are also looking at all sorts of national emergency planning and cyber-resilience planning. All of these things are live processes we are working through.

Building resilience at every single level of the energy system is what we see as absolutely crucial. It is one thing for company boards to say it is important; it is another thing for the person operating the terminal to see it as their absolute priority. We are driving that forward at the moment as well.

Dan Micklethwaite: To add to that, the industry and the Department work really closely with the National Protective Security Authority and the National Cyber Security Centre as well, which regularly brief and work quite closely with the industry on the steps they need to take.

Bringing it back to something the Minister said before about the energy transition, that of course is a once-in-a-generation opportunity to ensure that what we are building is resilient by design, which is far more efficient than retrofitting or trying to patch up the system that may be in place right now. There is just a big opportunity with the transition to strengthen things as we build them.

Q221 Torcuil Crichton: You anticipated my next question, Dan, which you can answer possibly. How well is this understood across Government and other parts of Government?

Dan Micklethwaite: I would say very well. It is something that the Government take incredibly seriously, not just because of the wider external environment but because of how important the energy system is to households and to the rest of the country’s critical national infrastructure as well. It is understood very well and we work quite closely with the rest of the Department to make sure, as they are developing their policies, that they take security and resilience as a fundamental requirement of that.

Q222 Torcuil Crichton: Just one last question in passing. There is a concern about cyber-security but also physical security. We have heard reports of unidentified lights, possibly drones, around North Sea structures and infrastructure. Without giving something away, what practical steps are you taking to make sure the physical infrastructure is protected?

Michael Shanks: Again, physical resilience and security are hugely important. I would say that although we take these threats very seriously and they are developing at all times, we have a very good success rate in protecting that infrastructure. But clearly the threat landscape is always changing so we have to stay on top of that.

On drones in particular, we have been working with industry to make sure they have appropriate guidance on what steps they can take. Drones are obviously becoming much more prevalent than ever before and people can get their hands on drones in a way that they couldn’t before. We are exploring whether we do need to go further in that area, looking at the National Security Act to take any additional powers through that, including looking at whether we need more restrictions on flying and other aspects. We keep all of this under review.

Again it goes back to the point around bringing industry with us on this. Ofgem has taken security and resilience very seriously to make sure that the companies it regulates are investing in their physical and cyber-resilience to make sure it is as robust as it needs to be.

On the previous point, I think this is something that is taken seriously right across Government. Dan Jarvis is the Security Minister who is chairing a number of ministerial groups to make sure every single bit of Government is focused on critical national infrastructure but also, as I said before, on domestic resilience, to make sure we as a country are prepared. We are a bit of an outlier as a country in how much we bring people with us on their own personal resilience. That is going to be important, not just for security but for responding to storms and other events, so that people have a bit of personal resilience as well.

Q223 Wera Hobhouse: First, when can we expect the energy resilience strategy?

Michael Shanks: We have said later this year.

Q224 Wera Hobhouse: Okay, later this year—as late as 31 December 2026?

Michael Shanks: That is as late as we could go in the year, so hopefully by then, yes.

Q225 Wera Hobhouse: Thank you. On physical attacks, I was at a breakfast meeting bringing together the industry on flexibility. The argument is very clear: it is much easier to attack one big central power station, for example, or an installation, than hundreds and thousands of little installations. In the forthcoming energy independence Bill, will there be a reflection on how we should increase the flexibility markets, recognising that lots of energy is now going to be created in a decentralised way and that, in many ways, that is the best way of physically protecting it? How are the Government thinking about this?

Michael Shanks: I completely agree with you. You tempt me to reveal what is in the energy independence Bill, which I will not do at this stage, but we will see it soon. You are right about both decentralisation of energy infrastructure and flexibility, and we see that as critical. That is partly why we are investing in the first long duration energy storage in 40 years. It is why we are seeing more battery and shorter duration storage deployment, so that we have that flexibility in the system. We also see much more deployment of the ability of consumers to make use of flexibility in terms of time-of-use tariffs that benefit the system as well as consumers.

On the broader point, from a resilience point of view, I think is absolutely clear, as Dan said earlier, that we have a once-in-a-generation chance to build a decentralised system that reduces the risk to individual parts of the system, because it is decentralised right across the country in tens of thousands of pieces of infrastructure. There are complexities in doing that but I would look to Ukraine as a really good example. Ukraine is building back from attacks on energy infrastructure by building the exact decentralised energy system that we are building, partly to decarbonise but partly because it is much more difficult to attack the individual parts and have a wider impact on the system.

It is about strength by design. That is another reason why we see it as a hugely important aspect of what the energy system looks like in future.

Q226 Wera Hobhouse: In terms of oversight, should the strategy seek to shift the burden of resilience from central Government to local government?

Michael Shanks: We want it to be a partnership, and it is at the moment. Resilience absolutely has to sit with central Government and local government in partnership, because the levers that we both have—the local knowledge and the local levels of trust in responding to incidents—have to sit with both. It is one of the things the Prime Minister has been very clear on: strengthening the devolution agenda and that partnership with local government at all levels.

It is also some of the work we have been doing on Storm Arwen. The report that came after that was partly around how the Government respond to major incidents, a storm in that case, but it would apply to other things. Rehearsing some of this, and building the training and the support that local resilience forums have, are really important parts of this as well, but it has to be about both.

Q227 Wera Hobhouse: Shifting to extreme weather events, then, to what extent will the energy resilience strategy actually talk about extreme weather events and resilience? Can you go into a bit more detail?

Michael Shanks: I can’t go into the detail on something we have not published yet, but it is about our general sense of resilience in the energy system, which is responding to a whole series of things. Storms and extreme weather events are going to become much more frequent. We saw the summer heatwaves having a real impact on gas generators being able to generate, for example. There are widespread impacts that we have to take into consideration. That will all be part of it. However, our response, and I suppose what the strategy is about, is focusing on the overall resilience of the system and the further steps we have to take, whether we are responding to storms or threat actors.

Q228 Wera Hobhouse: Okay; I will ask the next question about the strategy. You might say that you can’t tell me because the strategy is not published, but I will try anyway. Should the UK be prioritising the domestic manufacturing of components, with the subsequently increased cost, to reduce supply chain dependencies?

Michael Shanks: I think this is really important. I can say what the Government’s position on this is because we have been clear on it for two and a half years. We have to build supply chains much more in the UK. That is a security and an economic imperative, and it is a view shared across Europe as well about domestic supply chains in Europe.

It is really important because in an uncertain world, relying on supply chains from other parts of the world makes it more difficult for us to maintain our resilience. Obviously the more we manufacture in the UK, the more economic benefit we are getting from that. But also the transition to clean power is not just happening in Britain; it is happening right across the globe and there is competition for supply chains. If we can build and manufacture more of this in Britain, we have more of a chance of getting it quickly on to the system to help us deliver the outcomes we want to see. From a security point of view, it is important but actually it is about the economic opportunity of the clean power transition.

Dan Micklethwaite: Just to build on that, if I may, we have very good information on the supply chain and critical inputs to critical national infrastructure, working with the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade. As the Minister said, having clear policies in place to boost domestic supply chains is really important. I draw attention to the clean energy supply chain fund, which was £1 billion to support companies with high growth potential to develop those domestic supply chains, and the clean industry bonus, which was also to reward the supply chain development of investment projects in the UK.

Clearly, there is only so far you can go, and we work very closely with industry on what stockpiles and reserves they have of critical components. We are talking to them about standardisation of components as well to make it easier to move components around should we need to do that.

Q229 Wera Hobhouse: Slightly moving away from that, industry always tell us that the problem is that energy is too expensive. Michael Shanks: You are not going to get any argument from me or anyone else in Government when you say that energy prices are too high. We have been very clear on that—they are far too high. The Prime Minister has set out the ambition that we will equalise with the European average on energy prices to deal with that competitive issue that you raise, but it is not going to happen overnight.

The challenge is that the Government is investing significant amounts of money to bring down the costs of crucial industries, but we have to fundamentally break the link with gas if we are going bring down prices for everyone and in the long term. That, in the long run, is the thing that will help deliver domestic manufacturing.

I would just say, though, on the cost point, there is always a trade-off here, and we are really clear about this. We want to see domestic supply chains wherever possible, and we are doing everything we can to support those domestic supply chains, but we also have to tackle affordability. That is a tension, clearly, because it is more expensive to build things here than elsewhere. However, there is a social benefit that Governments for far too long have not calculated about domestic procurement that we have to shift. If we manufacture things in Britain, we have a more resilient supply chain, first of all, but we also create jobs, which creates additional tax receipts, and we deal with some of the fundamental issues in this country about us just being agnostic about industrial policy for far too long.

There are social benefits of saying we want to build things here in Britain, and there are energy security arguments for why those supply chains being here leads to a more resilient system.

Michael Shanks: I can’t say no.

Q230 Sir Christopher Chope: Minister, you have said on several occasions that gas will remain part of the energy mix up to and beyond 2050, so can I ask a few questions about gas security? The National Energy System Operator—NESO—has said that it has identified an emerging risk to gas supply security in the unlikely event of the loss of the single largest piece of gas infrastructure in the system. What is the single largest piece of gas infrastructure in the system, and what is the threat to it?

Michael Shanks: Each year we do an assessment, something called N1, which is essentially an assessment of whether the gas system can cope if the biggest single piece of infrastructure was—in an extremely unlikely scenario I should say—no longer available. For example, that could be an LNG terminal—Milford Haven would be an example of an LNG terminal.

The second part of the test is it has to be a one-in-20 demand day—the coldest winter day that the UK could expect in 20 years. A combination of things has to occur at the same time—that is the resilience test we have. In all likelihood, the system never has to reach that point, but the whole point is that it should be resilient, so we should be able to. We should have sufficient redundancy in the system to meet that test every single year. The Secretary of State has to be able to be assured that that is the case every year.

Q231 Sir Christopher Chope: That is a given then, that we are secure in that respect—guaranteed?

Michael Shanks: Every year we have to assess all the available evidence for the year ahead and make that assessment. We have to be assured that is the case, or take actions to make sure we can meet that.

Q232 Sir Christopher Chope: Where does the gas grid fit into this, because 23 million homes are dependant on the gas grid. Can you confirm the gas grid will remain part of our critical national infrastructure well beyond 2050?

Michael Shanks: It will remain important until the last customer has been disconnected. We are working—on a domestic and industrial level—to move customers away from gas, but that will not happen overnight. The transmission network and the distribution network that go into streets and homes will remain important.

We do have a challenge that we are working through at the moment that we need to work with industry on around how we continue to invest in that gas grid while we will see customers disconnecting. That will be quite piecemeal; customers will disconnect as they choose to take on other technologies. That is a challenge for us as a country, but the gas network is hugely resilient. It has been invested in and upgraded much more recently than the electricity network, and we need to maintain that while moving people off it as quickly as we can.

I do not have the exact number in front of me but we can share it with the Committee. Dan Micklethwaite: If we start with the cyber assessment framework under the Network and Information Systems Regulations, that is basically the regulatory basis for cyber-resilience in the UK. There is then also a timetable for them to get to the enhanced standard, which further strengthens them to more sophisticated levels of attack.
