Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Dame Nicole Jacobs and Eleanor Lyons.

Q1 Chair: I welcome our witnesses to this one-off session that the Home Affairs Committee is holding on the progress being made on the new violence against women and girls strategy, which was released in December last year.

We are delighted to be joined by guests including the new Chair of the Justice Committee, Catherine Atkinson; Anna Dixon from the Public Accounts Committee; Alex Brewer from the Women and Equalities Committee; and Peter Swallow from the Joint Committee on Human Rights, who will join us later. That demonstrates that this is a very cross-cutting, cross-Government piece of work, and that while it may be led from the Home Office, it is very much not being delivered entirely by it. Can I ask our witnesses to introduce themselves and then we will get into questions?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: Hi, I am Nicole Jacobs, the Domestic Abuse Commissioner for England and Wales.

Eleanor Lyons: It is lovely to be here. I am Eleanor Lyons, and I am the independent anti-slavery commissioner for the UK.

Q2 Dr Prinsley: Good afternoon. I am Peter Prinsley, MP for Bury St Edmunds and Stowmarket. You look very far away—we normally have a slightly smaller room. I hope you can hear us.

It says here that one woman is murdered every three days in this country, there are more than 100,000 rapes a year and about one in 20 of the population are said to be perpetrators of violence against women and girls. What impact has “Freedom from Violence and Abuse”—the VAWG strategy—had so far, and how does that compare with the impact of previous strategies?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: That question is really timely because it was just last week that our National Centre for VAWG and Public Protection set out the strategic threat assessment for violence against women and girls, and it was staggering. We also had some of our crime survey for England and Wales statistics last week showing an increase in domestic abuse over the last year. It is true that, in general, Government and society are starting to understand both the prevalence and harm of domestic abuse, and more widely, violence against women and girls. The Domestic Abuse Act has really helped to elevate things.

We are coming up to a year since the strategy you asked about was published. It was long awaited; we waited a year for it, with the new Government. One thing that was really positive was that it set quite a good context for the drivers of violence against women and girls. It certainly did not shy away from the idea that there are male victims, but it definitely took a gendered nature and understood and explained the gendered nature of these crimes, while, at the same time, not undermining boys and men who are victims. I thought that was very positive. It had a lot of language in it about misogyny, for example.

It took a cross-Government approach. Very often in years past, we have criticised strategies like these for being too criminal justice-focused. This was very much not only criminal justice-focused; you could see a real concerted effort across all Government Departments. Ellie and I are both hosted at the Home Office, but certainly in my role in domestic abuse I meet across all Departments—on housing, health and with the MOJ—and I can see how the strategy has influenced each and every Department to put their best foot forward. In particular, health is a very good example—probably the strongest example from my perspective in the whole of the strategy.

I have been in this role for seven years, so I have seen many variations of leaders and Ministers over the years. It was difficult for me to get a foot into the Department of Health and Social Care in the past, or what I would call a meaningful one. This strategy did create a commitment from that Department that is fairly substantial and I can also say is being implemented—creating a GP pathway called “steps to safety”.

The first 12 integrated care boards have just been commissioned. Fairly substantial funding has been put alongside that £34 million, which is actually not cited as a figure in the strategy that has subsequently transpired. Jess Asato MP has been given a role in the DHSC. I am sure we will go back to some of the positives. It is also positive in that it creates a view of a public health approach to these things, weighing much more towards prevention—early intervention not so much. We have often lurched in the past to crisis.

Frankly, my overarching worry about the strategy is that we do not have a grip on it, on some of the best parts of it. We are not transparent enough about where we are in terms of the actions. There is an entire document that lists all the actions in the strategy, and there is not enough understanding of the governance and accountability of the strategy in general. It has a lot of good ideas in it, but there are many, many actions where I cannot actually explain to you where we are right now, who is taking responsibility in Government for those actions, and how much funding and resource planning is attached to those actions. That is my overarching concern.

I highly question whether such a cross-cutting strategy should be led at the Home Office. I think we have to consider quite strongly our Prime Minister driving from the centre all sorts of things like rough sleeping—I was at the rough sleeping summit yesterday—and whether this strategy would be better governed from the Cabinet Office or No. 10. My last word on it is that we do not really have time to waste. This is a good strategy, but it has not got a grip and accountability on it. We are losing a lot of ground that we could be gaining if we were implementing the actions in it.

Q3 Dr Prinsley: Eleanor, would you like to say something about that?

Eleanor Lyons: Just to add to what Nicole said, I welcome that the strategy is cross-Government. I think it is ambitious. It is right that it is tackling the underlying issues that are driving the increase in violence against women and girls.

But from my position, I see the strategy as a huge missed opportunity to protect the women and girls who come and tell me about the harms they have suffered. Modern slavery is not mentioned in this strategy, and it was in previous VAWG strategies that we saw. In terms of the underlying metrics, we have that the Government are trying to halve domestic abuse, sexual violence and stalking—they are all very important things that we need to address—but we are not including in those metrics any national referral mechanism statistics or anything that outlines some of the harms that are happening when it comes to sexual exploitation and trafficking.

The consequence of that a year in is becoming really clear. The survivors I speak to do not feel that they have heard about the strategy or that it impacts them, and I think that is how we should judge success. They do not feel like they are being listened to in the delivery of the strategy, and the political will, attention and focus are going on areas that are explicitly outlined. So while I welcome the strategy—I think it is doing an awful lot of good beyond just transparency, accountability and ownership—I do not think it is truly recognising all the forms of VAWG. I would like to see sexual exploitation and modern slavery more clearly referenced in the strategy moving forward, and in the accompanying actions underneath it.

Q4 Dr Prinsley: Thank you for that. That was some stuff about the additional things you would like to see, but there is the question about the impact of what we have done so far. Can that perhaps be measured? There is a metric to halve violence against women and girls, and we have been at it for a little while now. Do you have any sense of whether we are going backwards or forwards at the moment?

Eleanor Lyons: When it comes to sexual exploitation, if we look just over the last year, for British women and girls, that has risen by 23%. That is not a metric that is underlying the strategy and what the Government are trying to achieve, but I still think it is meaningful. Sadly, it actually shows that, for some of the most vulnerable women, who often have overlapping needs—they may have been victims of domestic abuse or child abuse and are now victims of sexual exploitation—we are not managing to decrease that number. This is a moment, a year in, when we need to ask ourselves the question: what needs to change to be able to help that group?

Dr Prinsley: I agree; thank you for that answer.

Dame Nicole Jacobs: The strategy was published with metrics that were quite ambitious. When I first saw the metrics, I thought that it was a bold approach. The headline metric is quite pure; it is about the prevalence of women and girls among people aged 16 and over who are victims of domestic abuse, sexual assault and stalking. I think that plays straight into what Eleanor just pointed out, in that those are just three of the strands but not all of them. Some of the supporting metrics include female homicide, repeat domestic abuse and prevalence of sexual harassment, and there a number of sub-metrics as well.

My point is that, in general, we are almost a year in, and I do not know how these metrics are going to be measured—other than the headline metrics, which I believe is the crime survey for England and Wales. I have never seen or heard of a document—maybe there is one behind the scenes that I am not aware of—that shows me exactly how these metrics are going to be measured, and where we are right now. That is what I mean by the lack of grip and lack of transparency.

Q5 Dr Prinsley: What we are seeing is a fall in crime nationally, and all sorts of crimes are less, including burglary, assault in the street, car theft or radios being nicked, but what I do not think we are seeing—at least I have not seen any evidence—is a fall in violence against women girls or domestic violence. If anything, what you describe is an increase, which is very worrying.

Dame Nicole Jacobs: It is worrying. You would expect that the more you do, the more you would naturally see increases, because you may be helping people earlier, and people will be coming forward feeling more confident in systems. However, what lies behind the metrics? How are things being measured? What do we make of that analysis? A lot of that is not something that is being discussed openly and transparently.

I think we could accept some increases. For example, when you open up a GP pathway, you will absolutely see people coming forward to health early, as it is a trusted environment. If anything, you would naturally expect GPs to show more, and that would not be an increase, but it would appear as one. There is something about making sense of what the metrics are, but we can do that only if we are having a transparent conversation, and we know exactly how things are being measured, but that is not happening. It may be happening behind closed doors with Ministers—I can accept that that might be entirely true, but I am not aware of that.

Chair: It is very interesting. One of the points we made when were first talking about these metrics was that, if the focus is put on these things, you can have more people coming forward, and you will therefore have an increase in numbers. That is not actually an increase in prevalence, but it is an increase in reporting, and that is something to be careful of.

Q6 Mr Kohler: Thank you for joining us. The previous VAWG strategy focused a lot on pursuing perpetrators through the criminal justice system; that clearly has a huge a role to play, but it is not enough on its own. Dame Nicole, you mentioned the strategic threat risk assessment from the national centre published last week, which identified repeat perpetrators as a critical issue. Do you think enough is being done to pay attention to that to actually break the cycle of abuse?

That was my general question, and I also have a specific question. I am aware of the Drive Project at the Home Office, which I understand Cambridgeshire police are implementing. What is your view on that, and is there uptake from other police forces?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: There is still a lot in the strategy that has to do with criminal justice, and that is right. I know from victims and survivors of domestic abuse that the police will be one of the first ports of call. It is not a criticism to say that in the past we have focused on criminal justice; it was a natural place, but it is not the only place, and I think we have moved on from that. As you say, there are plenty of actions that the Home Office should be and is driving that are incredibly important, but if we look at what is in the strategy, in terms of early intervention and prevention, it is there, but it could be so much more robust.

It is also fairly underdeveloped, a year in. For example, there are commitments in the strategy that relate to schools. One of the things shown by the gathering of evidence prior to the strategy, which Government analysts conducted, was that schools-based work is well evidenced as effective, yet for the scale of the problem, we still have modest commitments from the Department of Education. At least they are there—that is better than before—but as an example, there is a fairly modest commitment on bringing together the right tools and information for teacher training or schools training. Again, I do not know fully how well under way that is, because we do not have a structure and a transparent way of knowing how and when the actions are taking effect.

There are some commitments about building the evidence base through the Youth Endowment Fund; that is definitely up and running. This is devolved to Wales, but from my travels in England particularly, there is a lot of activity in schools. There are lots of individual charities and initiatives, and some of them are very good, but we do not have enough of a community of practice or a coherent understanding of what good absolutely looks like and must be in each and every school. What is the whole-school approach?

So there is a lot missing and there is a lot half-implemented in that prevention strand, and I would say the same for the early intervention. It is a real mixed picture, and this is why I feel this sense of urgency: we do not have a lot of time, so let’s get on with seeing what those commitments are fully. When this strategy was published, we were told that it was the first version, that there would be other iterations and that it would build over the years. That is one area that needs quite a lot of building and a lot of emphasis that we do not have.

There is quite a bit on the online space, which I think we will come to. Drive is commissioned by the Home Office for a roll-out across the whole of England and Wales. It is one of many things that helps centre more focus on the perpetrator. Having a way of working for the police and partners that actually allocates a focus on the offender is really welcome. Some areas have taken to that better than others.

You will think that I am very repetitive, but again, if you said to me, “Domestic Abuse Commissioner, how many areas have rolled out Drive?”, I would say, “I don’t know, because I don’t have anything that is reported on in front of me.” I know anecdotally the places that I have talked to that are implementing Drive and how it is going. However, I do not know where we are in that roll-out. That is where we need much more grip and transparency so we can all get behind it.

There are lots of areas that would welcome getting behind some of this activity, but they have to know when they can depend on it coming, who is planning it and where they are looking to. I was in Wiltshire on Friday at an amazing event. People there needed to know from this strategy what is coming down the line that will help them get done what they would like to get done. We are all being held back by not having enough transparency here.

Q7 Mr Kohler: Repeat offenders are such a huge issue: a quarter of all suspects have committed three or more VAWG offences. Is Drive working or is it too early to tell? Is enough resource being put into dealing with this chronic issue of persistent repeat offenders?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: I do not think that Drive would answer all those questions. Drive is a good structure, and it helps create a positive structure of working, but it is not the only way to achieve this. My own strategy, separate from the VAWG strategy, very much focuses on the point of first contact with the police.

I believe that victims and survivors need and deserve an excellent first response so that we do not have a continual situation where there is no further action, where we are asking how many times that needs to happen, and where maybe the algorithms and some of the tactical policing methods are starting to kick in.

We need a much better quality first response from the police. That can, and should, be planned into police reform. We have that great opportunity in front of us. I hope that will happen, but again, that kind of thing is not necessarily written into the strategy. I felt that was an omission.

Q8 Mr Kohler: Eleanor, do you have any thoughts on that?

Eleanor Lyons: When it comes to modern slavery and human trafficking, the police have two roles. One is to help us identify potential victims and refer them into the national referral mechanism, and the other is to hold perpetrators to account when it gets to that stage. Looking across the numbers, they speak for themselves. There have been only 70 people prosecuted in England and Wales for modern slavery offences in the last year, against a backdrop of over 23,000 victims. There is clearly a big disparity there.

We know what works in terms of supporting victims through the criminal justice system with things such as victim navigators. However, taking a step back from the VAWG strategy, the Government have cut funding for dedicated modern slavery and human trafficking teams in police forces. In order for us to say that this should not be a high-reward, low-risk crime, we need that to be matched by Government funding, messaging and rhetoric beyond just the strategy. At the moment that is missing.

Sadly, when I speak to frontline services and survivors of sexual exploitation, they do not feel like their perpetrators are being held to account. That is compounded by them having to navigate a system that lasts for years. They are having to hold that trauma and live with that uncertainty. I know that we will come on to talk about the early release scheme, but they are also beginning to question whether it is really worth them navigating that long system to maybe not even end up with offences in place at the end or for their perpetrator to be released quite quickly.

When talking about the cross-Government nature of the strategy, we need to look at Government as a whole and the entire message that they are giving to female survivors. I do not feel like we are doing enough to prevent that harm when it comes to modern slavery.

Q9 Chair: I want to ask about data. It has always been the case that it is nigh on impossible to get data. Police forces record things differently, and things are also recorded differently in the criminal justice system. It is very difficult to get that information. We met a group of Romanian MPs last week and they have just passed a femicide law. It does not actually change the offences, but it makes femicide something that has to be recorded and properly identified. Do you have any thoughts on that?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: That sounds really positive. One of the things that has happened—I think it was a positive action in the strategy—is the notion of the DA-flagged offence. That sounds very simple but it creates a judicial finding so that, at the time of sentencing, it is very clear what is a domestic abuse-related offence. That then helps when we need to understand the data later, and very practically from the standpoint of probation—overseeing offenders upon release, and that kind of thing—it means that they actually know what the domestic abuse-related offences are.

We have not known that, believe it or not—I think that the Committee know that, but most people would not appreciate that that information is hard to find. We have to do much more to make changes such as what you said to make sure that the data is really clear. On domestic abuse homicide and suicide, we have made some progress in coming to terms with those tragedies and trying to learn from them, but there is a whole range of other femicide-related crimes for which we need to do the same. I think you are right, Chair.

Chair: We will move on to Government leadership of the strategy.

Q10 Alex Brewer: I want to dig into some of the things you said already without necessarily going over the same ground. The Government had a well-publicised and ambitious aim to halve violence against women and girls in a decade. Is it clear to either of you when that decade started?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: I consider that it started from the point of the strategy last year. A very important milestone is coming in December, when we will be a year into that strategy.

Eleanor Lyons: I interpreted it in the same way.

Alex Brewer: Okay. Thank you. It was not very clear to me whether it was immediately after the election or when the strategy was launched, or whether it had not even started yet.

Dame Nicole Jacobs: I mean, we could be wrong. It is possible. When did the metric start? I think you are making your point there.

Q11 Alex Brewer: Dame Nicole, ministerial responsibility for VAWG has changed recently. Has that affected the engagement with the Government that you talked about?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: Yes, it always does. As you will all know, any time there is a shuffling of key ministerial posts that creates disruption. For this strategy it feels very much that the day-to-day responsibility sits between the Home Office and the MOJ, with the Safeguarding Minister and the Minister for Victims. We are on our third Home Office Safeguarding Minister and our second holder of the MOJ post; some of the same people have also come back.

The larger point is that although those exceptional leaders help to drive the work day to day, the issue needs much more serious elevation to and attention from our Secretaries of State. I have seen the difference over time when that happens, and what happens as a result. It may be that Secretaries of State attend interministerial groups on the strategy; they may include the strategy in some of their discussions. I am not fully aware of those, if they are happening, but we need to see much more of the heft of Government.

I was delighted when I saw our Secretary of State for Health and Social Care speak in her conference speech about a public health approach to domestic abuse. That is what we need. That really helps people like Ellie and me, when we are interacting within Government, to push things ahead and drive accountability. There needs to be some kind of elevation.

Having yesterday seen the Prime Minister talk so clearly about rough sleeping, I want to see some equivalent leadership on this strategy. There are obvious ties to homelessness and between this strategy and other strategies in Government. I understand that in the new year the Prime Minister will be publishing and sharing with us a 10-year plan for Britain. Violence against women and girls absolutely must—not should, but really must—be in it. We need elevation.

Q12 Alex Brewer: Are you both confident that this is still a Government priority and is being taken seriously across the different Departments by Ministers and officials?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: I do not believe that, to date, this strategy has been implemented in a way that shows that it is at the top of the priority list, in terms of resource and all those things. I really feel that we can do much, much better to realise the ambition of the strategy.

Eleanor Lyons: I would broadly echo what Nicole has said. With the previous Prime Minister, when the strategy was launched, it was very clearly part of the Government’s mission. I think there was a lot of attention, excitement and hope for what the strategy would achieve.

It is hard to see a similar level reflected at the moment, but I think there is a new opportunity with the new Prime Minister to outline that we can and should be doing this and to start looking more broadly at how we measure it, who is accountable and where it should be owned—and make sure that it is reflecting all the vulnerable groups of women as well.

I have had positive engagement with the new Ministers, but I think that Nicole is right to say that when we get new Ministers, they understandably have a whole new brief and area to commit to. There is a chance or moment where we can hopefully see what that will look like in the new Administration.

Q13 Alex Brewer: Is the VAWG strategy advisory board a useful forum for influencing Government activity and ensuring that it is informed by lived experience?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: I stood on that advisory board, and it is useful in that many of the right people are sitting around the table. But there are limitations in that, with such a broad strategy, how could you imagine having over 200 actions without having more metrics and transparency in front of you?

I appreciate that it is not a governing board; it is very much set up with the terms of reference to advise and have some element of accountability because of the expertise in the room. But it is very hard to do that if you do not have information. For example, the papers are sent out a week in advance. A lot of the broad discussions can be about one subject at a time, but you are only meeting once a quarter, so you are not covering them all.

This is such an ambitious strategy that it requires, I would say, more sub-groups and clarity for each Government Department that is leading on particular actions with their own way of governing their actions. You can have an amazing, expert domestic abuse service, for example, and it may not be expert in all areas but it could be very useful in some areas of the strategy. There needs to be a broadening of what that good governance looks like, and I would say that that can be improved.

Again, one of my frustrations has been feeling that we could be going so much further, faster if we had some governance that we could lean on. Then I could understand who is leading, where the activity is and who I am getting in touch with in each and every Department. Helping to signpost would very importantly get survivor voice into the planning of those actions. But that is just not what I see.

Q14 Alex Brewer: Are you saying that that survivor voice is not being heard and fed through enough at the moment?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: In some cases, yes. In some of the things in the strategy, which will ultimately result in frontline service delivery by charities, they will likely have quite a lot of survivor input and may be led or run by survivors. We have something called “Our voices matter” at the Domestic Abuse Commissioner’s office, where we have over 1,000 people who we communicate with and engage with. We have brought some of those voices into Government as much as we can, but we have very limited resource for that—we have one full-time equivalent worker who is able to help us with that, primarily. Again, further investment in that is needed.

Eleanor Lyons: When it comes to the advisory board, I think it is a really good idea that they should bring together some kind of accountability mechanism for the strategy. But when it comes to the modern slavery sector being represented on that board and my office being represented on that board, so far we have not been. The consequence of that is twofold. There is a whole voiceless section of women who are affected by sexual exploitation that is not being heard at that advisory board. It also means that when policy could be co-created, collaborated on and better as a result of that, we are missing those opportunities.

I have pushed the Home Office a lot on this point. They assure me that the advisory board will have a rotating membership, and everyone will be heard on it eventually, but I am really struggling with the transparency around it, how that will happen and in what order they are going to discuss topics that are really important. I see that as quite a basic ask and a fundamental thing that we need to understand—who is being heard in this VAWG strategy, and what are we achieving against the things that are laid out in it? The advisory board will hopefully be expanded, but at the moment it is not working in a way that really represents the survivors of modern slavery.

Q15 Alex Brewer: It seems to me from what you are saying that neither the big picture horizon-scanning piece nor the detailed work of objective setting and meeting those objectives is quite hitting the spot. Have I got that right?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: Yes. Looking at the diversity and range of work in this strategy as it is laid out, if you want Government and civil servants to be getting the best advice and information, you would immediately recognise that you could never achieve that in one quarterly meeting.

You would have to have some kind of structure across Departments where you could really understand where you are gaining that expertise. You mentioned Drive and DAPOs. There are working groups—I am not saying that there are not—but they are fairly disparate, and I am not sure they are feeding into decision makers how well we are achieving these parts of the strategy. There is room for improvement there.

Q16 Alex Brewer: I have one final question. This is obviously a huge cross-departmental thing, as indicated by how many people we have here. Which Departments are really prioritising the actions needed for this Government’s ambition, and are there any Departments that could be doing more?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: I have already given a very clear indication that I have been very pleased in terms of understanding the Department of Health and Social Care’s commitments, where they are on them, how much money they are investing, and the kinds of things they are doing above and beyond what is in the strategy already. That is a really good example.

In terms of the Department for Education, if you put aside the schools work we have already talked about, their answer in the strategy to the kinds of services that children who are victims of domestic abuse—who we have defined as victims in their own right—will get is, “Look at Families First, broadly. Look at the £1 billion-plus of funding that we are putting in, which is essentially how we will redesign and implement children’s social care.” That is not answering the question very specifically. To me, there is much more that the Department for Education could be doing to step up and be clear about what that is achieving. That is a huge priority for children who are victims of domestic abuse, given the prevalence.

I would put the Home Office in the category of “well under way” in terms of, broadly, some of their commitments. But, again, there are some real hiccups along the way with regard to the protection order roll-out and some of the key strands there.

I am sure we will come to MOJ, but I feel that with early release and some of the pressures on our system, some of their commitments are getting overshadowed and not being implemented. It is the same with DWP. That is what I mean about looking at something whereby we could say, “What’s the accountability on these actions? Who’s making sure that they are getting done?” That is the sense of urgency that I feel. It is a question of having to face up to that.

Another huge one that I am sure we will come to is DCMS and all of the online space and harms. There are many question marks and underdeveloped areas, but there is some progress. It is a mixed picture.

Eleanor Lyons: I would echo that. Nicole has outlined some of the Departments that we have had very good engagement with. Jess Asato, leading the VAWG health work, has been hugely helpful. She is very inclusive in terms of understanding all the forms of violence against women and girls and implementing change. In the online harm space, we have had good engagement with DSIT and with MOJ. I always find them very collaborative and wanting to listen to victims.

I would say, taking a step back, that the Home Office is presenting quite a challenging message to survivors of sexual exploitation at the moment, because on one hand they have the VAWG strategy, which is of course saying, “We’re here to identify and protect you,” and on the other hand they are introducing credibility assessments for survivors of sexual exploitation in the Immigration and Asylum Bill.

If survivors have similar accounts to any other survivors, if they have taken too long to come forward or if there are inconsistencies in their accounts, which we know can happen because of trauma, their credibility must be questioned under the new law changes. The reality, when I speak to frontline services and survivors, is that that is the main thing that is impacting on them at the moment. While the Government may be doing really good work across Government to try to identify and protect them, we are sending a message to survivors of sexual exploitation that, actually, their credibility is going to be questioned.

For this cohort, that is undermining the trust in the overall strategy and in the hope that Government are there to protect and help them. So while I am having positive interactions across Government, I don’t think I can overlook this harm that is being done in legislation at the moment.

Chair: That is interesting because we fought for so long for victims to be listened to and believed. Training through the College of Policing and everything else has all been about victims being believed in order to have the confidence to come forward, so what you say is worrying. Nicole, you make very powerful arguments for, perhaps, ownership of this to be at a different Department from the Home Office—in the Cabinet Office or No. 10 maybe.

We are going to move on, and Anna Dixon is going to continue on the financial side of things and Government leadership.

Q17 Anna Dixon: I have just one quick follow-up question on cross-departmental working. And thank you, Chair, for inviting me to guest from the Public Accounts Committee. We looked at the last Government’s VAWG strategy, and one of our conclusions was that “the Home Office failed to secure the full support of other government departments.” Are we at risk of history repeating itself, Dame Nicole?

Dame Nicole Jacobs: Yes, we are. Again, it’s somewhat of a mixed picture. I will lead with a good example. This strategy didn’t say how much the Department of Health would commit to Steps to Safety, and they committed far more than I thought they would when I read the strategy a year ago, so that’s positive.

I didn’t mention MHCLG a moment ago, and that was a huge oversight, because of part 4 of the Domestic Abuse Act. There is some serious resource and commitment to accommodation-based duties. There has been a real willingness in that Department to see the intersections between domestic abuse and homelessness. So I should have said that earlier.
