Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Dr Ian Higginson and Matthew Hopkins.

Chair: Welcome to this one-off session of the Health and Social Care Committee. We will be examining winter pressures, although the pressures are now year round, which I am sure will be reflected in the questions and answers. We will start with our panellists introducing themselves and telling us a little about what they do. Can we start with Ian, please?

Dr Higginson: I am Dr Ian Higginson. I am a consultant in emergency medicine and paediatric emergency medicine, working in Plymouth. I am here in my role as the president of the Royal College of Emergency Medicine, which represents 16,000 experts in emergency care, both doctors and advanced practitioners. We are basically the experts in emergency care for the UK.

Matthew Hopkins: I am Matthew Hopkins. I am the acute and ambulance network director at the NHS Alliance. For those who are less familiar, the NHS Alliance was recently formed from the merger of NHS Providers and NHS Confederation. We are a membership organisation. We represent the majority of trusts and ICBs across the NHS. Through working with our members, the intention is to improve the delivery of care for the people we serve across England.

Chair: This is an issue that MPs’ inboxes get full up with every year. We thought it was timely to have the session now because, rather than examine the problem after the fact, we want to look at how well the planning has gone for this year. We had a session on corridor care with the Minister back in the spring and had very warm words from the Department about how it is much more on it this year and wants to bear down on this; I have had this said privately and publicly a number of times. Ian, from your perspective, how has winter planning gone this year? Is it any different? Is it better?

Dr Higginson: I would probably call it more winter preparations than planning at this stage.

Chair: What is the difference? Why is there a distinction in the word used?

Dr Higginson: A plan implies a concrete plan, whereas we are seeing preparations, which are perhaps potentially more diffuse. Just to give you some background, we are going into winter with our departments already in big trouble, and many are on their knees. I say that because something like 67% of departments in the survey last month said they were either fairly or very unsafe already. We are in worse shape than in previous years, and 88% of our clinical leads told us that in the same survey.

Our patients are still in corridors. I think you are aware that we did a spot prevalence survey earlier in the week. From that survey something like one in five patients in emergency departments at the time were in inappropriate areas. We know that about 16,000 of our patients in England are dying in excess of what is expected, which is linked to long waits in our departments.

We also know that our teams are exhausted, burnt out and psychologically distressed, and many are leaving. It has recently also become apparent that increasing violence and aggression—often related to long waits—is a particularly acute problem at the moment. When you put that in the context of the preparations, regrettably, we are saying much of the same old stuff.

Chair: Your answer to my question about whether it is different would be, not really.

Dr Higginson: There are some things that are different.

Chair: What is different?

Dr Higginson: There are some nuances about it. There is much more agreement, when you hear Ministers and various system leaders talking, that there is a system problem. There is much more agreement now that this is about the whole patient pathway. However, I do not think that that has yet translated into concrete action. While we see the words start to come out, the concrete preparations that underpin that are not there. If I am honest, when I look at what I am seeing, I am not seeing much that will make a difference. There are some announcements around money. That is possibly being directed at things that won’t make a difference. Much of what we need is actually long term and too late to make an impact on this winter. Worryingly, we are also seeing strong suggestions that money is king at the moment. That is getting in the way of what we would regard as some potential mitigations, which I am sure we will cover later.

Chair: When you say money is king, can you clarify what you mean?

Dr Higginson: There is a lot of financial pressure on leadership teams within hospitals and, I would imagine, within the wider systems. They are trying to balance priorities. For instance, about 60% of our clinical leads suggested that recruitment freezes were affecting nursing staff, and 40% that they were affecting consultants. That is in departments I have already described as actively unsafe, with staff that are pretty broken. There is a lot of balancing of financial priorities versus operational priorities going on. To me, it feels like money is the key financial driver, with performance either equal or a close second.

Chair: Matthew, looking at the wider system and its preparations, have you had any sense that this year is any different to previous years?

Matthew Hopkins: Ian is right. The picture is better in the context of the recognition that it is not just the A&E departments in each hospital that are responsible for flow and for making sure waiting times are not excessive. There has been a step change in that.

Chair: If everyone agrees on what you need to do to fix the problem, why did they not have time to plan ahead for something that would change this winter? It might just be incremental but the picture we have just had is that it is probably not going to make much difference. In fact, you are describing that we are starting from a worse basis. What is your sense of that?

Matthew Hopkins: Our members, as well as the membership for Ian’s organisation, are very much saying that, given the challenges during the summer—the demand increase—there is now no longer a summer lull across the NHS. People are working flat out. Of course, this is in the context of changes to the way in which integrated care boards operate.

Chair: Is it your view that the changes that are happening to ICBs are negatively affecting our ability—

Matthew Hopkins: Absolutely.

Chair: That is very clear. In our previous sparring sessions with whichever Secretary of State we might have in front of us—hopefully we have a period of stability now—they would tell us that they have guidance, are planning better, and have started earlier. Notwithstanding that we have had a very difficult year, is there anything in the guidance that could be better?

Matthew Hopkins: Given the reality that an emergency pathway flows from the work that GPs do to deal with urgent issues, through to the work that the ambulance service does in not conveying people who do not need to go to a hospital, and the work that is done within hospitals, there are elements of improvement required in all those areas. That requires the system—all those partners—to focus on the importance of providing good urgent and emergency care. The guidance sets out what each part needs to do but we know that across the country there is unwarranted variation in the way in which leadership prioritise and the way in which people work effectively together, both senior leadership and local operational leadership.

Chair: Can you spell out what you mean by local operational leadership?

Matthew Hopkins: The reality for the ambulance service is that it is measured in minutes in terms of its category 2 performance. The emergency department is also measured in minutes in relation to the four-hour wait; the clock is always ticking. As you move further out through the process of discharging patients out of hospital, you see that the organisations do not operate to the same drumbeat.

Chair: You have one part of the system that is in seconds, another in hours, and another in days and weeks.

Matthew Hopkins: Exactly, and therefore the leadership challenge in each system is to make sure that risk is managed and shared across the emergency pathway. For example, we see this in places such as Airedale in West Yorkshire, where they have come together as a system to manage effectively and make sure that people, for example in nursing homes, do not get moved into an A&E department; they get proper triage. There are really good examples across the country but the variation is the element that we are working with our membership to try to address.

Dr Gardner: You touched quite a lot on some of my subject areas and I am very pleased that you said “whole patient pathway” approach because the focus is sometimes heavily on ambulance delivery and the performance of the emergency departments. Often, and certainly within my own local hospital, the block is actually the flow out of ED and then you have that backlog. I am trying to jump the gun a little here but, Ian, I was very concerned about the survey where 67% of clinical leads in the emergency departments describe their ED as actively unsafe. Could you just expand on what that might mean, the risks involved and what is particularly driving this? If it is worse than previous years, why?

Dr Higginson: I will explain how the survey works. The survey is run through college communication channels directly to clinical leads. We ran this particular survey on 1 October. We had 163 responses, which is a really high response rate for this sort of survey and is directly from the mouths of clinical leaders. It is deliberately pitched as a sentiment survey: the language used is the sort of language you would use rather than drilling right down into it, because we are conscious of survey fatigue. To enable us to come to you with up-to-date evidence, we need to be able to do it quick and fast but we lose some granularity. Just be aware of that.

The question was basically, do your departments feel unsafe, with a Likert scale ranging from very to perfectly safe? Some 67% rated their EDs as fairly unsafe or very unsafe. Importantly, only 15% actually said their departments were safe. What do we mean by that question? It is usually whether we feel our patients are actively coming to harm. In almost every case the driver is overcrowding. Emergency departments are unsafe when you have patients absolutely everywhere and you cannot do your job properly.

In the old days, a typical shift for a consultant in charge of an emergency department was to answer questions from less experienced practitioners, go and see patients with them, teach, support, and supervise. My job now and the job of virtually all my colleagues is essentially traffic control and risk management: trying to find spaces for the next patient, trying to find a resuscitation bed for that next red phone call because our resuscitation room might be full, and trying to work out which patients we need to prioritise if we have very long waits. I might go to the waiting room to apologise to our poor patients who have been waiting there for hours and explain what is going on.

Dr Gardner: I understand those pressures and I do not think anybody here would be surprised at the things that you are listing. What I am trying to push is why it is worse than previous years. Why is it particularly now that is the problem?

Dr Higginson: It is not particularly now that it is the problem. This has been an emerging crisis for many years. It is a bit of groundhog day, is it not? I was before you in March essentially saying the same story, and if you bring me in in a few years’ time I would be telling you the same story. My predecessor was brought in and told you the same story. This is not a “right now” problem; this is a “getting worse and worse and worse” problem.

Dr Gardner: Why?

Dr Higginson: We are not adequately dealing with the root causes of the problem. The main safety problem is overcrowding. Overcrowding is caused because we either cannot find alternatives to admission for our patients or we cannot find them a bed when they need it. The reason we cannot find them a bed when they need it is that our hospitals do not work effectively across the working week. They are essentially weekday and a bit of evening services, so patients stay too long in hospital, and then when patients are ready to leave a large number of them are not able to do so. Something like 10% of our hospital bed base—about 13,000 beds—is occupied by patients who do not need to be there.

Dr Gardner: Thank you for that explanation. You are now referencing the flow issue. I worry that we focus at discharge and at admission, and we do not focus at that middle pathway. That is really important. In the report, there were some actions to mitigate the impact of overcrowding. I read somewhere in these horrible statistics—somebody might have to correct me—that if a patient stays over a certain length of time in ED it doubles their risk of dying within 30 days. It was quite shocking. Was it four hours or 12 hours?

Dr Higginson: The mortality impact begins at five hours and progressively gets worse.

Dr Gardner: That really concerns me. You suggested some mitigations. One of them is to require trusts and hospitals with overcrowded EDs to open and staff every available bed in acute community and intermediate care settings. I would like to explore that and look at flow-critical services and make sure that they are open 12 hours a day, seven days a week. Could you expand on these suggestions that you made? If money is a critical factor, how could we deliver these?

Dr Higginson: First, one bit of my previous answer that we did not get to is that I described the pathway and you asked why it has got worse. It is basically because the focus has been predominantly on the front—trying to persuade patients not to come to us—rather than fixing the problems with the flow. We are now approaching winter and those problems are not fixed.

As a royal college, what can we suggest that could be done now to try to make things a little better, given that our membership is telling us it is pretty rubbish out there and we are really worried about how it is going? The changes needed to fix what I have described are not quick fixes. When we proposed the mitigations, we were looking at what could be done right now that might have an impact right now. This is in a letter that we wrote to the Secretaries of State and, in a bit more detail, to the leaders of the NHS. This letter went out to Secretaries of State across four nations but we are England-focused here. We suggested five things.

First, we are going into winter, and I believe we are still 1,000 beds down compared to last year, yet in a worsening situation. Our request was that we require organisations and trusts to open and staff every available bed in hospitals experiencing overcrowding because we know the major problem is flow. For instance, we know some hospitals may still not have opened spare capacity they might be saving for winter. Matthew will be able to fill you in but I imagine that that would largely be due to financial reasons.

Secondly, there is a tension between the ambulance handover target, which says we must hand over quickly, and whether we are essentially dumping patients into already overcrowded emergency departments in order to fix that target. Our suggestion was that you should not be able to implement what is called a rapid drop-off policy unless the hospital is in the highest state of escalation, because to be generating that risk, things must be bad if you are implementing that protocol.

Thirdly, we suggested, ahead of the game, requiring ICBs and trusts to ensure that flow-critical services are open 12 hours a day—not 24—seven days a week. The reason we did that is that I am about to get a whole bunch of letters from departments saying, “Pharmacy is open these hours over Christmas.” That letter is really telling me when pharmacy is shut. We get loads of these letters telling us when services are open that are really saying, “We are not open here.” Over the Christmas period we know that things drop off. We were saying, “Plan ahead.” When we wrote it, there was time to plan rotas and resources to get those services open.

Fourthly, end recruitment freezes in services that are critical to flow, where they exist. Fifthly, prioritise local measures to support emergency department teams: psychological and physical security measures, given we know the stress they are under. Those are the five things we could think of that could be done right now. The only barrier would either be will or money, and we think there would be a bit of a mitigation. I think that answers your question.

Dr Gardner: I hear what you are saying. This is a frustration because we have been reducing bed numbers, with the laudable aim of people being cared for in their homes. Maybe the balance has tipped, but I have a whole load of empty community hospitals that have wards just standing there. It is very frustrating; they have been very useful in the past. The role of community hospitals would be very useful.

Matthew, I am going to come to you because I am interested in time. We have been dealing with quite a complex set of mergers and changes within the whole health ecosystem. We have the NHS England merger and modernisation and the ICB reorganisation, which is currently on hold because we are just trying to find ourselves, and a lot of expected financial savings. Will this impact NHS capacity to prepare for and navigate the pressures? Has this caused problems for trusts in terms of dealing with this?

Matthew Hopkins: Yes. The specific element of the changes around ICBs is that they previously had a convener role. They would bring all the key partners—ambulance services, acute trusts, community trusts, mental health trusts and local authorities—together to ask how to make sure that they are working optimally and in a way that enables urgent emergency care and patient flow to work effectively. Part of the challenge for ICBs is that their capacity has been cut by 50%. They are currently unclear about their role in performance management and convening. Is that the region’s responsibility or the ICB’s? There has been significant leadership churn, which in a number of systems has led to some loss of skills and capacity around convening urgent emergency care. That is the reality.

As I said, there are places across the country that are effectively working together. However, as Ian said, there is a significant trade-off at the moment where money and hitting financial targets are impacting the ability to do what Ian described would be short-term measures that could help, with the focus on reducing staffing numbers and where you can make appropriate savings. Closing a 30-bed ward probably saves about £2 million to £2.5 million recurrently, which are big numbers. Obviously the opposite is true of reopening a ward for winter pressures, particularly if you are going to need to use temporary staff, which is another measure that the NHS is being pushed to reduce—the dependence on bank and agency staff. The trade-offs are part of the picture.

Dr Gardner: Have you a suggested solution to help manage this winter?

Matthew Hopkins: From looking right across our membership, there are a number of things that we think could help, alongside the things that Ian has described. For example, the ambulance service is doing a lot of really good work around making sure that they are only taking people in that need to be taken. There is also further work that the ambulance service could be doing, and in some cases is, by innovating and going into people’s homes earlier in the pathway. For example, one ambulance service has a van that does on-site blood tests, whereas previously patients would have been brought into a hospital for that. Innovations could be put in place quickly if resources were available and prioritised.

Dr Gardner: Collating those innovations is great. One thing that I know is now mentioned is the difference between summer planning and winter planning and there being no lull now. We are due to have a very hot summer next year, and I know that that impacts people very much, particularly those with neurological conditions such as epilepsy and Alzheimer’s, and often the elderly. It is a change in demographic that might be impacted in the summer. Do you know when summer planning—not preparation—is expected to start? Will it overlap with the winter pressure period? Given that this seems to be the trend going forward, do you think it makes sense to still plan for specific seasonal pressures as winter versus summer, in the way the NHS does, or is there an alternative approach?

Dr Higginson: The real basics of planning are: look at the demand you are likely to face on your service, predict it and then plan for it. If you look at the basic attendance figures for emergency departments over the last 10 years, apart from an anomaly over the covid period, those attendances have consistently risen year on year. It should not be a surprise that more patients pitch up in urgent and emergency care services every year because that is what happens and what continues to happen. If you look at the demographics, it is going to continue to happen. Part of the failure of planning is simply not recognising the data in front of us telling us what is happening out there. Let us prepare for it. If we were any other industry, we would be saying, “Look at this demand, let us prepare for it, build the capacity, and build the skills so that we can see ahead.”

Chair: Why is that?

Dr Higginson: I ponder on this a lot, and it is partly recognised, but a lot of NHS planning is a triumph of optimism over reality. There is a tendency to put a lot of stock in the demand management side: let us look at that demand and try to keep it under control. Planning for patients with a sore throat and helping them see their GPs will not help an 83-year-old lady who has fallen over, has a broken hip and needs a bed. The investment and attention have been on the wrong thing. Everybody now seems to agree that that is the problem, which is really welcome. I heard the Secretary of State say it at your Committee very recently. However, we are not putting in the planning to back up those words. We are still relying on the wrong things.

When summer comes, demand is going to be up. There is no point calling it unprecedented; it is going to be unprecedented almost every day because it is just going up. There was a heatwave that definitely made things worse, but that will happen again; you can predict it. This winter more patients will get respiratory illnesses. There will be flu, RSV and norovirus. We know that that is going to happen. All we need to do is accept that and plan for it.

The basic failure is not accepting that demand is doing what it is doing and we have to actually invest in our health and social care services to deal with the demographic and health realities. We have avoided looking at those really big questions because the numbers can be quite scary; they are certainly expensive and it requires long-term planning, which does not necessarily fit with short-term political or leadership cycles.

Matthew Hopkins: The challenge for many organisations during the heatwaves was building stock: aged buildings and the requirement to keep data centres, MRI scanners and operating theatres cool. Most hospitals have cooling systems in sunlight all day, which puts significant pressure on them. We have consistently called for capital allocations to help organisations address this. Given the scale of the NHS maintenance backlog sitting at about £18 billion, there needs to be targeted capital investment into helping right across GP surgeries, community hospitals, mental health and acute hospitals so that they can be more resilient from the building stock perspective.

Paulette Hamilton: My questions are going to be directed towards you, Matthew, because they are about the ambulance service and some of the challenges. You have sort of answered my first question: what factors are driving the challenges that you are currently facing in the ambulance service?

Matthew Hopkins: There are multiple factors. Ian talked about the rising demand and how best the ambulance service can respond to that. Most of the 10 organisations across the country now have pretty good see-and-treat and hear-and-treat mechanisms. When people ring for an ambulance, they talk to a clinician, so clinical advice is being given. The ambulance service is moving away from being seen as an organisation that only conveys somebody from A to B, and instead focuses on treatment over the phone or in people’s homes and on directing them.

Paulette Hamilton: Just moving on slightly, we are always looking at performance. Do you believe that if you can do what you have just said the service wants to do, you would improve performance?

Matthew Hopkins: The performance question is interesting because only about 10% of ambulance activity is going to accidents and life-and-death situations. The big performance measure that the ambulance trusts are measured on is called category 2, which may be people who are experiencing severe, urgent and emergency situations, but the data suggests that only 50% of people who have an ambulance sent out under category 2 actually need it. The real question is what the right performance measures for ambulance services are, rather than just a time from A to B.

Paulette Hamilton: For me, performance is really important, and you have said how important it is. Where are you with that piece of work?

Matthew Hopkins: We are working closely with ambulance trusts at the moment to rethink what the more appropriate measures should be for them to be measured on. There is agreement that waiting-related targets are important, but there is also a sense that this is about what the quality outcomes are that are important from a patient perspective, alongside how long people have to wait for an ambulance to arrive. We are in the early stages of that review, but that is part of the NHS Alliance stepping back and asking whether the constitutional standards are the right ones now in the modern-day NHS for organisations to be measured against.

Paulette Hamilton: I will move on because I am going through this slowly. Is there anything within the ambulance service that could be addressed? Do you believe it is the ambulance service itself where the problem is, or is it the wider system?

Matthew Hopkins: As I said, the parts of the country that are doing well around urgent emergency care are those that work really effectively together where there is good leadership, good clinical engagement and consistency around risk sharing across the organisations. The ambulance service has obviously got a part to play. We know that the cause of corridor care and ambulances waiting outside A&E departments is multifactorial, but it is a system-wide challenge.

Paulette Hamilton: I am going to be a little more challenging. I am fed up with people telling me it is system-wide. I am a health professional, so I understand that term. Explain this to me: when you say system-wide, where do you feel the blockages are? Going into ED is a nightmare. Ambulances cannot get in and they are waiting longer than they should. Come on now, where in this system do you think we may have issues?

Matthew Hopkins: There will be areas for improvement in every part of the system. We know that since covid the number of patients that are in acute hospital beds and mental health hospital beds that should be discharged has risen. A number of our acute trust members describe perhaps between 20% and 30% of their bed base having patients that do not meet the criteria to be in that bed.

The one key area that parts of the country are focusing on is that bit around delayed discharges. As we have heard already from Ian, the point about flow backing up into EDs and waiting rooms is where there is significant risk. The ambulance service would also say that ambulances waiting outside an ED to hand over is creating risk for people who are waiting in their homes for an ambulance, which is why I am describing the fact that this is multifactorial and it is across the system. If our membership right across the board, along with community and social care services, reduces that delayed discharge percentage in hospitals, it would make a difference.

Paulette Hamilton: You are starting to warm me up now. My next point leads on from what you have just said. If you had a crystal ball throughout this system, where you say it is multifaceted, where in the system would you like to see some real improvements to help address the issues you are currently facing?

Matthew Hopkins: I go back to the point that the real challenges in acute and mental health trusts are delayed transfers of care—people who are in beds that no longer need them—and we need to see everybody working more effectively together on that. Healthwatch England has recently published a members’ survey that shows that patient experience is really poor when waiting unnecessarily long for discharge. There is a flow challenge, but there is also a patient experience challenge. Of all the elements of improvement that are required, that is the area that would make a difference.

Paulette Hamilton: The Health Service Journal described ambulance performance in September as declining sharply. Meeting waiting times in many of these places is actually now out of reach. Emergency departments are overwhelmed. We cannot get patients in or out during many times of the week. With this backdrop in mind, how confident are you that ambulance performance can be improved and patients will not continue to be treated in an inappropriate setting?

Matthew Hopkins: It is a really important question. What we hear from our ambulance trust members is that in the past, across a year, they have been able to bank some good performance during the summer period that would then be offset by the challenges that we have heard already around winter, which means that, across the year, they were able to meet the waiting time targets that were set. The challenge is now consistent throughout the year, including the likelihood of continuing heatwaves, so that is creating the real performance challenge that, as you quoted, the data tells you.

This year, it is going to be very, very difficult. In fact, it will be unlikely that the NHS meets its category 2 target because of the difficult period that we have had during the summer in the heatwave. The question that ambulance trusts are asking themselves, and that we are supporting them with, is, how do we get those innovations that are happening in each of the ambulance organisations consistently across the country, in all those services, and how do we make sure organisations have the right resources? You have probably seen reference to ambulance trusts raising concerns about their ability to respond to a major incident. These are the kinds of things that need to be addressed to support ambulance trusts to be able to improve their performance.

Andrew George: Matthew, the issue of delayed discharge, which you were referring to in answers just a moment ago, has been endemic in the service for the last 30 to 40 years. One of your predecessor organisations—the NHS Confederation—I made lots of comments on at the time. It reflected what NHS managers were pushing for at that stage and what had, indeed, been going on for some time, and it has happened and been delivered, perhaps surreptitiously. I do not have the figures in front of me, but something like 10% to 15% of beds have been cut since that time. Obviously, delayed discharges are still with us. Do you think there is such a thing as a golden bullet? Is this something that successive Governments and healthcare managers have simply failed to do? Is there something that can be done to address this?

Matthew Hopkins: I do not want to repeat myself, but there are opportunities. We see examples of this across the country where there are really effective ways of working with health and social care partners that enable beds to be freed up. The problem is that, in a number of acute trusts, beds become available only after they are needed in order to create the flow that emergency departments have. There is still quite a bit to do in a number of acute trusts.

Chair: I do not understand that.

Matthew Hopkins: Patients who are simple discharges because they do not require a social care package, a commode or changes to their home should be discharged before 1 pm in order for beds to then be available for flow in the organisation.

Chair: People get stuck overnight.

Matthew Hopkins: It creates a bit of movement and bed availability for those patients that might need to be admitted. There is definitely room for improvement in many organisations in that regard. There are lots of things being focused on, but getting it right in every element of that is where people fail.

Andrew George: There is no one single bullet and you are talking about the multifactorial, but you are saying that some parts of the country have done it. Are you saying that that can or cannot be replicated?

Matthew Hopkins: The challenge that the NHS has had historically is being able to share and adopt good practice, and part of our job at the alliance is to try to help and support to make that happen.

Dr Higginson: The search for the magic bullet is part of the problem. This is a complex, or wicked, problem and by definition there is no single solution.

Andrew George: It is a bad question.

Dr Higginson: No, it is a good question because it exposes the problem. The search for the magic bullet has led to focus on the wrong things, with an expectation that it will solve things that it will not. As an emergency physician, if you say, “What is the problem?” the answer is, “Beds.” The root cause for us is that if you say to me on the shop floor, “What do you need?” I need enough staff to see the patients. They are brilliant in the right place. Once we have that, I either need an alternative to admission or a bed for a patient who needs it.

There are probably three key drivers for the bed for a patient who needs it: getting hospitals working effectively so patients do not stay in longer than they need to and everything works smoothly; focus on delayed discharges; and the number of beds we have in the system. When all that is done, we probably do not have enough beds. That is when you get to the bits that have not been fixed. The search for the magic bullet combined with having collective heads stuck in sand and not acting on what has been happening progressively over the years is the root of the problem. The solutions are all at the back door. If our emergency departments were not overcrowded, we would free up ambulances. They would be back on the road like they were in the old days and performance would improve.

The last thing to say on the points Matthew has raised is that there is loads and loads of innovation happening with ambulance services trying to avoid admission. The ambulance services are brilliant; they hate bringing patients who do not need it to hospital as much as the patients hate it. Services need to be there when they are needed. For example, if a patient needs a frailty intervention or investigations that can be done at home on a Saturday, it will not happen, because it is a Saturday and they will end up at our door. The emergency department becomes a default for when the system fails because it is not operating when it needs to. That comes back to the same thing: systems working operationally seven days a week and over extended hours. That would support the ambulance services, us, and the hospitals. Therefore, the one thing is beds, and there are four things that drive that.

Chair: We are always looking for the thing to suggest to the Department that would actually be the right measure. At the moment, it puts a lot of stock in the four-hour wait and all that stuff, but is the better measure the number of beds available at 1pm?

Dr Higginson: The four-hour standard as a single measure of a whole system is unmatched and we cannot think of anything better. The patients want to be seen quickly and efficiently; it is what we as clinicians want, it is directly linked to outcomes, and it relates directly to hospital occupancies, which is what you are talking about. If you are talking about a broader suite of things underneath it, hospital occupancy is the next thing to go for.
