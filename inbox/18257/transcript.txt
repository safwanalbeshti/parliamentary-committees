Witnesses: Dr Angela Needle, Jenny Phillips and Charles McAllister.

Chair: Welcome to this morning’s session of the Energy Security and Net Zero Select Committee and our latest session on managing the future of UK oil and gas. We start with a panel on the gas supply and the network and I will ask you to introduce yourselves.

Jenny Phillips: Hello. I am Jenny Phillips. I am the Director of Energy Delivery at National Gas. I am responsible for the real-time operation of the gas transmission system in Great Britain.

Dr Needle: Hello, everyone. I am Angela Needle. I am Strategy Director at Cadent. Cadent looks after half of the UK’s gas distribution network.

Charles McAllister: Hello. I am Charles McAllister. I am the Director of the Green Gas Taskforce. The taskforce is a campaign group that represents the 17 largest biomethane generators, shippers and traders, all the GB gas networks, key sector groups and trade associations.

Chair: Thank you all very much. You are very welcome. We look forward to your evidence this morning. Angela, I will start with you. Can you tell us to what extent the UK can rely on domestic gas production to generate electricity to heat buildings and to maintain its industrial base?

Dr Needle: On domestic gas, clearly we have gas from the North Sea and we have the opportunity to generate biomethane. There is opportunity, while importing gas from overseas, to ensure that we also explore the UK’s resources. I am sure both Jenny and Charles will have fuller answers to these particular questions.

Chair: Jenny, what is the balance between imports and domestic production?

Jenny Phillips: On an annual basis, domestic production accounts for between 40% and 45% of overall demand, with the rest of it coming from Norway, interconnectors from Europe, LNG and GB storage sites. The breakdown is that 50% of that goes to the distribution networks, about 20% goes to power stations, about 20% is exported, about 5% goes into storage and 2% goes to large industrial suppliers. On the volume of gas that is produced domestically daily, around 100 million cubic metres is the maximum capability. In the middle of summer that is sufficient to meet the entire demand in the UK. In winter, when peak demand is about 500 million cubic metres a day, that is about 20% and that is when you are more reliant on the Norwegian, LNG, interconnectors and GB storage, all of which can contribute approximately 20%, plus or minus, depending on what the market is doing and various conditions. It varies across the year how much demand domestic production can meet.

Chair: It has been suggested to us that Government policy favours imports over domestic production. Do you think that is true?

Jenny Phillips: As I have just said, we need gas from elsewhere. We do not have enough indigenous gas to meet demand in the winter.

Chair: But as a matter of policy?

Jenny Phillips: As a matter of policy, it is important that we can attract gas from overseas, that the market functions, it attracts the LNG boats, which it is doing at the moment, and then where necessary it attracts gas in from interconnectors as well. Historically, that market has been very successful in providing a diversity of supply that has ensured sufficient gas.

Chair: Can increasing domestic production bring prices down?

Jenny Phillips: The evidence recently from Jackdaw suggests that it would not—but it can increase energy security, which is important.

Chair: Charles, from what Jenny has just said, do you agree about the improvements in energy security by increasing our domestic production? To what degree is green gas an option for doing so?

Charles McAllister: Today there are 140 biomethane plants in GB. There is sufficient capacity in those plants to feed and decarbonise about 1 million homes. What is amazing is that those homes probably don’t even know they are carbonised. We commissioned a piece of work by an expert group called Alder BioInsights to assess the potential for biomethane in the UK. That analysis concluded that there is sufficient sustainable feedstock in the UK to generate 120 TWh of biomethane by 2050. That is more than a tenfold increase on existing capacity. Compared to existing gas demand, it is about 20% but compared to 2050 demand in the NESO central scenario, the holistic transition, we could be in the two thirds share, so it can make a material contribution to our energy security. To give a real example, 1 TWh of gas produced in this country, or biomethane, is the equivalent of one LNG tanker, so 120 TWh of biomethane is 120 LNG tankers equivalent.

Chair: How realistic is a tenfold increase?

Charles McAllister: Very. Not only did the analysis we commissioned conclude this, but NESO commissioned a piece of work, although theirs varied slightly—it said 109 TWh. The reason that is different is our analysis was UK-wide and NESO’s remit is GB only, so it does not include Northern Ireland.

Chair: What is the effect on price of that kind of switch?

Charles McAllister: One of the things that we can do from biomethane, if we supply it into the market, is biomethane can help to set the marginal price. The good thing about biomethane is that the price is flat as a pancake. There is no variation in the cost. The average cost of producing biomethane in the UK is about £90 per megawatt-hour, with the potential to get the cost down by about 25% if we build bigger plants, unlock some of the co-benefits of biomethane such as digestate—we prefer to call it renewable fertiliser—and also bio-CO2. Biomethane plants produce a really pure stream of CO2 that you can use in industry, the health sector and so on. If you unlock those, it gets the cost down materially.

Chair: Can you quantify what you mean by materially?

Charles McAllister: We can get the cost down by 25%.

Chair: Over what timeframe?

Charles McAllister: That depends how quickly you can build bigger plants—if the Government were to introduce a future framework, which is a consultation we have been waiting on for quite some time, and introduced a policy that incentivised bigger plants and unlocked the benefits, valorised those feedstocks, it can be as soon as that.

I will add quickly that this Government are obviously very focused on the importance of local jobs and growth all over the country. For oil and gas you have to take a geology-first approach. You go with the geology that makes sense. For biomethane, because the feedstocks vary so broadly from food waste to animal waste, rotational crops or whisky waste, they are spread all over the country. If you spread out the 57,000 jobs that could be delivered if we unlock the sector’s maximum potential, that is about 90 in every single constituency of the UK. They are good, well-paid, skilled jobs, 20% above the national average, in every constituency.

Chair: Are these jobs for the transition for people working in other parts of the energy sector, in particular the existing gas system, or not really?

Charles McAllister: Whoever wants them.

Chair: For whoever wants them. They are skilled jobs, skilled engineering jobs?

Charles McAllister: Correct. It is engineering, plant design, plant management. If any of you would like to come and see a site, there are 140 from the top of Scotland to the south of England. I can help to facilitate that, no problem.

Chair: I am sure a Committee visit is pending.

Charles McAllister: Very good.

Wera Hobhouse: Can I ask a quick question? I had a meeting with Wales and West Utilities last week—very timely—and it was basically talking about grid connection and we are having a similar problem with the electricity grid. The grid is currently not really fit for providing a decentralised supply of gas. Can you expand a little bit on what it actually needs and also the investment that it needs to make your vision happen?

Dr Needle: Can I pick up this one, as it is a gas network question? Depending on where the biomethane production happens and where the gas network is in that location, you can put the gas in the network. For there to be space in the network for the biomethane to be added, there needs to be a pull from the network for that demand. We have mapped across our region looking at the capacity of the gas system. In some places there is no need to do what we call reinforcing the gas network to take biomethane and in some places there is a need to do that. It is dependent on location as to whether you would do that or not. The cost to reinforce is not significant, we already do that today, and currently the cost of reinforcement is on the producers to pay for rather than consumers. We can also look at hubs. With smaller biomethane plant opportunities we are looking at, rather than them all connecting individually, which is potentially expensive, them bringing their biomethane in to a central hub where you would then connect. There are lots of different types of solutions to managing good capacity to maximise this, but it is regionally specific.

Charles McAllister: Can I jump in very quickly on that? The reason I say the Green Gas Taskforce has done so well is because the biomethane producers and the gas network are working together to deliver a common result. The gas network is the necessary prerequisite to deliver biomethane at scale, nationally, to a broad range of end users and at least cost. If the gas network was not as expansive or did not exist or if we were not working so closely together, biomethane would have to be transported by other more expensive means. That is key to unlocking it.

Chair: Coming back to you what you said about costs, you said 25% off production costs. How much off bills?

Charles McAllister: Biomethane can provide stability to bills, so there is no volatility. That is the key benefit from biomethane today. The natural gas price today is 250p per therm.

Chair: That is three times what it was in January.

Charles McAllister: That is in the same range. We are paying a large amount of money to import gas when we could produce green gas in this country. Point 1, that is a material way to get well-paid, skilled jobs into every constituency. Point 2—another important point—UK content is a key part of maximising the broader benefits to UK plc. For every £1 you invest in biomethane, 85p stays here. That is because we have a really good supply chain that is able to build. We have been campaigning that biomethane is reliable, renewable and it is ready. It is a proven technology and the supply chain can deliver it. Importing it does not give us any of that.

Graeme Downie: I want to talk a bit about storage and security in particular. Jenny, we know that we have lower UK storage and EU storage than we have had for a number of years. Can you talk about what the implications are for UK energy security of that reduction in storage over this winter in particular?

Jenny Phillips: I would just like to take a minute to explain how GB storage works, because it is different from that in Europe. In GB there are known storage sites that are connected to the transmission network. They can fill up from empty in two weeks and they can empty from full in two weeks, give or take a couple of days, but that is not the way they operate. The way they operate is that they sit partly full and respond to what is going on in the market. Today they might be putting gas into the storage sites, but if there is a loss of supply or an increase in demand and the market needs to balance, that gas can stop going into the storage sites and it can turn around within a day. They provide a really important service 365 days a year to the market to provide flexibility, to go up and down as and when the market needs within a day. Having said that, if there is a cold snap coming, we also see that they fill up significantly before that cold snap, so they can either provide the flexibility of service or they can just flow the gas out into the network consistently.

That is really different from the model in Europe, where there are very large storage sites that very slowly fill up over the summer, and we have all been watching them very slowly fill up over the summer. The gas is there in the winter and it can very slowly and stably come out in the countries where the gas storage sites are. It is a different model and it provides an important flexible service to the market in GB.

Graeme Downie: To go back to the question, what are the implications for UK energy security from the amount of gas that is stored this winter either here or in Europe?

Jenny Phillips: I have described the way that it operates. It is one of five sources of gas in the UK. We have LNG. The LNG facilities have some storage capability. The boats land, they fill up the storage sites and there is an inherent level of storage there. There is some flexibility in our network and in the transmission networks to do with what we call linepack, the volume of gas that is in the network, which can vary during the day. We can get gas from Norway and interconnectors. When you add up all of those diverse sources of gas, there is sufficient flexibility to deliver the amount of gas that is required for energy security.

Graeme Downie: How do the costs of gas storage measure against the benefits of the UK system that you described? In your view, would you say it is a fairly effective system of storage at the moment? What are the costs of that versus the benefits that consumers see?

Jenny Phillips: I am not going to comment on the cost of gas storage. I am not able to comment on that, but the value that it provides, the flexibility it provides to consumers, basically means you don’t have to get gas from elsewhere. It is of value to consumers. I am just not able to quantify that.

Graeme Downie: We received evidence about floating storage regasification units, if I can try to say that at this time of the morning. How much of a role do you think they will play in future energy security in the UK?

Jenny Phillips: This is where we are looking forward to the Government publishing the outcome of their gas system transition consultation. We are hoping that will set a security supply standard that will define maybe the volume of gas that the Government want to be held onshore in different types of sites for different durations, and the FSRUs could be an important part of that security standard going forward. The options to have gas could include big strategic storage sites, the likes of Rough but not limited to Rough. It could be the FSRUs. They can provide a really flexible service, I believe for about four days, where they can just provide gas into the network. It could be that you have different contracts with interconnectors or you contract for some of the gas in the LNG storage tanks.

All of those are options that it would be good to explore. There are different costs and benefits associated with them but they are all predicated on having a clear articulation of the risk that the Government want to take, what level of security of supply they want to secure, and then the market participants and ourselves would engage in that and try to get to the most effective answer.

Graeme Downie: Peak demand for gas may not change for the next decade. Do we need these kinds of new technologies to maintain the supply to consumers over that time? What is the point where, as demand falls off past the decade and beyond, the system no longer needs those kinds of things and what is the role of those technologies and storage while we are still at peak demand?

Jenny Phillips: It is a really important point that as long as there is that peak level of demand, you have to have the supply infrastructure, the network infrastructure and the right markets to attract the gas to come in and to have the physical capability to move it around. As you say, at least for the next decade that level of peak will be—

Graeme Downie: Is there a risk that as peak demand falls, it is harder to attract gas because our demand is falling? Is there a risk of a cliff edge, essentially, where we are no longer at peak demand so it is harder to attract and the supply then falls off?

Jenny Phillips: That is looking long term as a result of the gas system in transition consultation. It is important to make sure that we do not fall off a cliff. That is a different market mechanism. We already have a standard. For example, for gas transporters, we all have a standard. We have to have a network that is capable of meeting gas demand that you would see once in every 20 years. On a really cold day with really high power generation demand and really high domestic demand, we have to be able to transport gas from wherever it comes in to wherever it is going. We will continue to maintain our networks, receive funding from the regulator to do so efficiently and safely for as long as that network is required. To attract the supply infrastructure, you have to provide different incentives to the market because the risk is that as the overall volume goes down, they do not want to bring the gas in.

Graeme Downie: I guess there is a risk that as demand falls, cost increases as a result because we have to spend more to make sure we are getting the supply and then past peak demand. Is that a risk?

Jenny Phillips: It is market mechanisms where the gas comes from, and I do not have a global ability to forecast the price of it. It is important that we set a really clear articulation of risk so the market knows what it is, knows what the requirement is and then you can run a process that gets you the most efficient way to attract those suppliers into GB.

Graeme Downie: I just want to move on to some broader questions about energy security and the role of the Government. Angela, are you confident that there is enough natural gas for UK households to heat their homes this winter?

Dr Needle: For the gas distribution network, we receive all our gas via National Gas’s transmission system, supported with biomethane coming in. As long as Jenny and National Gas are happy with the supply, we are content. From a security of supply point of view, we have been given advice that this winter is secure.

Graeme Downie: When you say you have been given advice, who is that from? Jenny is saying, “We have got enough gas, don’t worry, it is coming”?

Dr Needle: Yes, Jenny.

Graeme Downie: Just the two of you in the room.

Dr Needle: It is just me and Jenny managing the gas. From a security of supply point of view, it is worth saying that as a gas system we regularly run exercises to make sure that we are clear on what would happen should any security of supply issues transpire. We do that annually.

Graeme Downie: To pick up on that, what is the timescale for your annual exercise? How much notice do you give yourself for, “We are about to not have enough gas”? Is it a day, a week, six months, six weeks?

Dr Needle: No, we run an exercise that says, “Today we have a gas outage—what do we do?”. It could be a cyber thing. We run a range of different exercises regularly.

Graeme Downie: Can you give us a little bit of information about what those exercises tell you and what the biggest risks or threats might be? You mentioned cyber. What are the kinds of things where you say, “That is higher up the risk register than something. We need to do more training or more exercises for that”?

Dr Needle: Cyber is clearly important right now, but all the incidents that you ever look at end up with no gas at some point, so that is the outcome. Whether it is supply shortage or a cyber issue or a security issue or a fault with kit or equipment, that is the outcome. You end up managing that: how do you manage security of supply; how do you manage it so that you are communicating effectively and you are looking after vulnerable customers and managing industry and critical sectors? We have a sector-by-sector plan of what we will do in that instance, so everything matters.

Graeme Downie: What are the most important sectors that come up—“We really must make sure we are doing that”?

Dr Needle: We have an official hierarchy.

Graeme Downie: Can you reveal any of that or is that—

Dr Needle: That is probably best for a separate conversation.

Graeme Downie: Fair enough, no problem. Just thinking about the long term, Angela, is the way that we are currently producing or storing natural gas sufficient? Do you have enough long-term security as well?

Dr Needle: This is an upstream question.

Charles McAllister: My view is that this country is and has been screaming out for a gas supply plan—that encompasses obviously lots of biomethane, I hope, but outlines clearly where it is coming from and what the plan to procure that is. The UK typically procures LNG on the spot market, so we outbid 43 other countries. Many Government documents have been released on this, which typically say, “We are confident in the ability to meet our energy security, because we are confident we will outbid the other 43 countries to get it”. Will that always be the case? That is a matter for discussion. My argument is that a gas supply plan that maximises the amount of green gas we produce in this country minimises the amount we would have to bid to out-compete other countries on the spot market.

Graeme Downie: Some evidence from previous sessions is that security of supply is also a major cost. Whether the UK can afford to outbid is one thing; whether consumers can afford to buy it is the second thing, and security is linked to both.

Charles McAllister: One of the things we have been discussing as a sector is whether we could adopt a contract supply for biomethane, fix a price of biomethane in to specific end users or some sort of contract policy or a different style of mechanism to TPC, and whether that is a good way as part of a pragmatic gas supply plan to maximise: unlock more green gas, give greater energy security and so on.

Graeme Downie: I have one more question. Jenny, the Government have talked about the possibility of a strategic gas storage reserve. Given your description of the current gas storage system in the UK, do you think there is value in that? How might that work and be paid for?

Jenny Phillips: This is where we are actively talking to DESNZ. We are interested in the different models that could come up. One option could be that the Government could decide to buy the gas. They could direct us or the shippers to put it in storage and they would decide when the gas comes out.

Graeme Downie: So rather than letting it flex from zero to 100, you can only flex from 100 to 40 or something like that? It would fix that.

Jenny Phillips: Yes, but it is important to know it could be provided from a range of different providers. We have touched on FSRU, LNG and interconnectors. It is understanding what the standard actually is. There will be different ways to achieve that. There might be a demand side response that we can get in there as well. It is important to have a really clear articulation of risk so that we can get the right answer.

Graeme Downie: Do you want to say two or three of the things that might work best? Then I promise I will stop, Chair.

Jenny Phillips: My job is to move it around from wherever it comes in. We have the connections and the network that can be used to shift the gas around, so I am agnostic. The more gas, the better, as far as I am concerned.

Chair: Angela, a question for you, I think. We have heard about the delays in the future policy for biomethane. What effect is that having on the gas network and your customers?

Dr Needle: To be really clear, there is no impact of having or not having biomethane on the gas distribution network. Biomethane and natural gas are clinically identical, and so one just displaces the other, as would a blend of hydrogen. The gas distribution network is here and the amount of it that we have is about the demand for the gas network.

Chair: Do you share Charles’s concerns that we can outbid everybody else until we cannot?

Dr Needle: I am concerned about the fact that we have a really good and steady incremental growth in biomethane in this country and, as Charles explained, biomethane is more than just a means of generating gas. The production of biomethane on sites in farming communities has a range of growth and commercial benefits to the UK. In addition, it displaces natural gas. Popping it into the gas network, people using greener gas as a means to decarbonise without even knowing it, is an invisibly helpful thing to do. The fact that we have a subsidy scheme that is ending in 2028, although there has been an extension to 2030, means that for those who want to develop biomethane plants—and they take a while to develop because there is planning and all the kinds of things that go with building even small infrastructure—there is uncertainty and there are now projects waiting to put green gases on to the system because of the uncertainty on the subsidy scheme. The subsidy scheme is just £4 on the gas bill.

Chair: Right, so less than a pint of Guinness.

Dr Needle: Less than a cup of coffee at the Doncaster train station when I came in this morning. It seems to be a bit of no-brainer, considering the focus on decarbonisation coupled with energy resilience and growth that you should look to see if we can unlock that sooner rather than later.

Chair: Everybody says it is just £4 on the bill. The Chancellor said we are not going to put any more levies on bills. Is that not a problem in itself?

Dr Needle: Adding anything on to bills is a problem right now. We know that. It is clear and it is not just for biomethane. It goes for every subsidy across the entire net zero spectrum. You could single out biomethane, but you could do the same for any other subsidy. There are some things that you could play with in the subsidies that make them palatable if you are very worried about adding just £4. For example, in the last regulatory period, the regulator Ofgem added accelerated depreciation into gas bills, which added £10 on to the gas bill. Knowing that we are going to use a lot of the gas distribution network still—and I will come on to answering those questions—it may be premature to do that and that £10 could be used to subsidise biomethane without any changes to bills.

Chair: Charles: no more levies. Can you do it without putting the cost on to consumers?

Charles McAllister: When the Government release the future framework we will have clarity on their preferred model to back the sector. As I said, by 2028 it will go up to about £4.80, so again less than an average pint of beer—I checked; it is £5.50. The Green Gas Support Scheme is the existing mechanism. Previously it was the Renewable Heat Incentive. What this sector has shown is that when the Government provide support they will build it, plants will be built. There are 140 plants today. During the end of the RHI 70 were built between 2014 to 2016. The sector responds quickly because the supply chain is there to deliver it, local jobs, boosting our energy security and so on.

Chair: Burning methane produces NOx; the NOx from home use of methane in London is greater than that produced from transport in London. How green is biomethane?

Dr Needle: I am not sure that is true. There have been standards set on boilers, if that is what you are referring to, that reduce the NOx emissions significantly. That may be a question for the EUA in the next session.

Chair: Those are peer reviewed analyses. What is your peer reviewed analysis to counter that?

Dr Needle: I do not have it to hand—it was not a question I was expecting—but we can look at the data and get back to you on that.

Chair: You were not expecting a question? Okay.

Charles McAllister: On how green biomethane is—

Dr Needle: Sorry, we are not responsible for manufacture of boiler equipment as a gas network, but we can see if we can find out the information.

Chair: The fact that burning methane produces NOx is not a matter of contention, is it?

Dr Needle: I did not say that.

Charles McAllister: We will be using methane out to 2050 and beyond. That is not a matter of debate. Every single costed scenario shows that we will. It is a case of volume of demand. It is not a case of whether there is demand; it is a question of where it is coming from. The choice is whether you produce more greener gas here, or import it. That is it.

On the greenness of biomethane, you will not get the Green Gas Support Scheme subsidy unless you can prove your biomethane is at least 70% less carbon-intensive than natural gas. At least. Most of the plants are much further—

Chair: Talk us through it so we have the evidence for the Committee. What is it about biomethane that makes it greener than imports of LNG or taking gas from the North Sea?

Charles McAllister: I have a very good example. Denmark is one of the largest regional per capita producers of biomethane. They have a big pig industry. Danish bacon is not only delicious, but it means 40% of the Danish grid is biomethane, which is incredible. If that waste was left to rot it would release methane into the atmosphere, a very potent greenhouse gas. They collect that, it is cleaned up or upgraded and injected into the gas network, then the remaining digestate—renewable fertiliser—is used on farms in Denmark. It is the exact same model in the UK.

The Green Gas Support Scheme will not give you a penny unless you can prove it is at least 70%. Most of the plants are more than that. The big benefit for biomethane was the subject of a review about GGRs by Alan Whitehead before he was biomethane Minister. Today the bio-CO2 is typically used to make bubbles in drinks such as Irn-Bru. If you use that, combined with carbon capture and storage, then biomethane can be net negative emissions, so a greenhouse gas removal as well.

Dr Needle: Can I add a simpler answer to the question—sorry—which is biomethane makes methane from what you call short cycle carbon emissions. Plants grow, it goes into a digester, it makes methane. Long cycle—which is your fossil fuel—is the methane that has been trapped underground for millions of years. When you burn that, it is additive. When you burn biomethane it is not additive of CO2 into the atmosphere, whereas natural gas is. Hopefully that helps.

Chair: I will come back to that.

Catherine Fookes: Dr Needle, given that in your bio you talk about hydrogen projects, do you think hydrogen can play a role in substituting for gas in the UK?

Dr Needle: Sure. There is a long road on hydrogen. We do know that hydrogen can be used for industry and power generation. It can be manufactured in the UK. You do have to extract hydrogen from either water or natural gas for it to be useful, so there is a cost to making hydrogen. Can you distribute it and use it for industry power generation? That is proven to be true.

What we know is that NESO’s holistic pathway to net zero has around 100 TWh of hydrogen. The CCC also supports hydrogen for power generation and industry in its carbon budgets. It is not whether I feel we can do it or not; we cannot get to net zero without having some hydrogen in the energy system. That is simply because you still need molecules and gas in the 2050 energy system. You will still have some natural gas, hydrogen and biomethane, and that is because gas supports the electricity system.

In the 2050 net zero system, gas plays a supporting role. It supports the electricity system in back-up, it supports back-up supplies of power generation, district heating, and datacentres that need resilience. There is a big role for gas in 2050 in your net zero world. Some of that will be supported by hydrogen, simply because you can store it for long periods of time. It is a resilience support system.

We absolutely should be still supporting hydrogen. We were quite disappointed—but it is understandable—that the Government have chosen to move the budget that was there to support the first hydrogen transport business model. We are having to pick up the pieces on whether the projects are viable and we are trying to work out what replaces that funding stream. As it currently stands, the continued development of hydrogen in the UK may not get to scale simply because things are being pushed.

Catherine Fookes: Do you think we may end up having to import hydrogen from Saudi Arabia or other countries again? Why are we not taking up that opportunity?

Dr Needle: It is more likely that we will end up buying more natural gas at this point in time. There are lots of different ways of making hydrogen but we have significant amounts of curtailed wind in Scotland. Unfortunately, the past hydrogen strategy is a year and a half old—we have not seen the new hydrogen strategy yet and we are still waiting for it—but some guidance that sets out where hydrogen will be made at lowest cost would be helpful. There are ways of making hydrogen at low cost in the UK.

Catherine Fookes: Using that excess wind?

Dr Needle: Excess wind, and then you can sleeve it into the gas network or you can have repurposed or new pipelines to distribute it. I do think we need to look at it again.

Catherine Fookes: Thank you, that is helpful. We cannot use the existing gas pipes to deliver hydrogen and use hydrogen for heating homes?

Dr Needle: You could do that if you wanted to. The cost of hydrogen right now is quite high, so it would be quite expensive to do so. It has already been proven that you can burn hydrogen in boilers—you can make a boiler; we have seen them and tested that historically. The existing gas network can carry 100% work, and it can carry a 20% blend. It cannot do both things: it cannot carry natural gas and hydrogen. It has to be one or the other.

Catherine Fookes: Angela, to what extent does the national gas network rely on continued use of gases for heating buildings? Could industry and those who use gases other than for heat sustain the gas network as it currently exists?

Dr Needle: Let me explain more about what the gas system does, because it adds helpful context.
