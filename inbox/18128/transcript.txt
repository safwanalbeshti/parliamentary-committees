Examination of witness

Dominic Cummings.

Q50 The Chair: Good morning, Dom, and welcome to this meeting of the House of Lords Constitution Committee. Today, we are holding the fifth evidence session of the committee’s inquiry into the UK’s constitutional safeguards. We are delighted to be joined by Dominic Cummings, former chief adviser to Prime Minister Boris Johnson. Mr Cummings, welcome to the committee. You are very welcome. I remind all members of the committee to declare any interests relevant to this inquiry or today’s meeting the first time that they speak. Just to confirm matters, I have no relevant interests of my own. That is the introduction.

Dominic, as I said earlier, thank you very much for coming along. We are looking at the balance of various powers in the constitution and between different aspects of it, such as No. 10 and the Palace. Of course, we are very much believers in the sovereignty of Parliament. You have over a long period of time been a very vocal critic of various arrangements. You previously argued that the British state, including the Whitehall system, is dysfunctional. What are the most significant weaknesses in how the UK is governed? How do you think these should be addressed? Is this committee hearing at least one way of getting the arguments out into the open?

Dominic Cummings: That is a very broad opening question. I start with the core arguments that I would make. No. 1: many of these problems are obviously not unique to Britain. We saw during Covid, and we can see in Ukraine, many of the same pathologies across pretty much all western countries. Lots of these things are common. The first thing is that over a period of decades you have had a very marked shift in elite talent, where it goes and how it spends its time. In the old days, a large fraction of elite talent went into public life, politics, the military, intelligence services, various kinds of public services and so on. For all kinds of complicated reasons, post 1945, that has changed a lot. Now, very little elite talent goes into public life, and a huge amount goes into some kind of mix of maths, computing, hedge funds, private equity, venture capital, technology, lots of private research efforts and so on. When you look at previous crises in Britain, war efforts and so on, and you think of certain famous people and ask where their equivalents are today, most of them are not in public service. We saw that very clearly during Covid. That is one big thing.

A second big thing is that the old British constitution that you can see pre the 1850s and very clearly in the 1790s, the life of Pitt and the whole period of the French Revolution and whatnot—you can see it in letters from Palmerston to Queen Victoria in the 1830s and 1840s—was obviously based centrally on the principle of ministerial responsibility to Parliament. Responsibility was real. You can read many letters from people like Palmerston explaining it. The principle was that the Minister answering questions about a department in Parliament actually understood and was truly responsible for what happened in that department.

That obviously has become a complete fake charade, for many complicated reasons. The shift of the Northcote-Trevelyan system was a huge part of it. Essentially, in that system we substituted the old British approach for a Chinese meritocracy approach of basing everything on exams like the old Mandarin system. That began a process of transforming British institutions and the constitution, and you can see already its effects in World War I and in World War II. You can read memoirs from people such as Alanbrooke and RV Jones in World War II, and you can see already the signs there of many things that would come to haunt us during Covid and now over Ukraine. That fundamental collapse of ministerial responsibility is central.

The third thing to point out is that, if you go back and look at the history of the Cabinet Office, it was created out of the terrible crisis of the trench warfare in World War I and worries in this building that we were near collapse. Out of that, the Cabinet Office was created to try to change how the centre of power operated. If you look at the history of it, absolutely central to that history was the idea that of course the Cabinet Office must not become the policy-making entity, and of course, even more important, the Cabinet Office must in no sense take over responsibilities for personnel and appointments. That was agreed. There were many arguments about the creation of the Cabinet Office, including in this place, but everyone agreed at the time that it would obviously be a disaster if the Cabinet Office had anything to do with policy and appointments. Fast forward a century, and who is in control of all policy and appointments? The Cabinet Office. You have had here former Cabinet Secretaries say on the record that they regard the Prime Minister’s Office as an accounting unit of the Cabinet Office, and I think that reflects the general view inside the Cabinet Office. Certainly, the Cabinet Office does not in any way see its job as to execute the views of the Prime Minister—obviously.

I would start with those three big basics. If you look at anything that really works well in the world, you see great talent, responsibility inside institutions and speed of execution. The way in which the British system has evolved means that all three of those things now are more or less unlawful in the current situation.

The Chair: It is about the quality of people, the responsibility that is put upon them and their inability to cut through the bureaucracy, if I can paraphrase what you have said.

Dominic Cummings: Sort of, yes. It is a complex system. There are lots of arguments saying, “Oh, it’s the people”, or, “No, it’s not the people. It’s the institutions”. You have these arguments, but they are not really fruitful because you have to look at the overall thing and how they interact. If you have duff people, the institutions are going to be duff. If you have duff institutions and duff leadership, you cannot recruit great people. You see this constantly inside the Whitehall system. There is a constant demand of, “Why can’t we get these great people into the system?” It is because great people have options, and they are not going to sit in stupid meetings with PowerPoint after PowerPoint, and no one actually taking responsibility for anything.

Lots of great people came in to help the system in 2020 because it was the worst crisis since 1945. Literally every single one of those great people who came in in 2020 was either pushed out or left in disgust. Now you have this building running around going, “Oh my God, what do you do about AI?” The Cabinet Office literally pushed out people who came in in 2020 to help the British state deal with AI, who are now running some of the most important programmes in the world in companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic. It is a pathological response of the system to push out such people. It is a feedback mechanism. You cannot expect those people to come in if they are going to be sitting around having normal meetings.

The Chair: Right at the start, when you talked about talent, if I can put it very bluntly, you said it is about pay and conditions, whereas in the outside world, particularly in the City and some of the tech companies, people are very highly rewarded for what they do and it is impossible for the Civil Service to compete.

Dominic Cummings: Money is actually less important. When I was working in No. 10, there were some amazingly good officials, but the amazingly good officials were almost all young. Now there is an inverse talent ratchet whereby the system sucks in very idealistic and able young people in their 20s, but they gradually become disillusioned, and then by the time they get to 40-ish they look above them and they look at their management and they say, “I don’t want to do this”. The most entrepreneurial and punchy people with the most options leave, and the worst kind of HR people get promoted. Then that filters up and up until you have a situation like 2020 and Covid, where you have a bunch of Permanent Secretaries who are essentially nightmare HR hires.

Q51 Lord Cryer: Based on what you just said, would you say that the British constitution is under serious threat? Sorry, can you hear?

Dominic Cummings: Yes. My ears do not work so well. I am cupping my ear. Do not worry.

Lord Cryer: All right. Would you say the British constitution is under serious threat? If so, what are those threats?

Dominic Cummings: Yes, it is under threat. It is under threat because there is a general collapse of trust in the competence and integrity of institutions, and that obviously includes the political parties, the media, Parliament and the Civil Service. That, as I said at the start, is a general problem across the West, including here. A fundamental aspect of this is that, because of the internet, people can see many of the lies that are told. People can also see a fundamental lack of responsibility for things. This is not an idiosyncratic view of mine. If you listen to Rahm Emanuel, who was Obama’s chief of staff in the White House and a very establishment Democrat-type character, and his analysis of why America is in the situation that it is in, he will say that we have had Iraq, Afghanistan, the 2008 financial crisis, Covid, and disaster after disaster, and a perception among voters that the old system lies about it all and refuses to ever take any responsibility for it or to change. The response always seems to be doubling down on, “Well, you should trust us more and give us more money and power”.

Yes, the British constitution is under threat. Yes, there are foreign adversaries and enemies who hate us and are trying to cause problems. But by far our biggest problem is here. Putin is a tiny problem relative to our own Ministry of Defence and the lawyers in the Cabinet Office. It is not Putin who is launching lawfare against the SAS. It is not Putin who has destroyed procurement inside the MoD in a nightmare of corruption and hundreds of billions of pounds wasted. It is not Putin who is stopping the MoD learning from drones in Ukraine. It is the civil servants in the MoD who tell the people who come back from Ukraine, “This is what’s actually happening. Don’t tell anybody senior what you see in Ukraine about drones because that will jeopardise all our budgets”. It is not Putin and China doing these things to us. Our main problems are what we do to ourselves.

Q52 Baroness Laing of Elderslie: It is very interesting to hear what you say about ministerial responsibility being a “fake charade”. That is the kind of phrase that rightly shakes up thinking on this aspect of government. Would you consider perhaps that there might be too many arm’s-length institutions, quangos and other organisations checking the prerogative or statutory power of Ministers, so that if a Minister is trying to get something done there are so many hurdles put in the way by quangos and arm’s-length bodies?

Dominic Cummings: Yes, that is obviously a problem. It really accelerated in the 1980s with the Thatcher Government. A lot of people in the Tory party then thought that if we created all these independent agencies they would operate a bit like businesses with CEOs and whatnot. Of course, that was a completely false analogy to how the world works. All they really do is create things where responsibility is extremely opaque. Certainly, going back to my very first point, it undermined the old British constitutional principle of ministerial responsibility. Yes, that is a big problem.

There is an expression in Moscow: everyone is right and everyone is unhappy. It means everyone kind of has a point. The Civil Service response to what you said is—and civil servants have said this to me directly—”But look at the quality of the MPs. The MPs are so rubbish. They don’t want responsibility. They don’t want to be in charge. Imagine, Dominic, if we put so and so, Minister X, in charge of this. Yes, it’s a shambles now, but imagine if so and so was in charge; it would be even worse. They don’t want to be responsible”. That, I am afraid, is also very true. It is hard even to think of a Tory Minister whom I dealt with who actually wanted to be responsible for what was going on in the department. That is also an important thing. It is true that the Civil Service system has gradually disempowered Ministers, correct, but it is also true that the Ministers and the MPs themselves have become infantilised and have actually been largely happy with this process.

Baroness Laing of Elderslie: Would government work better and would the constitution be less threatened if there were fewer outside bodies wielding power?

Dominic Cummings: Yes, but I would go back to the pre-Northcote-Trevelyan system and have Ministers accountable to Parliament. That is the principle under which the old British constitution worked, and worked phenomenally well. It helped Britain lead the Industrial Revolution. It helped us beat Napoleon. Why can we not have that good old system rather than the nightmare bureaucracy that we have today, which is failing on all fronts?

Q53 Lord Griffiths of Burry Port: Thanks very much. It is fascinating to hear you. My background is not in politics but in street-level activities of various kinds. A lot of the things that we demonstrated about, wrote petitions about and organised community responses about, we always felt, were never being picked up anywhere and dealt with, but we went on doing that. It was strange for me, given some of the things that we remember you being reported for, that you are saying some of these things and identifying them at the very heart of government. I found myself very fascinated by that—how someone could get in there when we got as far as the door of No. 10 in order to present a petition, and that was as far as we got. I am in politics now, and I am conscious of the tensions and pressures that compete with each other within the political machine. You have properly pointed us towards good practice elsewhere and in history. You pointed to some key principles—ministerial responsibility and so on.

From the life that you have lived—it was a very public life after all—and the responsibilities that you have handled, have you found within the machine good practice that you can bring to the attention of others who were perhaps less well served? Was there part of a dialectic that you were placed to deal with, to present and suggest that we could learn from internal ways of acting as well as from external ones, either temporal or spatial? I do not know if I am making myself clear on this point. You seem rather well placed to have your views listened to, and I wondered if you could see centres of excellence and use those as part of the dialectic that you were responsible for.

Dominic Cummings: First, as I referred to earlier, private offices are actually remarkably good and remarkably understudied in general. Again, it is connected to the youth. Generally, they are much younger. It is close to 100% that that will be the case—possibly not always but in principle they are more junior people and there is a lot more talent density there. That is a strength. The Prime Minister’s private office attracts a lot of great people, and that is a strength. But I would also point out that a lot of those great people now leave. It used to be a very important pipeline of young, talented people, talent-spotted and encouraged to go to the Prime Minister’s private office, and then from there fast-tracked through to leadership positions in the future. To quite a large extent, that pipeline now is broken, and that has feedback effects on the system. It also means now that what has been true for a very long time, with able officials trying to get into the Prime Minister’s Office, to some extent now no longer happens, and people have been trying to avoid it because it is seen as such a constant nightmare and shambles.

In No. 10, there were obviously some centres of excellence, but you tended to find that the centres of excellence were being attacked by the Cabinet Office and Treasury. The Treasury was generally trying to cut the funding of everything that worked well and opposed helping them out. The year 2020 was interesting in a few ways. It was such a big crisis that the system had to change because you were faced with mass death on a weekly basis. Even then, Whitehall had to look a bit differently at things. We were originally advised by the Cabinet Office to do vaccines in the same way as the EU and beg to be part of the EU vaccine system. We looked at the system. It was obviously a complete shitshow, and it was not going to build anything or do anything, so we came up with a different plan, which was basically to do vaccines in the spirit of great things that have happened in this country and in America in the past. That largely meant stripping out of the way Cabinet Office and Treasury officials, so you could actually hire people and make people responsible. It was closed down.

We built sewage monitoring in 2020, which was then copied by dozens of countries, maybe by 100 countries or something now. It was closed down by the Treasury in 2021. The Cabinet Office has tried to restart it three or four times, each time so incompetently that it has not been able to restart it. All over Africa, there are now sewage monitoring schemes copying what we did in 2020, but Britain does not have one because Whitehall closed it.

With rapid testing, similarly, we got ahead of the game secretly on that. We built ourselves into a great position, it was closed down, and then the Government literally forgot to order any rapid tests for winter 2021. You can repeat this tragicomic thing over and over again.

What is one of the few remaining centres of excellence left in Britain? The British Special Forces. Who is their biggest enemy today? China? No. Russia? No. Iran? No. It is the Cabinet Office and MoD lawyers.

Q54 Lord Bellamy: Mr Cummings, accepting, as many people would, that the British state does not work very well at the moment, you have highlighted a number of dilemmas that we have, including the fact that the people who used to go into the Civil Service or politics go elsewhere now into hedge funds and so forth. We clearly have a problem, but one purpose of this committee is to work out what we should start to do about a problem like that. You mentioned a moment ago the possibility of going back to the old 1840s system of ministerial responsibility in its purest sense, but that was a long time ago, and there are very different circumstances there. From memory, the then Foreign Office had a staff of about 40 or something of that kind. If we were going to start tackling these problems in baby steps one by one to reach some practical solutions, what would be the first steps that you would take in order to progressively improve the public wheel?

Dominic Cummings: There are two things. First, with respect, you say that was long ago, but the principles of how things work well or do not work well are eternal. It is not a question of their being out of date. You see the same principles in 500 BC, 1800 with Pitt, or today in SpaceX—you name it. It is not correct conceptually to say that some of these core things have become out of date. Of course, times change and technology changes and whatnot, but basic principles of whether an organisation has actual responsibility, whether it has great talent density and whether it can move quickly are completely eternal principles.

On your second point about specifics, in spring 2020 you had a meltdown of the system and quickly trying to get out of it, but in a morass, nightmarish way. In summer 2020, briefly, the Prime Minister was in the mode of, “The system has nearly killed me. The system has already killed tens of thousands of people. Every part of the British state seems to be basically failing. We have to do things differently”. We had a series of meetings with the Cabinet Secretary, and I got, then, agreement from both the Cabinet Secretary and the Prime Minister to make some changes. You might call them baby steps but I think they were bigger than that. No. 1 was that Civil Service appointments should shift from closed by default to open by default. That sounds like a small thing, but it is actually huge. Going back to the whole point about the Northcote-Trevelyan system, since then, for 150 years, you have had a system that has morphed slowly into a closed-caste system, in which Buggins promotes Buggins internally, and that is why you have completely non-technical people put in charge of deeply technical programmes inside GCHQ or whatever. If you showed someone from 1810 this kind of thing, they would think that we had completely lost our minds.

You can change the default mode of the Civil Service from closed by default to open by default without tearing up the entire old Northcote-Trevelyan system and also without massive legal changes. I think you should tear up the old Northcote-Trevelyan system. You should do major constitutional changes by primary legislation. My point is that, because of the flexibility of how the system works, you can actually do things. The Prime Minister now as Minister for the Civil Service, working with the Cabinet Secretary, if they agree, can do very radical things with appointments that could have a dramatic effect today. Of course, as soon as I was out the door, all of that was immediately binned and nothing went ahead.

Secondly, again going back to what I said about the Cabinet Office and its historical evolution, the Cabinet Secretary now controls all the Permanent Secretary appointments. If you speak to the Cabinet Secretary and say, “How is the performance of these people managed? How are their priorities set?”, it is essentially a black box from the perspective of everybody who is elected. No Secretary of State has any input into it. The Prime Minister does not have any formal role. It is a whisky with the Cab Sec in the Cab Sec’s office and the Perm Sec at the end of the year, and then they dish out some bonus on the QT according to the ideas that they have themselves.

Another obvious thing to do is for the Prime Minister’s Office to say that the Prime Minister’s Office will play a central role in the performance management of the Permanent Secretaries, bearing in mind that the Prime Minister, working through the Cabinet Secretary, can actually replace these people, and bearing in mind that the Prime Minister is the only person who can replace them. Again, why did so much of the Covid chaos happen? Because, constitutionally now, everything is blocked other than through the Prime Minister’s Office. Only the Prime Minister’s Office can unblock certain things, particularly in a crisis. Things cascade into the Prime Minister’s Office, making the whole system even more mad, dysfunctional and compressed inside there, because people in No. 10 are having meeting after meeting to solve things that they should not be dealing with. It goes there because, now, all this constitutional authority is funnelled in a way that only the Prime Minister or someone speaking on behalf of the Prime Minister can actually get things done.

Tomorrow, if you had a Prime Minister who wanted to have a Government that controls the Government, you could say that we are changing how the appointments system works for officials. You could also sweep away a whole bunch of completely insane HR rules, which we started to do in 2020 but that was stopped. That was not idiosyncratic to me, I stress. Everyone in the system knows they are mad, such as the whole two-year promotion system whereby you have to constantly move jobs to get a promotion and pay rise. That is completely insane. No decent organisation in the world operates like that, but the Civil Service operates like that now. You could sweep away all of those. You could have the Prime Minister’s Office integrated with the actual core definition of priorities for the Perm Secs. “Do we agree, Perm Sec at the Home Office, that if the following things don’t happen you’ve failed and you should be replaced?” They are just the most basic conversations that would happen in any competent entity and do not happen in our system. If you had a Government that wanted to control the Government, you could do that. I go back to what I said before: Prime Ministers have not wanted to control the Government, and Ministers have been happy with the fake system.

Q55 Lord Burnett of Maldon: A lot of the answers that you are giving have really increased in my mind a question that I would like to know your views about, which is where real power lies in this country and in our political system. You have given some examples in the last few minutes. You said that Whitehall closes things down, which suggests that “Whitehall”, however one tries to characterise it, has a power that overrides political power. Your basic thesis about Ministers being accountable to Parliament as they used to be in the old days, if I can put it that way, was also a manifestation of political power being exercised by Ministers. You have spoken in the past about the Cabinet Secretary’s power being massively understated and the enormous power that the principal private secretary to the Prime Minister exercises. I wonder whether you might be able to bring these thoughts together to give us a short explanation of where you think power really lies, whether it rests in the right place and whether there are changes—you began just now to explain one or two of them—that could be made to put power back where you think it should rest.

Dominic Cummings: In 2020, I did not attend a single Cabinet meeting. It is normal for senior political people to attend Cabinet, but I did not go to any. The reason is that, other than at very odd, peculiar times now of political stress, essentially, Cabinet has become an irrelevance, and the actual important meetings that decide what is going on are 50 metres away in the Cabinet Office. If you look back to 2020 and Covid, the media were all reporting Cabinet meetings on blah, and rows between A and B on blah—all basically Potemkin theatre nonsense. The Covid task force was the thing that was actually deciding things, 50 metres away in the Cabinet Office, not the Cabinet.

It is certainly true that the Cabinet Secretary’s role is very much underrated. People do not understand how much power there is there. Also, people do not understand how that changed from, I am not sure exactly when but, say, roughly the Gus O’Donnell/Heywood era, after which another huge change was that a lot of aspects of how things like the intelligence services work, the co-ordination between the intelligence services, the Cabinet Office, No. 10 and MoD—a lot of that machinery— basically got transferred inside the National Security Secretariat in the Cabinet Office. That essentially became a black box outside any ministerial political oversight or involvement of any description. There are things like the NSC(O) meetings—“O” for officials—where all sorts of crucial things are decided. There are very obscure little Cabinet Office committees that decide things like how to task MI5 and MI6 with priorities. All these things essentially became completely separated from any ministerial role. The defence of the system is, “Well, they are in the Cabinet Office so they are sort of under the Prime Minister”. Of course, the Prime Minister does not attend any of those meetings. If the Prime Minister’s most senior political advisers ask to attend those meetings, they will be told, “Sorry, but PET”—the internal Gestapo for HR—”would not be happy with that sort of thing”. That is another aspect.

If a bomb goes off or if some Secretary of State—in a completely hypothetical situation, you understand—were to have a very unsuitable girlfriend, which may or may not have happened in the last few years, the first thing that happens is that people in the intelligence services go and speak to the Cabinet Secretary. They have a meeting and decide, “Okay, the bomb has gone off. What do we think about blah blah blah?” They decide then what information is given to the PM. The PM sits in a little room and is told a few things but is very much not at the centre of what is going on. A whole subterranean system operates between these other different power centres in Whitehall, almost entirely out of the view of any Minister or the Prime Minister. That is a huge change.

As an example of the thing you are talking about, if you go back to Brexit, people often ask questions like, “What did the Foreign Secretary or whoever say about this aspect of Brexit?” The answer is, “I can’t remember and it wasn’t important”. What was important was: what did the private secretary in the Prime Minister’s Office think about X? I dealt with them multiple times a day. They wrote the agenda for meetings. We would argue about what the action points were. That official has never been named and is not in the media, blah, blah, blah, but had 50 times more influence on what happened in the Brexit negotiations to 2019 than any Minister did, apart from the Prime Minister himself.

All these changes are very profound but, generally speaking, no one really wants to talk about them. The MPs are quite happy for people not to face the reality of this. The media are happy taking photos of everyone walking up Downing Street and pretending that the Cabinet is actually where things are decided. The officials are obviously very happy with it. The officials have a good point: “Okay, but, if you change this, do you really want Hancock making decisions?” That is a hard question to answer. Actually, it is not a hard question to answer: obviously, no. I would almost always prefer some official in the Cabinet Office to be in charge of it rather than Hancock, but you see my point. From a constitutional perspective, you see how the whole thing evolves.

Lord Burnett of Maldon: In your answer, you have not mentioned Parliament at all.

Dominic Cummings: You have a similar dynamic there. The attitude in the Cabinet Office is that the MPs are thick, do not understand anything, are not interested in it and just blabber, so the less they are told about anything important the better. If you look at the MPs themselves, look at the last few years. Look at Covid, Ukraine or the MoD—shambles. Do you see the Defence Select Committee actually forcing any discussion on any of these things? No. You had a nuclear sub nearly sink. You have a complete and utter shitshow throughout the entire infrastructure for the nuclear enterprise. Is there one serious attempt in Parliament to ask about that? No, there is not. As MPs say, “Well, if we ask them, we’ll just be told it’s all classified and, ‘We can’t talk to you’”. Again, it is a system problem. Multiple different bits of the system have evolved in a certain direction fundamentally such that nobody apart from the Prime Minister now can break certain kinds of gridlock. No individual Perm Sec can fix this problem. The Cab Sec can influence a lot of this, being the second most powerful person in the country, but cannot actually fix a lot of it. Only the PM and political leadership could break out of this kind of system malfunction.

Lord Burnett of Maldon: I thus infer that you believe that needs to happen.

Dominic Cummings: Yes, I do. Do you want to keep going on the trajectory that we are on?

Lord Burnett of Maldon: Sorry?

Dominic Cummings: Do you want to keep going on the trajectory that we are on now?

Lord Burnett of Maldon: Happily, I do not have to answer any questions. Thank you.

Q56 Baroness Hamwee: Going back a little bit, talking about the people who are recruited and who want to be part of government—

Dominic Cummings: Do you mean officials or MPs?

Baroness Hamwee: Younger people.

Dominic Cummings: Oh, you mean officials.

Baroness Hamwee: Yes. Do you have anything to say about corporate memory? I wonder whether you think that is important. Secondly, following on from the scenario that you described about a bomb going off and officials working out what the Prime Minister should be told and presumably should not be told or bothered with—I am not sure—is this, in your view, malign, incompetent or what?

Dominic Cummings: Yes, the corporate memory issue is real. It became very visible after Covid. I left, I think, in November 2020. Huge numbers of officials then left over the next few months and into 2021. By the time we got to 2023, I was getting texts from new officials inside No. 10 asking me what happened then and why was this decided, because there was this incredible churn internally of so many people leaving. There is no doubt that that is a huge problem.

Something that always stuck in my mind was a story from a long time ago, probably 20 years ago or longer, of the Foreign Office destroying its own library. That happened a lot around Whitehall. The Department for Education similarly basically cracked up its own library. A symptom of pathological institutions is doing things like destroying your own library. That was an early sign of where these things were going, and for sure that is a big problem now.

In terms of keeping things secret, I will give one very obvious example. I watched repeatedly, and experienced repeatedly, this process where terrorist incident X happens. There was a meeting with the Prime Minister in No. 10, explaining blah blah blah. Clearly, there’s a rabbit off so you ask questions. People look at the ground and do not answer. You have another meeting afterwards with the same officials, and you say, “You let a guy out of jail after MI5 warned that he would almost definitely go and blow up something again. Obviously, you kept the guy under surveillance, right, because you were warned by MI5? You kept him under surveillance in jail before you let him out so you could see who he was talking to”. Everyone’s head looks at the floor. “Oh, you didn’t keep him under surveillance. Aha! Is that because there’s Cabinet Office legal advice that says that, because of ECHR rights to privacy, you are not allowed to keep even convicted terrorists under surveillance inside jail?” “Yes, Dominic.” “Why didn’t you explain that to the Prime Minister? Why didn’t you explain to the Prime Minister that we let out a guy who we thought was going to do another terrorist act, and we did not even keep him under surveillance because the legal advice is that it’s unlawful under the Human Rights Act and the ECHR?”

That happens routinely. I would say for the majority of terrorist offences that you see there is an ECHR human rights angle. Close to 0% are ever explained publicly. It is never discussed publicly. Part of the reason for that is that officials do not want Ministers asking questions, and the Ministers do not want it raised either. We had Buckland in charge at the Ministry of Justice, and all the officials knew that Buckland did not want anyone raising difficult questions about that. Therefore, it is not raised. Dominic Cummings: You also have jobs that you cannot do anywhere else. A lot of people will forgo money in order to engage in extremely interesting problems that can only be dealt with in government. AI in government, again, is a very good example. I know because I have personally done it.
