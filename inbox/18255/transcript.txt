Chair: Welcome, everyone, to today’s second panel on the Treasury and climate and nature delivery. I am delighted to be joined by Ms Jill Rutter, senior fellow at the Institute for Government, and Professor Jacobs from the University of Sheffield. First, can you introduce yourselves and let us know in the first instance whether you think the Treasury’s emphasis on expenditure control and fiscal discipline creates barriers to investment in long-term climate resilience and nature recovery?

Jill Rutter: I am Jill Rutter. I am a senior fellow at the Institute for Government now, but in previous lives I was at the Treasury for quite a long time—and quite a long time ago—and then I was reincarnated as director of strategy and sustainable development at DEFRA, where I worked with Mr Gardiner as one of our excellent ministerial team back then.

Does the Treasury create barriers? Not in principle to that spending, any more than any other type of public expenditure. The Treasury obviously is in control and sets fiscal aggregates. It is then up to the Cabinet and, in particular, No. 10 and the Treasury to decide what the priorities are and how they want to regard that. While a lot of the emphasis is on the Treasury as an expenditure controlling Department, the Treasury actually has lots of other interests. It sponsors the financial services industry. It has a very big interest in growth, the supply side and regulation. That may flux a bit depending on who is Chancellor. It is also the sole proprietor—with HMRC as its delivery arm—of the tax system, which is also very important. I think when you are looking at the role the Treasury plays on this, you have to remember those other functions as well as just the spending side of the equation.

Chair: Just on that, we heard in the previous panel that the constant change of direction discourages long-term investment. There seemed to be a direction towards electric vehicles, then suddenly there is the electric vehicle pay per mile tax, other examples on energy generation and others. If you are an organisation thinking of investing, the fact that the policies of the Treasury seem to be constantly tinkered with has an impact on your interest in making that investment. Do you take that point at all?

Jill Rutter: That is very valid. I would say the Treasury is a civil service Department. It is the Chancellor and the Ministers who make those decisions. One of the interesting things is that this area, and this may be a reflection of the relative weakness of relevant Secretaries of State, has been very vulnerable to chopping and changing policy, whether it is on that, or whether it is on investment in carbon capture and storage. When Rishi Sunak was Chancellor, he introduced a thing called the green homes grant as a covid employment measure and turned it off incredibly quickly.

One of the things that I think the Treasury institutionally fails to do when thinking about these projects is it does not seem to understand its market-shaping role—that it takes time to gear up to have enough accredited suppliers to do that necessary retrofit under something like the green homes grant. Then you find if you do that for a limited period and switch it off suddenly, the people who did invest in upskilling their workforce to undertake those projects and take advantage of the grants available suddenly discover that that was a wasted investment because that tap has turned off.

There may be other areas of policy that are as vulnerable to that chopping and changing, but I certainly think that that is a fair commentary on the last 15 or 20 years of policy in this area—that it has too often been subject to an on and off switch without regard for the need for long-term consistency.

Chair: Professor Jacobs, I invite you to respond to the same question about whether the Treasury’s emphasis on expenditure control and fiscal discipline discourages long-term investment that might support the climate transition.

Professor Jacobs: Good afternoon, everybody. I am very glad to be here. Thank you for inviting me. My name is Michael Jacobs. I am emeritus professor of political economy at the University of Sheffield and visiting professor in the department of economics and global school of sustainability at LSE. Probably more relevantly for your purposes, I was an adviser to Gordon Brown in the glory days of the last Labour Government, first at the Treasury where I was a member of the council of economic advisers, and then at No. 10 when he was Prime Minister, so first from 2004 to 2007 and then 2007 to 2010 when we were rudely ejected by the electorate. I was mainly focused on environment, climate and energy.

I think the term “Treasury” covers a lot of different things. I am sure you have covered this before. You have certainly covered it in previous inquiries of the Environmental Audit Committee to which I was also invited. Treasury can be the officials, the Chancellor and Ministers, and the process. Jill is absolutely right. As I am sure you will know, in the end it is the Chancellor who makes the decisions. Of course, the Chancellor is advised by Treasury and Treasury as an institution certainly has some cultural biases and so on. But having worked for Gordon Brown, I can tell you that a determined Chancellor can command what his or her officials do.

The process point is the one I want to come to here. The Treasury has a process that is completely unique, which is called the Budget. We still have only one of those a year, although don’t be surprised if there is another fiscal statement made. The Budget is completely unlike any other process because the Chancellor can make decisions in a Budget, which the Treasury would never allow any other Department to make, with no scrutiny and no legislative scrutiny. It is not just no scrutiny of the Chancellor and what he or she is doing, but no legislative scrutiny. It goes into a finance Bill, and the finance Bill goes through without any of the scrutiny that an environmental Bill put forward by DEFRA or an energy or climate change Bill put forward by DESNZ would have.

In a Budget, there is now an incentive—which of course does not come from my old boss at all—to fiddle. We had a lot of measures in the Budget passed a week or so ago—98, I think there were. Gordon Brown was known sometimes to pass lots of little measures, and those measures do not have any scrutiny, and they can be extremely disruptive to markets in the real world, business investment and so on. This is a problem, and I do not think it has had enough attention.

It is not about the environment; this is true in any field. The Chancellor can just muck around with the tax system and indeed subsidies, grants, and other bits of policymaking, which he or she and certainly their civil servants would never allow any other Department to do.

Chair: To what extent do you think that the Treasury’s challenge function helps or hinders Departments in developing and delivering climate and nature policies, Professor Jacobs, particularly where these involve significant up-front expenditure or benefits that will only emerge over the longer term?

Professor Jacobs: My information as to actual practice is out of date. I have not been at the Treasury since 2007. You will have heard, I hope, from people with more recent experience, but my general view of this is that there are two major problems. One is that the Treasury is very powerful—it literally has veto power on almost all expenditure decisions—but it is the assessor, not the proposer, of the policies in every area except those of fiscal policy and some growth policy. The Treasury’s job is to say yes or no, and quite often no. It is quite often “no” in a structural sense because there is always too much expenditure demand from Departments, so it has to say no to some things.

I was always very frustrated that some of the brightest civil servants that I encountered in Whitehall were in the Treasury saying no to things rather than in the other Departments proposing things. I think that is a structural problem, and there is still a cachet in being a Treasury civil servant, and it is somewhat true. I suppose it is still true, and other Departments might question this, whether the brightest graduates in the fast stream go into the Treasury.

As far as that is still possible, or even if they are not the brightest, but they are just very bright, it always frustrated me that all they did with the policy was to say yea or nay. That is a structural problem, because in the end, good policymaking is innovative and creative, and has to find ways of overcoming problems, satisfying different stakeholders and so on. I would like to see the brightest civil servants trying to do those things.

There is another way in which this is generally true, which is if you have even three-year spending settlements, everything has to work within the three years. Clearly, a lot of environmental policy is designed to work over much longer periods, both on the investment side, because it is aimed at trying to get investment, but also simply on the preventative side, on adaptation and so on. Things do not have three-year paybacks, and the Treasury system can pull things back to what can be done in three years, what can be proved in three years, the Green Book and so on. Yes, I do think that there is a bias. I do not think it is specific to the environment. I think this is true in social policy and various other areas as well.

Jill Rutter: There are some things that have improved. We have multi‑year spending rounds, which was an initiative under Gordon Brown, whereas previously we had essentially one-year spending rounds, so that gives a bit more certainty. This Government have extended the period for capital to five years, which is better than three years. The Government have recently reduced the discount rate, which will benefit projects with longer‑term benefits in response to—

Professor Jacobs: It is slightly delayed, given that the Stern review proposed this in 2006.

Jill Rutter: Yes, but the Treasury never regarded the Stern review particularly as being relevant to it, as opposed to the rest of the world, which I think was an issue with the Stern review, or at least a Treasury official told me that at the time, that “Stern was for foreigners”, when I suggested it might be influencing the next Budget—that was when I was at DEFRA, rather than at the Treasury. I do not think it is a challenge function.

We have written extensively at the Institute for Government about the oddities of the Budget process in a report we put out in 2017 called “Better Budgets”. It is very weird and very different from normal policymaking processes. One of the big frustrations, if you are in a Department like DEFRA, is that you will find policies in your area being developed by the Treasury without reference to what you are already doing.

Then, because the Treasury can almost pass you in the fast lane through the Budget process, you will discover that your painstaking work with stakeholders to build some consensus on something, which you think is your policy area, has been overtaken by the Treasury trying to do its own scheme in a similar area so that the Chancellor can announce it rather than your Secretary of State, or that some initiative you have been working on has been cut off by a commitment that the Treasury has made.

I remember we were working up something at great length on corporate sustainability reporting, which we had done quite a lot of work on, only to discover that a very senior Treasury official had persuaded the Chancellor that this should be dropped as part of a deregulation initiative after a lunch with the CBI. The Treasury can throw its weight around, and you can very often in another Department feel very much at the receiving end.

What you do not get enough of—in my time, I think it has probably improved to an extent now—is genuine co-production of policies in these areas between the Treasury and the relevant Department, particularly if it is something that is going to be in the Budget. We saw stories this year that John Healy was imposing more siloisation within the Treasury itself, more compartmentalisation.

The Treasury is incredibly protective of anything that the Chancellor is going to announce in a Budget. As a non-Treasury Department, you only ever get to see a little slice of that, if you get that. It may be when it is already in the documentation and too late to say, “We think there might be a slightly better way of doing that.” I think that is a bit frustrating, but I do not think it is a challenge function, per se, that is the problem. I think the problems lie elsewhere.

Barry Gardiner: We often say that prevention is better than cure, but the New Economics Foundation argues that the five-year spending review test to comply with the fiscal rules actually works against investment that would avoid longer-term damage both to the environment and ultimately to the economy. How should Ministers tackle that problem?

Professor Jacobs: Let me answer this one first. There is a very specific technical reason things that might prove valuable after more than five years are not properly assessed. There are quite a number of reasons, but I want to focus on one, because I have literally just written about this in a piece in The New Statesman, which is that the OBR has an economic model that literally assumes away any multiplier or long-term effect beyond five years, so there are none in the way the OBR models the economic effects of policy, which is a problem on the growth side—less on the social and economic benefits side, because the OBR does not measure those things.

On the purely growth effects, if, for example, you have an investment that is mainly going to pay off genuinely in financial or growth terms after more than five years or in generating more growth, or it is going to save you money and have a growth or fiscal impact after more than five years, it does not count, basically. This definitely militates against doing it, because on the fiscal rules side, in compliance with the fiscal rules, the way the OBR is going to judge your policy is important, and the OBR scores every policy.

As well as the Treasury’s fiscal scoring, the OBR has to do a growth scoring of policies. First, it literally does not take anything beyond five years, but even within the five years, it is a rather sceptical body. Technically, its multiplier effects are smaller than many other people’s estimates. It has a strong crowding-out hypothesis, so public investment tends to crowd out private investment. It assumes that the economy is nearly at full employment and the output gap is small. There is a very technical thing about the OBR model in all of this, which I think is worth the Committee—it is not just environment, but it clearly has an environmental impact—

Barry Gardiner: Professor Jacobs, very eloquently and with great erudition, you restated what I thought my question was.

Professor Jacobs: Then the answer is yes.

Barry Gardiner: My real question was: what should Ministers do? How should they tackle this problem? You clearly set out the problem, but what I want to know is, what should we be doing about it? How should we be changing the framework within which the OBR operates, if that is the way of doing this? Give us some recommendations that this Committee can make to Government.

Professor Jacobs: I would be very glad to. I think the OBR model needs reviewing. Some colleagues and I have literally just written to Professor Jonathan Haskel today suggesting that. If the Environmental Audit Committee also wants to suggest that it would probably count more than our letter. That is on the very technical side.

The more general answer—and this is the kind of an answer to many of your questions today, in so far as we have been generally briefed on what you are interested in—relates to the challenge function, which is that at the moment Departments propose and Treasury assesses. That is not a very good way of engaging the Treasury. The experience that both Jill and I had of the last Labour Government included the creation of the Office of Climate Change. If you are not aware of that, it is worth going back to. The Office of Climate Change was set up by David Miliband when he was DEFRA Secretary in 2006—

Barry Gardiner: It was 2007.

Professor Jacobs: Was it from 2007? It was a new office—

Barry Gardiner: The Act was in 2008, but—

Professor Jacobs: The Act, yes. It was mainly operating to that. I cannot remember exactly when it was set up.

Jill Rutter: It was 2007.

Professor Jacobs: I defer to Jill, whose memory is better than mine. It was cross-departmental and did some very good work at the Institute for Government about that experience. It had officials coming from all the Departments who had a dual line of accountability. They were still accountable to their line managers in their own Departments, but they were also trying to produce joint work, jointly accountable to the Secretary of State.

This was not put under the Cabinet Office; it was put under the person who had the official responsibility for climate change policy. It generated two things. One is quite a lot of innovation; we had quite a lot of new thinking. Secondly, we had more buy-in. It was much easier to get buy-in from the Treasury because it was involved in the process.

I would regard this as a good model in general for policymaking, not just in this field, which is that you bring the Treasury and all these bright civil servants into the process as part of the policymaking process. That also means that, when it goes to the Cabinet Committee, you do not have the Minister proposing and the Treasury Minister getting advice saying, “No, no, no.” That includes the other Departments too.

Some of you have been on Cabinet Committees, and they are a kind of disastrous process in which the Minister is the presenter and everybody else has their briefings from their officials, which say why they don’t like it. The fact that you are all members of the same political party has completely gone out of the window. More collaboration between Departments at official level, and I would also say at the ministerial level, is one way of overcoming that.

Jill Rutter: I agree. I think the OCC was a very good institutional innovation. David Miliband’s initial intention was that it should be a Cabinet Office unit. The then Cabinet Secretary did not want any more units in the Cabinet Office. That was why it ended up in DEFRA, with cross‑departmental sponsorship as far as people were up for that. It did work very well, I think.

I would say to do some more things. The OBR has quite a limited role. It is up to the Government. The Government set the fiscal rules. It is up to the Government how much weight they give to the OBR. Even if the OBR does not look beyond five years that is no reason the Government should not look beyond five years.

There is an interesting thing about filling in the gap between the OBR’s horizon that we see in accompanying Budget statements, which is the five-year forecast period, etc, and its 30-year fiscal risks report. It is an interesting question about should we not have a view, maybe a more operational view, of what is happening in that intermediate period?

There is also an interesting question about scope for collaboration, which may be helped by one big fiscal statement a year, if we can go back to that. I agree with Michael that next year might be an exception—hopefully just an exception—in getting more collaboration between the OBR and other people looking forward in the economy to ground some of that fiscal risks look.

If we did spending reviews with a 10-year-out projection, it would be quite interesting. You might then start to see some of the benefits of prevention spending paying off. One of the things that the Government should get credit for is the announcement last week that they are going to put a ringfence around prevention spending. What we do not know is whether that is going to be narrowly defined as preventative services as opposed to the normal day-to-day services, because we see them being raided regularly whenever there is a problem in acute services, particularly in the health service.

Alternatively, are the Government going to have a much more expansive definition that would apply to some of the things like building additional resilience, adaptation to climate change and so on? I do not think that is clear yet, but it may be a thing where the Committee would like to take a view.

Professor Jacobs: Can I add one more thing on the OBR while we are on the OBR? I realise we may be pre-empting some of your further questions. The OBR’s fiscal risks report, which looks out 50 years—

Jill Rutter: Thirty.

Professor Jacobs: Is it now 30?

Jill Rutter: Maybe it is 50. It is long term.

Professor Jacobs: I was literally just looking at it, and it does not talk about the environment. It has all the other risks in it, but not environmental risks. Now maybe there are none.

Jill Rutter: It talks about the climate.

Professor Jacobs: There is no heading in the executive summary of the one that was published this year on environment and climate. That might be another thing you want to ask them about.

Jill Rutter: It has had climate risk.

Professor Jacobs: It used to, but it does not have climate risk now.

Jill Rutter: Oh, right. That is interesting.

Professor Jacobs: Shorter answers. Apologies.

Barry Gardiner: The next issue is the Seventh Carbon Budget advice was published ahead of the spending review in February 2025. That gave detailed modelling of the investment needed for decarbonisation. It said that we needed £26 billion annually between 2025 and 2050, which would be substantially offset by savings. How much that investment is being delivered is one thing, but the question I want to ask you is: do we need a clear delivery pathway and financing for nature targets? It seems to me that the focus, such as it is, that we are able to put on climate targets is good. For nature, for the environment, it is pretty much non-existent. How should this Committee make recommendations to Government about that?

Professor Jacobs: I shall try to be shorter. The answer is yes. There is a big difference, legally, between environment targets under the Environment Act 2021 and the Climate Change Act 2008; the climate targets are legally binding, and the environmental ones are not. That makes a massive difference. There was a possibility that the Environment Act ones would have been legally binding, but the Secretary of State at the time, Michael Gove, unsurprisingly, said no. That difference in their status makes a big difference.

The Climate Change Act and its legally binding status is a proper driver of policy. If you are not on a reasonable trajectory, the High Court will tell you to go and do it again, and that has happened twice. It might happen again if some of the rumours in the newspapers are to be believed about the direction of policy. With the Environment Act, nobody can do that because they are not legally binding.

Yes, as far as they exist and those targets exist, I would like to see budgetary processes and investment assessments made against them. There is not much point in having those targets unless they act as some kind of constraint or driver—even better—of policymaking.

Barry Gardiner: Thank you. So we can take that—

Professor Jacobs: That is a yes, Barry. That is as short a yes as I can give.

Barry Gardiner: Lovely. The Edinburgh Climate Change Institute argued that climate investment can be disadvantaged where its benefits fall outside the budget of the Department responsible for funding it. Retrofit and transport programmes can generate household savings, improve health, and reduce pressures on public services. The payer does not always see the benefit. How do we get over that problem? Do we do that, by what you and Jill were talking about, in terms of getting together generic teams around problems rather than having the circulation around the Cabinet table with everybody fighting their own brief?

Jill Rutter: This is a problem that is not confined to this area. It is a general problem everywhere. Accounting officers are supposed to take account of benefits on programmes that are not their own. One of the real problems that we see is that if there is a strong central lead, that something is clearly a prime ministerial priority, and a Secretary of State is interested in it, then you see progress.

We saw a bit of that in Boris Johnson’s Government, when the Government produced its net zero strategy, which did have quite a lot of actions by individual Departments. Too often we have seen weak strategies that rely on DESNZ. I thought that was one of the problems, to an extent, with the mission that was originally defined for the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero by Keir Starmer and Ed Miliband. That was very focused on what DESNZ could do, which happens, and it was not used as a way of driving action in other Departments.

There are so many areas in Government where benefits—we see it at the moment particularly acutely in the Home Office and Ministry of Justice co‑management of the criminal justice system, where one has every incentive to up sentences and the other one has the problem that it cannot cope at the other end of it in terms of courts and particularly the prison estate. I think that is a big problem to solve, not confined to this area.

Barry Gardiner: What is your recommendation for solving it? How should this Committee—

Jill Rutter: This is where you need to have a proper cross-Government delivery plan for the targets. The Government sign up to a carbon budget, but there is only a point in signing up to that if you then have a cross‑Government delivery plan that will show what actions everybody is taking to deliver that.

Professor Jacobs: Can I give a more specific one, which is the spending review?

Barry Gardiner: I was just coming to that.

Professor Jacobs: Do you want to ask your spending review question? Under Gordon Brown, spending reviews had PSA, public service agreement, targets. There were 600 of them and some of them were ridiculous, but we gradually whittled them down. Many of them were cross‑departmental. The spending review got attached to a quasi-contractual arrangement—it was not always clear what the penalty for missing it was—between the Treasury and the relevant Departments.

Jill was reminding me that she was responsible for the whole of sustainable development. Then the PSA targets were abandoned after 2010; subsequent spending reviews did not have them. Jill Rutter: Yes, I was.

Professor Jacobs: They were responsible for delivering them, and that was a good way of doing that. Then you could do prevention and various other long-term things.

Chris Hinchliff: I want to ask a few questions about climate and nature as economic risks. My starting point for this is that it is clear from what you have said today that the Treasury is very good at orthodox economic thinking, orthodox economic assessments. It is that economic orthodoxy that has led us to the point where, very recently, we had the UN Environment Programme saying it is now inevitable we will exceed the 1.5 degree warming target. That brings with it huge potential economic costs, billions of pounds for maintaining infrastructure during extreme weather and flooding, and impacts on public health and productivity. Do you think the Treasury’s model of thinking is set up to actually deal with the reality of the costs that we are facing?

Jill Rutter: It is quite interesting. The Green Book requires any project lasting to 2040 to be assessed against a 2 degree climate risk, and any project going beyond 2040 to be assessed against the risk associated with 4 degrees of warming, at your top end. The interesting question with these things is, does it then change the decisions that people make?

One of the recommendations that we have made from the Institute for Government, particularly on adaptation to climate change, taking account of climate risks, is that the Treasury should very explicitly make this a responsibility of the accounting officer—effectively the permanent secretary—in every Department, to ensure that your spending, your programmes are resilient to climate risk.

You could add in other wider environmental risks and have a core value for money requirement. It is quite a good articulation of longer-run value for money; if you are spending money in a way that looks good value up front, but requires massively expensive retrofit down the line, then that is not good value for money. That would bring home and surface those risks better in Departments.

This summer and the repeated heat waves have raised consciousness quite a lot across Government of the risks particularly associated with heat in the summer. There is quite an interesting climate resilience assessment that has been produced by the Ministry of Justice where it talks about the fact that terrifying amounts of the prison estate are vulnerable to both flooding and to overheating. That obviously has enormous consequences in the cramped environments, with risks of violence and things like that. We have seen what is going on in France with schools and so on.

It is rising up, but I think an explicit way on resilience and adaptation of saying—the Treasury could be saying it, but why does the Treasury not wire it into the system in the same way as accounting officers being very aware that they should not overspend their cash limits or that they need to preserve the propriety of spending?

Chris Hinchliff: Professor Jacobs, do you think that the Treasury is culturally capable of understanding the scale of investment that needs to be mobilised to avert these problems?

Professor Jacobs: I think if it is told to, yes, I certainly do. In defence of the Treasury, the reason we are going to overshoot 1.5 is not entirely its fault. It is very helpful to have procedures, because there can be cultural biases in how people are educated and trained, particularly economists who, despite the Stern report, do have a discount rate in their head, and it is higher than most of us have. Discount rate is a major problem in anything to do with this because it basically devalues the future, both costs and benefits.

Yes, there are definitely biases, but the Treasury needs procedures. Value for money is exactly where it should be done. If you look at Treasury procedures—indeed, this is true of Government as a whole—there is value for money, which has very high priority, and then there are lots of other things that you should be doing, like qualities and various other things, which just get lower priority and are much easier to do as box‑ticking. It should be in the Green Book, and it should be in the value for money, and it should have accountable officers.

I think Jill is right. Basically, the problem of climate adaptation has been brought forward, which means that the discount rate is lower, and we need to do it now. That was not true until very recently.
