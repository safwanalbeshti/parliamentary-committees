Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Cathrine Brun, Dawn Chatty and Harmonie Toros.

Chair: Welcome to the Foreign Affairs Committee session on Syria. We will hear from two panels today: first from regional experts and secondly from representatives of the Governments of Lebanon and Turkey. Will the witnesses please introduce themselves?

Cathrine Brun: I am the deputy director of the Centre for Lebanese Studies, an independent research institute based between Beirut and the UK. I am also a fellow of the faculty of history at the University of Cambridge. I am a human geographer.

Dawn Chatty: I am emeritus professor of anthropology and forced migration at the University of Oxford and a former director of the Refugee Studies Centre. My most recent research has focused on displaced Syrians in Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan.

Chair: Spot on—exactly what we are looking at.

Harmonie Toros: I am a professor in politics and international relations at the University of Reading. I have been particularly following the question of British citizens in north-east Syria.

Chair: Have you given evidence before?

Harmonie Toros: I have, yes.

Q69 Chair: We are looking at the situation of Syrian refugees, particularly those living in countries around Syria. As experts who have been looking at this for some years, how do you feel the position has changed over the last couple of years, particularly since the fall of Assad?

Dawn Chatty: Perhaps I should say, as a preface, that Syria lost about half of its population of 23 million: 6 million across international borders, mainly to Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan, and another 6 million were displaced internally.

After 2024, I think there was a sense that everybody was going to rush back, but the country was, in its 12 or 13 years of armed conflict, really destroyed. The regime, in battling the opposition in the country, pretty much destroyed schools, health clinics, hospitals—these were all targeted—and neighbourhoods, particularly neighbourhoods of informal settlements, which I would say are the very poor elements of the country.

After December 2024, some well-placed elite rushed back to see what it was like, but those who were on the borders of Lebanon, Syria and Jordan were very cautious. They do want to return, but the key word is dignity: they want to return in dignity and in safety. Very few of them feel that is possible yet. I can comment more on that later, but that is an opening—unless you want me to say a little more.

Q70 Chair: That is great; thank you. You will have plenty of opportunities to develop your thoughts, but that is a good opener. Do you want to add anything, Cathrine?

Cathrine Brun: I think that is a very good description of the situation. I would be a little bit cautious, though, to say that everyone wants to return. After many years of displacement, I think the picture might be a bit more nuanced. It is important to understand how people have developed their lives in exile, even under very precarious and difficult conditions, particularly for young people who might have grown up in the host countries. That means that return might be a possibility in the future but, as Professor Chatty said, it might take quite a long time to prepare for it. It is important to keep that in mind when we think about the discussion on return.

Q71 Chair: People have been going back, haven’t they?

Cathrine Brun: Yes.

Chair: What are the drivers of that?

Cathrine Brun: What we know is that people who have gone back have had more access to housing and livelihoods. Many of those who have gone back have also reported many difficulties in going back. But those who are sitting on the fence, so to speak, many of whom would want to go back, do not have access to housing and livelihoods. Those are the key factors that are holding them back. That makes it very hard, if you are responsible for a family, to go back at this current time. Even though the situation where they are might not be a situation where they can develop their life a lot, it feels more predictable, even in conditions of war, like in Lebanon, than going back to a situation where you do not know whether you can find shelter or whether you will have a livelihood to support your family.

Q72 Chair: We saw some refugees in Lebanon, and I asked the women whether they would go home. They said that they wouldn’t until they felt secure and until there were schools. It really brings home to you just how bad the situation is in Syria, as Dr Chatty was saying, that even the most basics cannot really be guaranteed.

Cathrine Brun: Yes. Particularly for women, it seems that security is a big issue in return considerations. It is also about the freedom of women. People are worried about what their daughters will meet in Syria currently. Education is a more mixed picture—

Q73 Chair: We will ask some specific questions about education later. Dr Toros, can I ask you the same question?

Harmonie Toros: In terms of the situation in north-east Syria, the largest camp, al-Hawl, essentially collapsed in chaos in January and February. That had considerable repercussions for Syrians and for third-country nationals, including British citizens and British children. That has led to quite a change in terms of where people have been brought to. The remaining camp, al-Roj, still has British citizens in it.

We have an added concern now, in that when al-Hawl collapsed, and when the Panorama prison was opened, US and US-backed forces moved 5,700 men into prisons in Iraq. From a British perspective, it is possible that there may be British citizens and British children who have now been moved into prisons in Iraq.

Q74 Chair: Does anybody know? The Red Cross would always be the people one would go to; are they able to say how many Brits there are, or have been, in these camps?

Harmonie Toros: I get figures from various sources; very often they do not want to be quoted when they give me figures. There is clearly an issue here. I would love the British Government to inquire, because they have an embassy—we have an embassy in Iraq, right? We know which prisons they are in and we can go and ask whether there are British citizens there. Sentences have been taken on by the Iraqis. Finding out whether and how many British citizens have been moved to Iraq, and in what conditions they are being held, aside from those who are still in Syria and in the other camp, al-Roj, are all really important questions. I agree with you.

Q75 Chair: Could you give us even a guesstimate as to how many Brits may have been moved to Iraq?

Harmonie Toros: No, I can’t. Everyone I spoke to told me it was most likely that some British citizens were moved, but we are talking a dozen at most. We are not talking very big numbers.

Q76 Chair: How many Brits are left in al-Roj?

Harmonie Toros: There are at least 40, and most of them are children.

Chair: And women—mothers.

Harmonie Toros: Yes, it is only women and children—there are no men in al-Roj—and most of them are minors.

Q77 Sir John Whittingdale: You have talked about whether the refugees in neighbouring countries want to come home and what might be holding them back. What is the attitude of the Syrian Government? Are they putting out a message saying, “You are welcome. We will look after you. We would like you to return”?

Dawn Chatty: Let me put it the other way: there is no message trying to stop people returning. As you know, under the Assad regime there was a statement, “If you left, you are considered a traitor; you can’t come back,” particularly towards Palestinian refugees in Syria.

Chair: I did not know that. That’s extraordinary.

Dawn Chatty: Well, that was a fact. There are a number of Palestinian refugees from Syria who have gone back—middle-class people who were not living in Yarmouk but were part of the urban establishment.

The Government of Ahmed al-Sharaa, which is a transitional Government, has not encouraged everybody to rush back, and the reason for that is that he is really desperate to start the reconstruction and rebuilding of Syria, which is very difficult because sanctions have not been entirely lifted. Outside the sanctions, there are many, many Syrians in the diaspora—there have been since the 1980s—who would like to find ways of sending money to help to support their towns and villages, but the banking system still prevents money from being transferred to Syria. They have not really caught up with the reality of what is taking place.

I would like to make one more point here. Turkey has taken a very humane, instrumental approach to return. As in the Dayton accords, it has permitted Syrians, over a period of about six months, to do a look and see. Heads of household of Syrian families in Turkey have been allowed to return and see whether it’s okay. That window remained open only for about six months, whereas if a head of household in Jordan went back to see whether it was okay and whether their house still survived, they could not return to Jordan. That look and see, which was first established in the 1990s in the Dayton accords—article VII—should be encouraged by all the neighbouring states.

Q78 Edward Morello: I want to follow up on your previous point, Professor Chatty, on the state having an open-door policy—“Please return”—although not pushing it hard. To what extent is that a result of the fact that a significant influx of returning Syrian nationals would put additional pressure on the state? My understanding is that it is already struggling to provide essential services to vast swathes of the country, so a massive influx of individuals would add pressure to that.

Dawn Chatty: Absolutely. The urban infrastructure in Homs, Hama and Aleppo and on the outskirts of Damascus has been almost entirely destroyed. In many of the cities in the country, people have electricity for only four hours a day. There would be a terrific burden if everybody were to return, but obviously they are not going to. They want to return when it’s safe.

As Cathrine pointed out, many of these families have been out of Syria for 12 or 13 years. Their children have grown up abroad. Their education is really important, and schools have not yet been completely rebuilt. As of last week, there has been a huge debate as to how much the curriculum is going to be changed, besides dropping the Ba’athist propaganda. Right now there is huge opposition to the proposal to make the curriculum quite conservative, and those who have been educated abroad are looking at that really carefully before they have their families go back.

Q79 Abtisam Mohamed: I want to take us back to 2011, when there was a large-scale movement of Syrian refugees into nearby countries. I am keen to hear your views, Dawn, about the impact on those countries economically and in terms of cohesion.

Dawn Chatty: First, I should say that Turkey is the only country that has put into effect a domestic asylum law, in 2014, which meant it provided temporary protection to Syrians not as refugees but as displaced people.

Abtisam Mohamed: What is the difference?

Dawn Chatty: For Syrians it was a big difference. They did not want to be called refugees. It was a matter of dignity. It also meant that the UN Refugee Agency was not going to be responsible for them—it was the Turkish Government, under the AFAD programme. It provided them with healthcare and free education through to university, once the Syrian children learned Turkish well enough. In the end, I think about 200,000 Syrians took on Turkish citizenship, alongside their Syrian citizenship. These are people who are going to take a long time to make any decision about returning.

In terms of the impact on the economy, the population of Turkey is similar to the population of Germany, even though it took four times the number of Syrians that Germany did. I do not believe there was a terribly negative impact on the economy. Some could say that parts of the economy flourished. It is not the same for Lebanon, of course, because before the armed conflict you had half a million Syrians in Lebanon, working in agriculture and construction. The figure of a million probably includes a lot of their families. These were—I am going to say—the poorer Syrians. Middle-class and upper-class Syrians also went to Lebanon, but they invested and so on, so there was not that impact.

Several reports suggest that the impact of Syrians in Lebanon was not quite as severe as is often made out, because a lot of the landlords in the Beqaa valley made a lot of money from renting land for Syrians to put up their tents and so on. Obviously, Lebanon has suffered from many other things at the same time and it all compounded, besides the implosion of the banking system in 2020.

Chair: We met a woman whose brightest, her eldest daughter, went off to work for the landlord every day. She was doing that in part exchange for the rent on the land where they were able to put their tents.

Dawn Chatty: In Jordan, again, the figures vary, but some figures suggest that about 800,000 Syrians went to Jordan. In the first couple of years, about 300 Syrian factories moved to Jordan, so in the first couple of years there was a decrease in unemployment. But obviously over time the numbers had a significant impact on the infrastructure in Jordan. I have just come back from Jordan; access to water is a huge problem there, and the extra numbers make it worse.

In 2016, something called the Jordan compact was put into effect, which I am going to say was not very well thought out. It restricted work permits to people in construction and in agriculture, but many of the Syrians who fled into Syria were skilled workers or professionals, who found it very difficult to work.

Perhaps Lebanon has suffered the most, because you are talking about 20% to 25% of their population being from Syria. There are other issues in terms of the relations between countries, and discrimination. In Lebanon, obviously Hezbollah was very supportive of the regime in Syria, which created issues for those who were fleeing the regime. There was a lot more tension in Lebanon over the number of Syrians in the country.

Q80 Abtisam Mohamed: I would like to follow up on that and come to you, Cathrine, on cohesion and how Governments portrayed new communities coming into the country. Were the statements the Governments put out supportive, or were there different factors in play there?

Cathrine Brun: I will add to what Dawn said. We did a macroeconomic study of the impact of Syrian refugees on Lebanon, and similar studies have been done in Jordan and Turkey. At the macroeconomic level, it has not had a huge impact, but I think it is important to note that a lot of the impact is felt locally. Aid that has come into the countries has been used to keep up finances in some ways, but both the nature of aid, which we might want to discuss more later, and the fact that local communities have been overwhelmed by infrastructure and other things has had a big impact and also made tensions between groups.

What we know about refugee situations generally is that they often are protracted, but when the messaging from the beginning is, “This is going to be temporary. You are only here temporarily. You are a burden to the community,” that can also create tensions. Sometimes, with all the other crises that are going on simultaneously, both in Lebanon and also in Jordan in terms of economic developments and so on, it has been easy to blame the refugees.

It is important also to think at different levels, but I think the messaging has been very clear, particularly from Jordan and Lebanon, which I know better, about refugees being there temporarily. That has therefore impacted how you could think that refugees could have been part of developing the economy. Actually, in terms of economic development for example, of course, Lebanon lost huge export opportunities to Syria during the war, but then the purchasing power of Syrians in Lebanon kind of balanced some of that. The discourse was always a question of being a burden, and in the context of compounded crises, that has also been very important.

The discourse of return is now also very strong. I have an important example; if you have been to Lebanon, maybe you have heard about this. About 130,000 Syrian refugees have been internally displaced during the Israeli war on Lebanon. That means secondary displacement, in a way. Syrian refugees did not feel welcome in the shelters that were open for Lebanese displaced, and I think that says something about some of the tensions that are often felt even in an emergency.

Q81 Jessica Toale: Cathrine, you pre-empted my question a little bit by mentioning aid and overseas development assistance, but I particularly wanted to look at the impact of cuts from lots of donor countries on Syrian refugees and also the host countries. I wonder if you could start with that as a reflection.

Cathrine Brun: That is a very big question and a very important one; hopefully we might come back to this in the next session as well. There is a huge need for assistance in countries that take such a big burden of refugees. There is the question of burden sharing or responsibility sharing. What has happened over time is that aid going towards refugees is often labelled as humanitarian, which means it is short term and it goes in very short cycles, so it is more life support than thinking about development.

To some extent, you could say that some of the aid that has come in over these years has also led to what we often call an “NGO-isation” of civil society—where NGOs have become service providers more than maybe taking what we often think of as a civil society role and creating change and so on. With the different crises and so on that are going on, and with this kind of life support approach, when aid goes away, there is a huge gap, and that is felt very strongly at the moment. It has an impact on the civil society that has existed on that, but it also has a huge impact on access to education and health services, and it is currently also impacting food assistance. Partly, that is a problem we need to solve now, but it is partly that we need to look at how we are organising aid to create that dependency. Does that answer your question?

Q82 Jessica Toale: That is very helpful. I think we recognise that in a lot of humanitarian situations, there is often a gap between the short term and the longer term funding cycle.

Dawn, looking at UK aid specifically, is there a way that that can be organised more effectively? Could we be doing anything other than giving aid as a UK supporter in these crises?

Dawn Chatty: In terms of this particular series of events or crises—I am not sure which word to use—the UK could do a great deal more. It is not so much emergency assistance that is required in Lebanon and Jordan, and Turkey does not need this kind of assistance. It is so different from what we are seeing in Gaza and in the west bank.

They could focus a great deal on promoting and helping in the development of education and healthcare. This is probably a study that you are probably very familiar with—one of the big problems in Lebanon is that where UNHCR was working, they would have an MOU and be working very effectively, but they couldn’t cover the healthcare requirements. Refugees often had to find money or borrow money in order to get emergency care or cancer treatment and so on.

It is not so much the emergency. It is helping to develop a health and education system that can support displaced Syrians while they are in these countries. Also, I think the UK needs to focus on Syria itself. Education is the big issue. If a really sound curriculum is put into effect and there is a real development in the rebuilding of schools, people are going to be more interested in returning, because that then allows them some sort of dignity.

It is not really emergency aid that the UK needs to look at in this context.

Q83 Jessica Toale: My understanding is that the majority of refugees are in urban settings in these countries. Is there a difference in the type of support that is needed between the urban and more rural setting?

Dawn Chatty: In Lebanon, many are rural. The Beqaa valley is holding most of the Syrians. There is then of course a huge amount of support, because of local associations or non-governmental organisations in the region that have supported Syrians, mainly through helping them to access education.

In Jordan, other than the Zaatari and Azraq refugee camps that still exist, most Syrian refugees are in urban contexts. Turkey closed down all of its camps a few years ago and has really promoted the self-settlement of Syrians along that southern border.

There is a distinction. In some cases, it is urban. Certainly, for assistance to Lebanon, I think it is mainly rural.

Q84 Alex Ballinger: You mentioned the Jordan compact, Professor Chatty. If I remember it, there was also an element that allowed refugees to work in the garment sector, in manufacturing, although the numbers that did that may be debatable.

I am interested in a bit more detail on why you think that was not a successful model. Can you maybe compare that to the larger EU programmes in Turkey? I am not sure whether you were going to say that was more successful, but could you do a comparison of the types of support and how that worked well or did not work well?

Dawn Chatty: The Jordan compact in particular was very focused on a report that was put forward by Alex Betts and Paul Collier. Paul Collier is an economist who knows Africa very well. Alex is a colleague of mine, but I have to say does not know the middle east, and didn’t know the middle east.

The compact was going to fail right away—it was recognised by many Jordanians, but there were many other elements to the compact, in terms of the money that would be provided to the Jordanian Government if they went through. It was meant to provide work permits for Syrians to work legally in Jordan, but only in agriculture, construction and in free zones, where they had these factories.

There was kind of an assumption that Syrian women would leave their families all day and go and sit in a factory—Syrian women are not going to do that; it is not going to happen. And it did not happen.

Even in terms of the sign-up for legal work permits, it was very limited. I have forgotten the numbers, but they were aiming at 200,000 or 300,000 and for the longest time, I think it remained at around 50,000. That was for reasons that differed from the same kind of effort that was made in Turkey—where a lot of people hiring Syrians did not want formal work permits because then it meant paying higher taxes and so on.

The Jordan compact came out of some academic research that was not contextualised to Jordan, which is always a big issue. There was a rush to do something in 2015, 2016, and so you had a project that did not really work in the way it was supposed to work.

Q85 Alex Ballinger: Across Europe, including in the UK, the big debate after the civil war was about the number of Syrian refugees who made the trip beyond the bordering countries to countries such as the UK. A lot was made of the support given to Turkey, in particular, to prevent that and help refugees stay close to Syria and get the support they needed in the neighbouring countries. Was that an effective policy? Was there a link between the ODA—the international aid—that was provided to countries like Turkey and the migration that came across into Europe?

Dawn Chatty: Definitely there was. In 2015, 2016, there was the EU-Turkish compact—whatever you want to call it—which slowed down the onward movement, but that onward movement could not have been totally prevented, even if you doubled the amount given to Turkey. At that time, Turkey had a number of very good refugee camps near the border, but a lot of people were self-settled.

You have to remember what was happening in Syria at the time. At that point, ISIS—the Islamic State—had taken over about 80% of the country, and Russia had come in to support the regime and was really upping the aerial bombardments in the urban centres. Many families, even those in Turkey, thought, “We are not going to be able to go back any time soon,” and they decided to move on. About 800,000 were welcomed in Germany, and large numbers in Scandinavia. They could not get into the UK. The UK had its vulnerable persons resettlement scheme, under which it agreed to take 20,000 over a period of five years. That is a really minuscule number, compared with the number who found asylum in Germany.

I do not know that it was such a disaster for Germany. Some reports have come out from the German Government showing that Syrians who received asylum in Germany had the opportunity to start working after six months. They started paying tax much earlier than had been expected, so within four years most of them were paying tax and were paying back the cost that had been spent on them.

That kind of funding can never replace what is happening on the ground. Displaced Syrians recognised in 2015, 2016 that this was not going to be over very quickly. They felt that the regime was going to remain, or that it would fall to ISIS, which was perhaps going to be just as bad. And then it stopped. That was only one year, and in the next year the numbers were greatly reduced. Maybe you can say that that was because the programme was in effect, so those who felt that they really had to move on did, but it was not as though you had another 800,000 or 1 million every year after 2015, 2016. There was a huge drop in numbers coming to Europe afterwards.

Q86 Alex Ballinger: In a very short period, as you say. Thank you. Professor Brun, I want to move on slightly. Many refugees have lived in Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan for a generation, and some have had their entire education in those countries. Could you tell us a bit about the type of education that they have been able to get? Has that enabled younger Syrians to have the skills that they might need to return to Syria or carry on their lives in those countries?

Cathrine Brun: That is a big question, and we have done some research looking at that. For example, in one project we compared what happened in Lebanon, Turkey and Australia. We looked at different kinds of countries and at how different programmes have been working. We saw that the question of how you are included in the national education system eventually means something for how you succeed. We give a lot of credit to Turkey here. It had a more gradual approach to get people into the education system. In Australia, it was much more of a quick integration into the national system. In Lebanon, you have had second shifts, as you also had in Jordan.

Q87 Chair: How many Syrians were there going to Australia? You talk about them being integrated. What are the numbers?

Cathrine Brun: I cannot tell you from the top of my head and I am not going to try to come up with a number. I can give it later. The point is that in what we often call northern states or in Australia you integrate people much more from the beginning and with a more long-term perspective and think of them as part of the education system. In Lebanon and Jordan they are included in the education system, but it is in second shifts. It is shorter hours than the normal shifts and often teachers with less competence.

Q88 Alex Ballinger: So Jordanian children go to school first and then there is another shift of Syrian children going to school?

Cathrine Brun: Yes. That is in some schools because not all schools have this. Currently about 40% of Syrian children in Lebanon go to school. A huge number of kids are not going to school and most of them do not reach secondary school. That means that we have a lost generation in terms of education. That is not a good sign in terms of contributing to rebuilding Syria. Because of labour restrictions, Syrian teachers cannot work in Lebanon or Jordan, which means that there is deskilling of the labour force that could go back to Syria.

I would like to come back to aid here. Similar to the Jordan compact there was a Lebanon compact. Maybe more could have been done from donors as well to think about how we build the competence and capacity of both the host societies and the refugees in exile. Particularly when it comes to Lebanon, Syrian children have lost out quite dramatically. That is going to be a challenge for how we think about return as well.

Dawn Chatty: That is a really important point because throughout the world, particularly in Lebanon and also somewhat in Jordan, the refugees or the displaced Syrians settle in the country’s poorest communities. It is just absolutely calling out for the extension of aid to the host community and the refugees in it—especially in terms of education, because Lebanon does not have a very strong public education system nor a very strong public health system. This kind of aid would be of benefit to Lebanon and its people as well as the refugees.

Cathrine Brun: To add one more point to that, again, it comes back to aid. UK aid has been quite important in supporting education in Lebanon and Jordan. The problem is a little that aid has contributed to somehow create a parallel system rather than strengthening the national system. As Dawn says, in Lebanon, about 70% of Lebanese go to private schools and 30% or less go to public school. It is the public school system that is the last resort for many Lebanese, and it is also where Syrian refugees end up.

Unfortunately, aid has not been able to strengthen that system, but rather in many ways created almost a parallel system. There are many reasons for that including low trust in Government in terms of handling funds and so on. However, there are ways of thinking about how to strengthen that system. That is one of the things that we need to discuss because maybe 700,000 Syrians have gone back from Lebanon to Syria, but there are still a lot of children in Lebanon, Jordan and Turkey. We cannot forget about them, and we need to think how we strengthen those systems to prepare for a return in the long term. I think that is a very important point.

Q89 Jessica Toale: I appreciate the point about skills deterioration in education. I wonder if there are any examples from other sectors—perhaps healthcare, where Syrian doctors have been able to practice—where you have seen the skills maintained and the population supported?

Dawn Chatty: I can speak to the health system. Doctors and nurses are not permitted to work in either Jordan or Lebanon. There have been some exceptions in Turkey—funnily enough, because healthcare is provided by the Government. The Turkish Government have permitted doctors and nurses who were certified outside of Syria to practise because most Turkish doctors cannot speak Arabic and so they needed them. That is normally in the south of the country. In Jordan and Lebanon a number of studies have shown that doctors have sometimes been able to work informally—I am going to say illegally—in existing clinics under the supervision of Lebanese doctors, who also have to be very careful because they are not meant to be employing them.

Q90 Chair: On education, my impression was that Dr Chatty was implying quite heavily that one of the ways to encourage people to go back into Syria is to have an education system that parents would be confident in and that the concern was that, given the trend of the current Government, the education system might be very restrictive. Is that your view?

Dawn Chatty: There is currently a very big debate in Syria because the transitional Government are suggesting that the curriculum— besides removing everything related to the Ba’ath party—change the cartoons in primary schools so that any image of a woman is with a hijab, which is not how Syrian women always dress. It also suggests removing some of the elements that promote an understanding of democracy and how democracy functions. That is being deeply challenged by Syrians who remained in the country as well as those who have recently gone back. There is a huge debate. I think it would be wonderful if European countries could help—although I am not going to say swing the debate, because obviously, in the end, it is what the Syrians decide on. There is a very loud minority and that is trying to make changes that a lot of the population are not happy about.

Q91 Chair: So there is a readiness? Part of our inquiry is looking at what the Foreign Office could do as a positive assistance to Syria. We have heard, and I think that we will hear more, that part of it should be an investment in agriculture and making sure that people can go back to the fields.

Dawn Chatty: Electricity is also in very short supply. There are also landmines so agricultural fields have to be cleared.

Chair: So agriculture, landmines, electricity, water and education.

Dawn Chatty: What is really important is that there needs to be a much more nuanced view of Syria today. The FCDO advisory has the whole of Syria red, which means that we cannot send our doctoral students to Syria. The university will not allow it—they say that it is too much of a risk. Whereas in Lebanon it is very nuanced and there are areas of Lebanon where you can go. If we really want to know what is happening on the ground, it cannot all just be from secondary evidence from our students coming back or what we are reading. We need to look at how Damascus is perfectly safe and parts of Aleppo are also perfectly safe. There are certainly areas where they think that there are ISIS cells, but we can keep those marked red.

Q92 Chair: You think that Damascus and Aleppo are safe.

Dawn Chatty: Yes, absolutely safe. Also, Daraa—except that the Jordanian air force struck it a couple of days ago.

Q93 Chair: And travelling between the two?

Dawn Chatty: No problem at all. The borders are easy to traverse. I think it costs you $100 for a visa at the border. You do not have to do it in advance. A lot of my doctoral students have gone back and forth, but researchers need to go back.

Q94 Chair: Have you?

Dawn Chatty: I have not yet been. I was meant to go last week, but I ended up having to do something else in Amman. But I would have gone—it is not a problem. I am planning to go next year.

Q95 Edward Morello: Professor Toros, I would like to return to the topic of the camps. When you gave evidence to us last year, you talked about the inherent risk of the security of the camps being removed and the camps collapsing. The Syrian Government obviously took over responsibility for security. You mentioned before that al-Roj is still open and al-Hawl was closed. Was that an organised closure? Can you talk us through what happened there? You mentioned that 5,000 of the detainees were removed by the US to Iraq, so what did that closure look like? How many detainees, to the best of your knowledge, were there in that camp, minus the 5,000? What happened to the remaining?

Ultimately, we want to reach a situation with the refugees where the families can go back safely and with dignity, and then the breadwinner can become an economic migrant who is legalised, so they can go back and forth and provide an income for their family from their work in Lebanon. We also need to help our social institutions such as hospitals and schools, because the past 15 years have been taxing on them, especially with the economic collapse; and our security forces and the Lebanese army to help them better control the border, taking from your experience as well.

Q122 Abtisam Mohamed: Your Excellency, Türkiye has been developing a range of infrastructure support in Syria. What could the UK do to support that?

Osman Koray Ertaş: We have been in close contact with the UK on all aspects of Syria. I have been in regular contact with the UK Syria envoy, who has now become your ambassador in Yemen. Just last week, our Deputy Minister was here just for consultations on Syria and how we can work together to support Syria’s transition and the rebuilding of the country. Our development agencies are in touch—TİKA is our development agency, and it is in talks with our colleagues at the FCDO.

We have to work together, especially on financial services, where the UK has specific expertise, as well as on, obviously, education and critical infrastructures, including larger ones such as the airport. We have to support the new Government in their transition efforts, as well as their efforts to rebuild the country. We will focus on project-based, specific examples where we can work together.

Q123 Alex Ballinger: This question is slightly connected to what we were talking about before, Your Excellency, which was the Kurdish armed forces or militias and their integration with the Syrian army. From Turkey’s perspective, do you see that as a positive step and perhaps a step towards normalisation of the tensions between the various Kurdish groups and Turkey? Will that be a step towards a more secure and stable environment in the north of Syria?

Osman Koray Ertaş: Of course, we want to see a peaceful transition, and we want to see the Syrian Government in effective control of the entire country, so the deal between the SDF and the Syrian Government was a welcome agreement for us as well. As Türkiye, we have been very supportive of this process from early on.

As you know, in Türkiye, we also have an ongoing political process, which is managed and supervised by our Parliament, with the support of most of the political parties, and that process also helps what is going on inside Syria, in terms of the integration. We continue to monitor the process in a positive way and to support the agreements so that eventually there will be a peaceful transition to a unified state in Syria.

Q124 Alex Ballinger: I think it is a very positive development. The further this process can go towards a sustainable peace between the Kurdish groups and Turkey will be very positive.

Osman Koray Ertaş: Sometimes there is a feeling that Türkiye or the Turkish people are against the Kurds. That narrative is false actually, and politically loaded. Kurds are essential elements of our society, of our country. They own our country, together with other ethnicities. Regardless of any ethnicity, every Turkish citizen belongs to the country.

For years, for decades, we have had a very good relationship with the Kurds of northern Iraq, for example. Today, Erbil is one of the most advanced cities in Iraq, and that is mostly because of that relationship. During that time, Türkiye has campaigned for the restitution of the rights of the Kurds in Syria. We have no issues with the Kurds; our issue for decades has been with PKK terrorism, which has cost tens of thousands of lives and trillions of dollars of financial burden. Hopefully, with these new processes, we will leave the difficult issues behind us and move forward to a peaceful future.
