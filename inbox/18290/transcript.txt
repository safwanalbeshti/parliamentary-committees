Examination of witnesses

Philip Duffy and Malcolm Lythgo.

The Chair: Good morning and welcome to the Lords Committee on the Environment and Climate Change. Today, we will be hearing from the CEO of the Environment Agency, Philip Duffy, on two subjects. The first is waste crime, which will be the third follow-up session of the committee’s waste crime inquiry. The second part of the meeting will focus on drought to, in essence, understand the situation since the committee’s report on drought was published in May this year.

Before we start, I remind everyone that the session will be webcast live on Parliament TV and that a transcript will be taken and made public. Members are reminded that they should declare any relevant interests the first time they speak. I will take the opportunity here to say that I am a director of Peers for the Planet and, when we come to the drought report, that I own a borehole on our home in South Oxfordshire.

Today is a little unusual, colleagues. Before we start, I will let everyone know that, at 11 am, we will stand to observe the two-minute silence in remembrance of the armistice. The committee’s clerk, Alastair, will give me the signal when it is almost time. That is just a heads-up.

We should start business. First of all, Philip Duffy, thank you very much for the letter that we received yesterday. We had been waiting for it for some time, but it is good that it arrived in time for this meeting. The first question that I would like to put to you is to ask you to update the committee on Operation Unite and, in particular, what challenges were faced and lessons learned from this campaign. Please introduce the campaign to us.

Philip Duffy: Thank you very much, Chair. It is great to be back here talking to you about this important subject. When I was last in front of the committee, I flagged Operation Unite as, in essence, an experiment to see whether we could improve the response across the organisation and with our law enforcement partners.

There were three big things that we were trying to achieve. The first was being genuinely intelligence-driven, so not just waiting until we are looking at a piece of rubbish in a field, but thinking ahead about where we think criminals are infiltrating the waste system, and taking a more proactive, targeted approach to where we put our enforcement activity.

The second approach was to look at permits. It is, to my mind and the minds of many who look at the Environment Agency, peculiar that the agency issues permits to regulate all sorts of activity, but that we very rarely revoke or suspend them, which means that you can, in some cases, breach those permits year after year with no great consequence. We want to change that, and so one of the central bits of work that we did over the last 18 weeks was to visit 42 permitted sites, and we revoked or suspended 17 of the permits held at those sites. That sent quite a strong signal to people in the waste sector that we do not regard compliance as optional. It also sent a signal to communities, many of which are experiencing smell, poor odour control, dust and flies from poorly maintained waste sites, that the agency will not be tolerating that year after year. That is exactly what people expect the agency to be doing in its regulatory work.

Thirdly, we were really joining up with law enforcement partners, notably the police forces, the National Crime Agency and HMRC, to apply much more effective pressure on organised criminals. To give a sense of the activity that took place over that 18-week period between April and October, there were 37 waste crime arrests. We also issued 13 restriction orders banning people access to land to dump. We concluded 71 waste-related court cases in that period, of which 52 led to conviction. There were six not-guilty findings, so that is a reasonable performance, I would say.

We have also been trying to strengthen our work on asset confiscation, which was raised with me when I was last here. Waste crime is, fundamentally, an economic crime, with waste and community consequences, and the question that we are grappling with is how best to deny those criminals access to significant profits. The best way of all is to deny them access to the waste stream and to take their permits away, but the second best way is to go after their criminal proceeds.

We launched six criminal confiscation investigations as part of Operation Unite, and £4.4 million of criminal money was identified in those exercises. We also have nine live money laundering investigations with partners coming out of Operation Unite.

The Chair: We will be coming to confiscation orders later on in the session, but, for now, can I just concentrate on Operation Unite? It is taking action against permitted sites. The OEP did a report on permitted sites in May this year, and sent us a copy. One of the things that it found was that, while the Environment Agency aims to inspect all permitted sites at least once a year, 29% of sites were uninspected in 2022. How are those 29% of sites that are not inspected treated?

Philip Duffy: The OEP makes a very good point about the state of performance in the 2021-22 financial year. We are in the process of issuing a long series of control strategies. They are quite short and say, in essence, if you hold a permit with the Environment Agency, how often you can expect to be visited and what level of work you need to do. It will vary because we have some parts of the economy that are very compliant, and others that are very non-compliant, so you would expect there to be a variation in how often they would do that.

A visit once every three years is not an unacceptable level of performance from the agency, because we know that many of these sites are quite compliant and do not require an annual inspection. If, of course, we find or have reports of non-compliance, you would want to move to an annual inspection. If you have a situation in which a site has been D/E/F band compliant for more than one year, you would expect multiple inspections over the course of a year until that is resolved. That is our thinking as to how we are going to approach that in the future.

The Chair: The review found that the 29% of sites that are not inspected by the Environment Agency are automatically recorded as being in band A, which is the highest compliance band. It goes on to recommend that the Environment Agency does not place uninspected sites in band A by default. Do you accept that recommendation?

Philip Duffy: Yes. I agree with that recommendation and we have written back to the OEP to make that point.

The Chair: Will you be making changes?

Philip Duffy: Yes. In a way, they do not have a band because they have not been inspected. We should not assume that they are non-compliant either; that would not be appropriate. We should reserve judgment until they have been visited. As I say, a visit every three years at a minimum is an acceptable level of resourcing. Below three years is a problem.

The Chair: It does skew reporting. Some 97% of the Environment Agency’s permitted sites are recorded as being compliant, but this does not give us the confidence to assume that that figure is real.

Malcolm Lythgo: Our compliance assessment is based not just on our inspections. We receive a lot of data from permitted sites. Even though we may not have inspected certain sites, we will have reviewed their data. We will go through their monitoring data and their data returns, and that gives us an indication as to whether they are taking more waste than they are allowed. We can do some remote assessment, but visits are important and we should do those too.

The Chair: The OEP report also has reservations about remote monitoring. I will not go into that, but will ask how you treat band C sites and the anomalies within that. Band C sites are recorded as compliant with their permits, even if non-compliance has been detected and the site “must improve in order to achieve permit compliance”. That is unbelievable.

Philip Duffy: I do not think that it is unbelievable. You have to have a proportionate and staged approach when dealing with bits of infrastructure that are extremely important for the functioning of the economy. There is an important point to make about understanding that one or several breaches that are minor, such as driving routes or a failure in dust control, would not normally lead to immediate enforcement. We have a whole range of enforcement activities. At the most basic end, it is guidance to an operator to do that, and then we step it up.

Of course, to some degree, there is a judgment as to whether something is a C or a D. We can talk about whether that judgment is right or wrong, and there may be people out there living near a band C functioning permitted waste site who would disagree with the notion that it should stay open, because these sites can be difficult to live nearby. On the other hand, we have to take a balanced view, make sure that our regulation is proportionate and give operators the chance to recover the situation.

What is not acceptable is that we allow operators to stay in D, E and F when we have really quite severe concerns about non-compliance year after year. That was the situation that we had previously, and that is clearly unacceptable. Our simple message here is that, if you are in band D, E or F, you will get an opportunity to improve. If you do not improve, you will be suspended. If you still do not improve, you will have your permit revoked.

The Chair: Operation Unite is targeted at permitted waste sites. We understand that, if a permit is revoked, the operator or waste carrier must permanently cease operations. What happens once a permit is revoked? How many permits are revoked versus how many are suspended in the figure that you gave us?

Philip Duffy: Let us start with revocation. It depends on what permit we are revoking for what kind of activity, because we have not just landfills, but also facilities that process materials and waste handling stations that weigh materials. It will depend on the issue.

If it is a handling situation, the business closes; there is not really much more follow-up to that. The issue is much more severe if we were to revoke a permit on a landfill. We have done that at Walleys Quarry in Stoke-on-Trent during my time at the Environment Agency. That raises all sorts of quite complicated policy and social questions.

Landfill operators are required to post with the Environment Agency a financial bond, which is intended to cover the immediate make-safe costs should, for some reason, the business stop operating, either through insolvency or through regulatory closure. In general, we have found that that level of resourcing enables us to, for example, secure the site with fencing and make sure that the gas management systems are in place to remove gases that could become dangerous or build up on the landfill site.

We do, however, face really serious choices in some landfills around the balance between a peremptory closure on very sound regulatory or criminal grounds, and the ongoing running of that landfill to make sure that it is closed in good order. If you have studied landfills, you will know that it is important to make sure that the shape of the waste is right and the facing of the waste is right. We are very alive to that trade-off that we have to make. We want the criminals and the non-compliance driven out, but we also need to make sure that communities are not left with a landfill that has been unresolved.

There is an issue that we are discussing with the Government, who you will know are taking a very close interest in the whole question of waste crime, about whether this sort of regime is adequate for the future and provides sufficient financial guarantees to make sure that, at the higher-risk levels of landfill, we have enough resources to manage, should the site be closed unexpectedly for any reason.

The Chair: As of yet, you have been focusing on whether a site fulfils its permit and bond requirements, et cetera. Have you also been looking at sites where misdescription of waste and criminal activity may be taking place? Have any of those permitted sites been addressed?

Philip Duffy: It is a great question because the truth is that the largest degree of harm from waste crime is not the visible dumping in fields or poorly managed permitted sites. It is tax evasion and people misdeclaring, while treating the waste in a perfectly compliant manner. The waste itself has gone to a landfill or a treatment centre precisely in compliance with its permit, but the paperwork around it has declared that it was inorganic waste, such as construction material, when, in reality, it was organic material attracting a higher rate of landfill tax. That is the primary source of criminal arbitrage in this sector, not the sorts of offences that the EA is dealing with, and we have a lot of intelligence about that.

One thing that we have been doing in Operation Unite is trying to integrate our intelligence with our partners, particularly HMRC and the police, and thinking about how best to disrupt that sort of activity. You would not disrupt it by revoking the permit, since the permit is in good order. You would have to disrupt it in some other way. How might you do that?

You are then looking at, in essence, law enforcement through forensic accountancy on the part of HMRC, disruption to flows of money, so that the receipts from this tax-evading money are being properly dealt with, and making sure that people who knowingly do business with this tax-evading entity understand that they may be involved in crime. We can do that by calling out those offences, or by making sure, for example, that people face fixed penalty notices for driving waste to sites when they do not have the right paperwork.

The Chair: Have you revoked any permits?

Philip Duffy: No, not for that kind of offence. We would not, because the permit is generally compliant.

The Chair: The other issue that we have heard about is transfer stations, where we are talking about 38 million tonnes of waste each year being diverted. This is on an industrial scale. We are not talking about the odd fly-tipping instance, although that is where a lot of it will start. That waste is diverted and ends up somewhere such as Hoad’s Wood or Kidlington. This is waste that has been treated in some way, we understand, through a waste transfer station. Instead of continuing its journey through the legal sector, it finds its way into our environment and is illegally dumped. Landfill taxes are avoided, et cetera, and the taxpayer is left to pick up the bill, as has happened in all of the cases where you are clearing up the waste. Has Operation Unite done anything to address this particular issue?

Philip Duffy: Your description of the offending is accurate. I do not think that anyone in the EA would quibble with that. The key point of Operation Unite is that, as you say, these flows of illegal waste that are going into the dumps have passed through the legitimate waste system. Some people in that system knew full well that they were handing that waste on to criminals. They may or may not have been corrupted, but they certainly knew they were not exercising due care, as they are legally required to do, over who was taking their waste forward.

A quite simple diagnosis for that particular problem is that we have to make sure that, if you do not exercise due care over your waste, there are very severe consequences. The most severe consequence that is in our immediate gift is to suspend or revoke a permit. There are more things that we did not do in Operation Unite that we should do. For example, we do not have a good, well-developed approach to anti-corruption in the waste sector, in either public and private authorities, so we do not know whether the waste criminals are bribing people to acquire this waste. Focusing, as you have said, on the transfer facilities and the MRFs—material facilities—to make sure that they always routinely understand where their waste is going is the most important aspect of turning this around.

The Chair: Thank you. We will come on to ways in which we can do that.

Malcolm Lythgo: For all 42 of the sites that we visited under Operation Unite, we had intelligence that there was criminal activity happening. We did not inspect them because of their permit.

The Chair: What sort of criminal activity?

Malcolm Lythgo: There was a variety. It would be that waste had been handled legally, but that fraud may be happening, or that waste was being taken from that site to an inappropriate site. Our intelligence pointed us to all those 42 sites, so all of Operation Unite was about going up stream, trying to track that waste, and seeing what happens. I am pleased to say that our intelligence was pretty good. The fact that we can revoke that many permits that quickly means that the intelligence was telling us something, and it triggered 30 criminal investigations off the back of it. All of Operation Unite was very much focused on going up stream and dealing with that sort of criminality, not permit compliance.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: Good morning and welcome. We had the 10-point plan published in March this year. How has the plan changed the approach that the Environment Agency takes to deterrence across the whole waste supply chain?

Philip Duffy: The point behind the 10-point plan is to attack the problem from every direction that we can. Probably the most impactful thing is the construction of better intelligence capability. I have been very surprised by what we can achieve by bringing together not only covert but overt intelligence. Who is buying and selling land out there? Who has an HGV licence from the traffic commissioners? What address did they give for that? Who is applying for a new carrier, broker or dealer permit? We are trying to bring that together so that the sorts of targeted work that my colleague is discussing can be more effective. We are spending quite a lot of money this year on the IT build to do that, and we have had really good co-operation from partners across government.

The second thing that is important for this committee is transparency. You can sometimes feel like there are all sorts of ways of assessing waste sites and their severity and social impact. We have taken an approach of publishing as much as we can so that committees such as this can have a look at that data and see whether you agree with our judgments—you may or may not, and we will listen to your views on those things—and updating that to make sure that communities can see what we are doing. That is really important for reassurance and public confidence.

We have covered a lot about permits being at risk if you are engaged with criminality. It is a message that I want to repeat here. Behind the scenes, the joint working is really important. We have the joint unit on waste crime. We are getting very strong support from the National Crime Agency, from the constabularies, and from HMRC. I will not go into the details of the cases, but we have seen some of the case handling. We have moved cases between different organisations. We have said, “This is really an HMRC case. This is really an NCA case. We are not the right body” or, “EA should take on this case”, so all those disruptive tools that we can use are there.

It is too early at this point to say whether this has moved the dial. We have anecdotes, because we track a lot of the organised crime gangs, that show that they are feeling the pressure from UK law enforcement and reconsidering their business choices. That is merely an anecdote at this stage, and we have to look at the overall surveys that we are going to do, but we are feeling quite positive that we have gone in the right direction. There is still a huge amount more to do, to be quite clear, but that has been a positive step forward.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: Have the powers that you were seeking been granted to you under the police and criminal evidence enforcement powers?

Malcolm Lythgo: The powers are still a work in progress with government. We have gone back with a list of things that we would like to have. Other organisations have been involved in that discussion, and Defra is taking those forward. Some of those, where they are related to secondary legislation, are at a more advanced stage, but Defra is working through what we can do within that and having discussions with Home Office and others on the mechanism for delivery.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: So you still do not have powers of entry and evidence at the moment.

Philip Duffy: We had some farcical scenes over the summer, so it might be sensible if I can just highlight for the committee some of the problems that we have with the powers. We need to get restriction orders. We have been saying to our teams, “Get there in 48 hours”, because we want people on the ground quickly. When they find that there is crime there, they want to stop the site being accessed, but we have to have a restriction order.

We have had a case where we just could not get court time. We kept going. It was adjourned. The landowner was unrepresented. That was adjourned. There is no default. We have gone for weeks and weeks when we and the community know that there is a problem, but there is no restriction order in place. We need a system that says that we have a faster response. There is no point in us being there in 48 hours if there is nothing that we can do and we just watch the offending going on, so there are a few things there. The Government are well aware of that, and we are having very constructive conversations with them about what would be necessary to turn that situation around.

There are powers in the tax system, which I envy, about whether someone is fit and proper to hold a permit. I have been quite clear that we would like those in the environmental situation. It absolutely cannot be right that a person who we know through intelligence is involved in offending can apply for a waste permit and the Environment Agency must grant it. That cannot be a justifiable situation to be in. On this issue of powers, we are going to have to keep arguing for that and, clearly, it may require legislation in due course.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: It helps us to know that. Could I move on to fixed penalty notice interventions? These were announced by the Government in July 2026. There are two schools of thought here. How successful will tackling waste crime be when previous fine regimes have failed?

Have the fines been set at the right level? Even though they have been increased to £5,000, having heard what you have just told us today, it is thousands if not hundreds of thousands of pounds that the perpetrators can make. If you give them a £5,000 penalty notice, is that a deterrent? Can you then go on and prosecute them if you have the evidence in court?

Philip Duffy: They have a role, but they are not the answer to the problem that Baroness Sheehan has just described of very organised, large-scale offending. However, there is merit in making the lives of organised criminals just harder in general by adding more friction to their lives.

I see a real role for fixed penalty notices in making sure that it is harder for them to hire a driver who is somewhere on the margin. They have been paid some cash to drive a load. They do not know about the overall plot. They do not know about the tax situation. They are just helping. If we were clear that drivers should not drive loads without having proper documentation because they would face a financial penalty, that could dissuade some of these operations at the margins.

There is a role, and a lot of our field teams think that there is a merit to it, because they want to send a signal in real time to people who may be collaborating with dodgy operators that they should not do so. We are piloting it. We have done only 64 since I last came before the committee, which is not very many. We have piloted them in two broad areas: people who are transhipping waste, so sending waste overseas, without proper documentation, and people who are driving without proper waste documentation.

At the minute, that is not issued in the field. We find the offence, take the address down, authorise it and write to the person, and they have generally paid the fixed penalties. We know that timeliness and certainty are massive factors in deterrence. The ability of our officers to issue an FPN in the field and say, “You now owe me this money” will be really transformative. That is our next stage of work and is what my field teams who work for the EA really want to see happen.

As you said, the Prime Minister has said that he wants to increase the level from a few hundred pounds to £5,000. We also need to look at how we enforce those penalties, because penalties that are not enforced are a waste of everyone’s time. It is currently only a criminal offence not to pay a penalty, which would require us to take a criminal prosecution; that is not a brilliant way of running a system. We have some operational concerns that we are discussing with the Government about how you would operationalise a higher fine level. It is a niche role, but quite useful for enforcement.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: Could you go on and prosecute? That was not clear.

Philip Duffy: No, not on that offence, I do not think.

Malcolm Lythgo: You would not be able to prosecute on that offence, but you could prosecute them if you caught them committing more serious offences, even though you had issued a fixed penalty notice.

Lord Rooker: There have been very interesting answers to the questions. I have reread your letter, which I know came to us late, but the word “prosecution” does not appear to be in it. There is a reference to one case in Wigan, where five men were arrested. If they had been charged, I am sure that you would have told us, so I assume that they have not. What have you done to improve your record on securing prosecutions? Do you have a desire to prosecute when the circumstances are right? I get the impression that you do not.

Philip Duffy: I do not think that that is right. We are very keen to use criminal law wherever we can. Let me just provide some statistics or some reality as to what it is like currently trying to prosecute criminal cases. Over the period between April and October, the EA brought 71 criminal prosecutions in court. We got only two immediate custodial sentences and eight suspended sentences from that, and the fine total was £118,000, which is quite small compared to the scale of the offending.

It can take us over a year from arrest to laying charges. If the case is complex and involves lots of evidence, some of which might have closed material procedures, that can take time. Court listings can be very slow; it can take us years to get to one, so you will have to be patient on court cases. There is no lack of will from the EA to bring these cases. We are quite forward leaning.

I would say, though, that permit revocation, going after assets, civil offences and other forms of disruption can also apply pressure on committed criminals. If you were talking to other people from UK law enforcement, they would be saying, as I am saying to you, that you have to look at the complete range of disruptions, including travel bans, asset seizure and proceeds of crime orders, to dissuade criminals who may not be particularly dissuaded by a relatively short custodial sentence.

Lord Rooker: In answer to an earlier question, you mentioned 71 prosecutions. Why is that not referred to in your letter? The word “prosecution” does not appear in this letter. You are claiming that you have made 71 prosecutions. I would have thought that it would have been quite important to let us know about this, rather than keeping it to the last minute. Have I got that right?

Philip Duffy: I hope that you feel that I am being transparent with the committee. I am making sure that you have all the facts that you need about the situation. If you need more facts and figures, I will provide those. Transparency is incredibly important in working with parliamentary oversight.

The letter was really talking about the structural changes that we are making, which are about going after the very serious organised criminality that the Chair has raised. It was not about our current operational prosecution work. I am very happy to talk to the committee at any point on what we are up to in the courts, our strategies, and the outcomes.

I would highlight to the committee that, despite that number of prosecutions and a high level of convictions, the fine total was £118,000. As the committee itself has recognised, we are dealing with multimillion-pound offending here, so I have to question how we effectively respond to an incentive structure that is in the tens of millions, when the fine level that we are achieving in court from all those prosecutions is £118,000. That is something that the committee should reflect upon.

Lord Rooker: I accept that completely, but, of course, the prosecution is on the offender’s record, and that has to be a factor. On your point about the plan, notwithstanding all the plans that you have talked about, is there anything else that is preventing you from being more effective in the enforcement of waste crime?

Philip Duffy: There are many things. First, we have covered the whole issue of getting restriction orders more quickly and making the landowner make the case that they should not have a restriction order, rather than us having to find court slots to do that. That would be very important.

Secondly, we have real problems in the permit system, which is an environmental law that crosses over with the tax evasion and criminality problem here. We need to say, “You can’t have a permit, even if you did comply with the environmental law, because you have criminal convictions and are not a fit and proper person”.

Thirdly, we need to look at who really controls permits. We have seen market manoeuvres where there was a legitimate permit, with a legitimate permit operator, and the company or the asset was bought by someone else. The permit transfers with them and we cannot say, “Hang on. We don’t want that permit run by you”. We have no re-permitting process.

Fourthly, you can find an old permit that has not been revoked and you can reopen the site. In the meantime, the planning authority could have granted homes around the area and people may have changed. That feels like a real problem for communities around the country.

I mentioned anti-corruption. We have a real problem with who is signing off these contracts, either in local government or in the supply chain. Are we confident that we have enough controls to drive out corrupt practices from those areas? Transparency in that area is really important.

Lastly, deterrence requires not only a response but certainty of conviction and a high enough penalty. At the minute, the risk of losing money, going to prison or having a fine is not high enough to deter people. You mentioned criminal records; many people who we are working with here have very long criminal records. If having a criminal record was dissuasive, they would not be where they are now. If you really want to dissuade them, you have to find something else that is going to be more dissuasive. If that is not jail time, what is it going to be? It probably going to have to be financial penalties or loss of assets.

There is quite a lot there, end to end, that we need to look at, and that may require legislation.

The Chair: You have just listed a litany of loopholes, so it is no surprise that the criminals are running rings around the enforcement agencies. Which government department’s responsibility is it to close those loopholes?

Philip Duffy: We are working very closely with Defra, which is the lead department for waste. Under the Secretary of State’s chairmanship, it has called together a cross-government, multiagency group called the Waste Crime Crackdown Group, which met last week. At that meeting, the Secretary of State was very clear to the Home Office, the National Crime Agency, the National Police Chiefs’ Council, senior people from HMRC and Treasury Ministers that this required a whole-of-government effort. We should be adopting the best practice that we see in things such as dealing with tax evasion and moving it into this regime.

That may require legislation, and no one is denying that, but the Government are thinking very hard about that. The Prime Minister has been public that he thinks that this is a serious scandal that he wishes to resolve. There is a lot of work under way within government to look systematically at all the loopholes—I could have gone on; there are more—and make sure that they are being systematically addressed.

Baroness Whitaker: Good morning. Perhaps you have captured this, but is there no equivalent to the industrial prohibition notice, whereby the inspector can just stop an activity there and then, without the expense and time involved in going to court? I should perhaps declare that I have previous, in that I used to work at the Health and Safety Executive. It was a long time ago. Prohibition notices were very effective. They can be appealed against, but, on the whole, they were not.

Philip Duffy: The answer is that I do not know.

Malcolm Lythgo: I am not sure about prohibition notices at waste sites. At permitted sites, though, we can suspend very quickly and there is no right of appeal.

Baroness Whitaker: How quickly?

Malcolm Lythgo: As quickly as coming up with the paperwork and getting it issued. You would have to go back and look at the permit, and we would run it through the legal team, but you are talking about a matter of a day or so, I would imagine.

Philip Duffy: I have seen this in the COMAH regime for health and safety and industrial emissions, but not in waste.

Malcolm Lythgo: I do not think that there is a stop notice within permitted sites. Similarly, for illegal sites, we do not have that power. We have to go to court to get the restriction order issued.

Baroness Whitaker: Would you like to have something like that?

Philip Duffy: It would be much better for us to have a power that says, “You are dumping waste in this field. You must stop immediately. If you think we have got it wrong, you go to court and explain why we have got it wrong” than for us to have to go to court and say, “Can we please get a court listing to stop this person driving on to a field and dumping waste?”, which is how it currently works. That feels to me very suboptimal.

Baroness Whitaker: I must leave that with you, because I have a different question to ask. The Government gave you £45 million last March, which I expect you found very welcome. What are you doing with it to improve the response to waste crime?

Philip Duffy: The profile of the money is roughly £4.5 million in the first year, and then £20 million in each of the subsequent two years. That is not unreasonable, because you cannot immediately hire people and spend money in year. It is not just about public campaigns, but is also about making sure that we have those areas.

We also know quite a lot about behavioural science and water consumption. We know that, unlike in other areas of environmental activism, your individual behaviour impacts on water resources in the place where you live. Water does not move. There is genuinely a relationship between how much water you use and whether your severe drought will be continuing. That is an important and powerful message. We have seen, from places such as South Africa, the importance of making that connection when things are really difficult.

People also want fairness. They want to know that, if they are making sacrifices, others are too, and that what people regard as being unserious use of water is not tolerated. We have to be very careful to make sure that any use bans or hosepipe bans are seen to be credible and fair across the whole system in the community, not just arbitrary.

Lord Trees: I was pleased to hear you mentioning moving water in the previous answer. That was one of the recommendations that we made in our drought report, to involve drainage boards, canals, and so on, and we have big systems, but the Government rejected that recommendation. I wonder whether you could comment on that.

Philip Duffy: The way in which water moves currently is, in general, through private law agreements between these privatised companies. There is also then a layer of regulatory activity through a joint unit called RAPID between us and Ofwat, which looks at opportunities for schemes that we can take forward through the price reviews to fund the movement of water. We have some schemes of that sort currently under way.

If the system is under greater pressure—and it is—and if the Environment Agency is, for example, saying to Anglia Water, “You must fill Grafham as quickly as possible” or, “You must fill the west London system as quickly as possible”, some of those agreements may come under some tension because the company has to prioritise its own water assets, and so there is a case for a strategic overview of the system. Where is the water moving between the companies? Is it adequate? Do they make consistent assumptions between the areas? We have sometimes found that they do not have consistent assumptions.

I understand that there is going to be a Bill to look at the reform of the water system, and one of the things that you might see in that Bill, when it is published, is some new capability to look at the system as a whole and ensure that, when we do price reviews, the interconnectors and the balance between companies has been properly thought through. Occasionally, that has not been the case.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: You have rightly identified the issue for on-farm reservoirs as being the environmental permitting regime. I would just like to put on record that Yorkshire Water separately spent millions of pounds on a grid moving water around what is a very large region, and we still find ourselves in a drought situation for the reason that Lord Redwood gave, which is that the water is going out to sea.

Another recommendation in our drought report was that abstraction licensing should be moved into the environmental permitting regime. Can I simply ask that you use your good offices of the Environment Agency in this infrastructure body to which you referred to make sure that we get an urgent date for when this is done?

I am a firm believer that smaller reservoirs are the way forward. We still have not heard from the Environment Agency about the Balmforth review and what is required. I bear the scars of Cow Green reservoir being built in Upper Teesdale when it was not required. Many of these larger reservoirs serve a purpose, but they take 10 years to be constructed, and there is a huge demand for on-farm reservoirs now.

Philip Duffy: I need to say something about this issue of water going to sea, because there is an important counterargument. We are balancing some pretty extreme measures to tilt the system towards public water supply at the current time, and that is cutting the available water for nature. There was a period this summer when the River Exe was running at its 99th percentile of low flow, which has consequences for fish populations and for Atlantic salmon. We may or may not prioritise that, but I need to record that we have a legal obligation to consider those factors in this trade-off. We consider them very seriously, but we are tilting that abstraction in favour of public water supply.

There are limits to how quickly you can pump. You have to have the right pumping equipment and the right set-up for depth, and it can be difficult to pump when a river is in full spate because of the volume and the speed of the water. We are doing our best to make sure that, when we have heavy rain, we are not missing a day of it.

On the question of EPR, I totally agree. Some abstraction permits date from law from the 1960s and are on paper. To operationalise the hands-on within constraints/hands-off system that I have just discussed with Lord Redwood, you would need to have the right technology and a more dynamic permit that was clear that you could change those things really rapidly.

We also have really strange things in our abstraction permits. We have dates, based on historic farming practice, of when you can start or stop abstraction. They often say that you can start on 1 November. That is fine if you were looking at the rainfall pattern when the permit was issued, but it may not be fine for the current conditions. This is an inflexible system. It is not automated. It needs to go into EPR to be fixed. Ministers have heard that from us loud and clear.

I was not quite sure about Balmforth, which is about the health and safety of the reservoir system.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: It is the de minimis rule that I understand is a factor preventing smaller reservoirs being built, and yet they tend to be non-threatening to communities downstream. I really think that we need to have an answer to that quite soon.

Philip Duffy: I favour a system that says, “If the reservoir looks like this, it is not going to pose risks, and you do not need to have all this work. If it looks like that, so raised in certain locations, you are going to need to get a safety case”. We saw at Toddbrook how serious the issues could be from reservoirs. We are worried about reservoir safety. We take our Reservoirs Act responsibilities very seriously.

You are also right about on-farm reservoirs. They are sunken, so they pose a risk of people drowning in them and they need to have a fence around them, but an on-farm reservoir in the east of England for arable farming is not going to pose significant flood risk concerns, and we need to be transparent and proportionate in that.

Lord Ashcombe: I would like to take you back to leakage. I work with oil and gas companies. Let me tell you, if you had leakage on anything close to the scale that we have in water, you would have a problem. They get concerned about any oil getting out of a pipe. I work a lot in America, where they get the book thrown at them extremely quickly. They also have a huge number of smart pigs, which they run through the pipes to make sure that they are sound. Why is that not happening here, and why are the pipes not being repaired?

Philip Duffy: There is a lot of innovation in the sector, and you can see that. A number of companies are doing exactly that, using smart mobile sondes to look at the system. Colleagues would correct me if I did not mention the fact that you need some level of leakage in water, because one of the ways that you keep the pipes sterile is by putting chlorinated water through any gaps in the system, so you need quite a high-pressure system.

Lord Ashcombe: How much would that be?

Philip Duffy: It would be a low percentage, and not 19%. Clearly, the judgment reached in the companies has been that the cost‑benefit on further leakage reduction just is not there, because they have access to water and did not need to spend the extra money on fixing these systems. What we are learning is that that cost-benefit judgment could be wrong, and that the cost of providing alternative water, so desalination, very difficult environmental issues, further abstraction and trade-offs with industry, might be so high that you really do need to focus on getting your leakage rates cut further, even though it is very expensive to do so in many areas.

Something that Sir Jon Cunliffe observed in his review of the water regulatory system is this lack of a real understanding of the asset lifecycle in water. When are your assets are going to be life expired? What is the correct level of ongoing maintenance that you need to maintain an effective performance of those assets? Do the regulators, in which I include the EA, have a good enough understanding of the asset lifecycle to point out to the companies, “This needs to be replaced. You need to be fixing this at this point”?

With the new regulator that will arrive, we are trying to build a system that is supervisory. One thing that it should be looking at is, “Do you have the kind of asset lifecycle that you need to show that your leakage is up or down, and where have you priced the marginal pound in terms of repairs to leakage?” A lot of water company chief executives right now are realising that they may have drawn that line in the wrong place, because the cost of meeting their public water supply obligations is going to go up a lot if they do not get the leakage down. It is about half our gap of water supply leakage.

Lord Ashcombe: Do the water companies have a full view of the state of their pipes?

Philip Duffy: Some do and some do not.

Lord Ashcombe: What are you doing about it?

Philip Duffy: We are challenging on it. We are talking to Ofwat about it. We are asking them to take further steps to give us monitoring data. We are also looking, in the context of next year’s legislation, at whether we need more powers to direct the water companies in certain areas. We may do.

Lord Lennie: On the leakage percentage, we were told by witnesses that 19% was about the European average. It was not high but was not particularly low. I do not know what the number is for leakage in the American system. Do you have a comment on that?

Philip Duffy: I do not know.

Lord Lennie: What are you aiming to get it to?

Philip Duffy: If you look at the cost of meeting the public water supply, given what we know about population growth, as Lord Redwood said, as well as hotter summers, which we have also seen, the question is around the most cost-effective way to do that. The view in the industry was, “It’s not leakage. We could build more reservoirs. We could find other ways of sourcing water. We could get desalination”, in the case of Beckton.

We would say that that judgment needs to be looked at again, because the cost of doing this is very high. If you look at the impact on the economy of the water constraint that we see somewhere such as greater Cambridge, you could see real loss of economic output caused by inadequate supply of water, and so you should find every cost-effective way of reducing it further. We are not saying that it should be zero, but 19% is still too high in our judgment.

Lord Krebs: Could I just go back to abstraction? As I recall, in our inquiry, we were told by some representatives of the farming community that the EA was reducing the amount of water that farms could abstract because they had an obligation to hold back water to support nature. I just wanted to check whether that is correct and, indeed, you are taking water away from farmers to protect nature. Given what you just said a few minutes ago about abstracting water to support the public water supply, in that case, how do you balance the requirements of nature versus those of the public water supply?

Philip Duffy: In some of our catchments, we have to make that trade-off every day between our legal obligations towards nature, which requires a certain level of flow to support things such as migratory fish—there are laws passed that require us to think about that in such a situation—our obligation to meet public water supply, what farmers are allowed to have and what industry needs. We have to balance all those factors in our work every day.

This summer, particularly in places such as Norfolk, we saw such low flows in the rivers that we did, in fact, tell farmers that they could not take any more water. They had hands-off restrictions. While they have an abstraction licence, they cannot do that below a certain level of water, because the judgment that we have made is that you would be causing irreversible damage to the natural environment if you allowed the river to run dry. You could make a different judgment and allow it to run dry; that is what the law says currently, but we have to make that judgment all the time.

We completely understand why we need to preserve water supply for farmers. It is very odd that you can have a position in which, if you are accessing water from the public water supply, you can still access water, but, if you are a farmer who has either a private borehole or a surface water abstraction licence, you get your water supply turned off in the same season. That does not feel to us to be particularly justifiable. It is logical, because they come from different sources—the public water supply comes from reservoirs, and this is directly from the natural environment—but we need to see it as a system.

I mentioned fairness of how we do that. Some farmers felt that this was unfair, and I can see why they did. That is an area that we are going to look at very carefully for next summer, because we know that some of these rivers, particularly in the east of England, will be very low next summer.

Lord Krebs: Another thing that we were told was that the price of abstracted water does not necessarily reflect its scarcity.

Philip Duffy: It is not priced currently in that way. There is a fee given to it, and that fee supports a whole range of regulatory activities and environmental support, but you are not charged per unit or for what you are doing with it. It is not a tax. It is a requited fee, in the Treasury’s terminology. Some people are taking water and making a lot of money with it, and some are taking it and making no money with it, but they pay the same fee schedule. There is a perfectly good reason for looking at that. It would be converting the Environment Agency’s regulatory fees into something a bit more like a tax.
