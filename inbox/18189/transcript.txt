Examination of witness

Witness: Rt Hon Lucy Powell MP.

Chair: Welcome to our Education Select Committee oral evidence session. This morning we will hear from Secretary of State Lucy Powell in her first appearance in front of the Committee. Welcome, Secretary of State; we are glad to have you with us this morning. I understand you would like to make a short opening statement.

Lucy Powell: Yes, if that is okay. Thank you very much for having me. I am delighted to be here. As a former member of this Committee—some of my best years in Parliament—I have huge respect for you as the Chair and for every member of the Committee, the work you do and the reports you produce. I know what work goes into those reports, and I have studied them closely.

I am delighted to be the Education Secretary. I pay tribute to my predecessor, Bridget Phillipson, who did a huge amount of work on child poverty and the early years. We saw some of the results of that recently with a good level of development improvements in pre-school.

I have been really clear about my priorities, having been in and around education for a while. We have to look to the future. We have a duty to our children and young people, in this fast-changing world, to make sure they are equipped with the skills, knowledge and know-how to navigate the world they are going into, not the world of the past. That is what we have been focused on. I am really lucky to have an exceptional team of Ministers who I know many of you have interrogated or questioned in the past. I look forward to working with you over the coming weeks.

Chair: Thank you very much. The Department for Education’s annual report and accounts were due to be published around about July and have still not been published. Can you explain to the Committee why they are so delayed and when you expect them to be published?

Lucy Powell: We are expecting them to be published very soon. Obviously they relate to a period before I was the Secretary of State. There are some ongoing discussions with the NAO and the audit team there. They have given us a few pointers to work through, which is what has been happening, but I think they are fairly imminent.

Chair: The Department is an outlier this year—I think historically it is an outlier. Do you know why there is such a significant delay this time?

Lucy Powell: I don’t think there is any cause for concern. As I say, I think there were just a few pointers that needed to be worked through. As far as I am aware—I have asked those questions—there is nothing to be concerned about and they will be published imminently.

Chair: We were surprised not to see them published before the Budget, given the delay since July.

Lucy Powell: Okay. I hear that. I will make sure that is fed back and that that does not happen in future.

Chair: Thank you very much. The Government have a big focus on technical and vocational education. We agree with that focus—it was very much reflected in the Committee’s report last year on further education and skills. You have announced a new technical pathway for children post 14 to be delivered, in order to put technical education on an equal footing with academic study. There have been previous efforts to place technical education on an equal footing with academic education; for various reasons we have never quite succeeded in doing that in this country. What are you doing to make sure that this time it is going to be different?

Lucy Powell: You are absolutely right. Thank you for the report and the work you have done in this area in the past, which has really helped to shape a lot of what we are doing post 16. As you know, that revolution is already well under way. Post 16 we have the new V-levels starting next year. I think I first did an inquiry on T-levels when I was sitting on the other side of the table quite a few years ago. They are now properly embedded, and they are really quite a desirable qualification as well, for all those years. The same is true of degree apprenticeships, which are in huge demand. When the qualifications are designed well, have quality underpinning them and really do lead to great outcomes in work, we are seeing more and more young people and their families choosing them as an option. That is now what we want to bring into the key stage 4 phase as well

We will be bringing in some new vocational GCSEs, which will be underpinned by national standards and overseen by Ofqual. That will be unlike the current suite of tech awards. As you know, nearly half of all children take at least one tech award at the age of 16 already. This is about raising the quality and standards in those new GCSEs, but I am also looking at the current suite of GCSEs to make sure that practical subjects like drama, music and computing, as well as tech GCSEs, have a lot more practical assessment in them and are fit for purpose. It is not just about doing that on its own; it is part of that wider piece. We want to make sure that the new qualifications at 16 really relate to the qualifications that we now have available post 16 and beyond, so that the pathways are a lot clearer for young people and their families.

Chair: In your own mind, what is the measure of success of these reforms? Is it a shift in the range of subjects and the proportion of children taking those subjects, or is it about raising the game for the quality of qualifications for children who are already pursuing vocational pathways in education? Or is it a mixture of the two? What proportion of children taking the new vocational GCSEs would you regard as a good level of success for the reforms?

Lucy Powell: Good question. It is about quality and outcomes, and it is also about them becoming desirable, so that my kids or your kids will want to take them. This is about offering a blend for young people—not a distinct pathway, one or the other, but a broader range.

Currently, if you look at the tech awards—as I say, about 45% of young people took a tech award this summer—a lot of those are actually BTECs, like PE or drama, where the GCSE isn’t fit for purpose. Once we reform the GCSEs and the new GCSEs come in, we might see a dip in tech awards, so it will not necessarily be about a quantum, but about quality and outcomes, and this being an offer that young people are choosing to take, to complement their academic study.

Chair: The Committee visited a school that we talk about a lot, Aylsham high school in Norfolk, which offers construction skills as part of its curriculum, and what we saw there was very impressive. You are talking about a construction GCSE, but we saw at Aylsham what you need to deliver construction on site: a big construction workshop, which most schools do not have at the moment and which requires quite a lot of space to accommodate it. What is your view on the capital investment and the skills in the teaching profession that schools will need to offer these new technical qualifications? Do you envisage a role for FE colleges in educating children from the age of 14, which would be a significant departure for them?

Lucy Powell: I do. I visited a similar school, Harper Green in Bolton, which has an on-site construction hub, where they have seen huge demand, far outstripping the supply of places. That is obviously at one end of the spectrum—offering a construction qualification, which requires the space and the capacity to learn how to build a brick wall, and so on. Schools are already doing that, as you say, where there is local demand and demand from students to do that, so they are finding the capacity and the space to do that.

We will work with schools on delivering the qualifications and how that will work, but a key part of it is about working with the mayoral authorities, where there is one, to link up with local employers and, as you say, FE colleges, which are quite keen to help by using their facilities. I went to another school near Preston recently where students do a day a week in a college on these kinds of courses. Those partnerships have evolved and grown organically, and we want to put some rocket boosters under that.

You are absolutely right that there is then the workforce issue as well. We are putting extra investment into recruiting, for example, design and technology teachers in the coming years, because there are transferable skills for some of those technical kinds of qualifications. One of the first vocational GCSEs will be in finance and accounting, and in digital tech, which will be a branch of the computer science department in a school. There is a whole range—it is not just the hard-hat qualifications, if you like.

Chair: What are the implications of the increasing use of AI for the assessment of technical qualifications? How will you respond to that in the design of the new qualifications?

Lucy Powell: AI is a big issue right across the piece in education. It is particularly prevalent in higher education as a tool for dissertations, research and so on. We are working very closely with Ofqual, of course, which is very keen to make qualifications high-quality and high-esteem, and to do that you have to have credibility in the awarding of them, which is a crucial part of Ofqual’s job. Some of it will be about assessment—there are other ways in which we will do that—but Ofqual is really clear, as am I, that marking and the awarding of qualifications really should be done by the human eye, because otherwise you can exacerbate prejudice and other issues and in awarding them. Alongside having more practical assessment in practical subjects, we will be raising the game in terms of the teacher assessment piece to make sure there is real rigour in the system.

Caroline Voaden: Good morning, Secretary of State. I would like to talk about the devolution of funding for 16 to 19-year-olds. The Government have announced plans to transfer responsibility for 16-to-19 education funding to mayoral strategic authorities, but some areas do not have a mayor, and some areas, like mine, are only at the very beginning of the journey to becoming a mayoral strategic authority, which is going to take some time. Some areas do not have many post-16 further education colleges at all, and young people are travelling huge distances. How will you ensure that greater local control does not create a postcode lottery in access to high-quality education and training?

Lucy Powell: Your question gets absolutely to the nub of it. How do we ensure local delivery, local best practice and the joining up of provision with employers and labour markets—which is where we can really get some game-changing results, especially where that is joined up with back to work schemes, DWP schemes and so on, which the mayors will take hold of over the coming years—while making sure it is all underpinned by national expectations, nationally agreed outcomes and nationally agreed qualifications and pathways?

Every young person, no matter where they live, should have a guarantee of a college or post-16 place to do what they want to do. This will not happen in one big bang; it will be something that we work through. I recently had a summit at No. 10 North with the mayors, loads of business organisations, and school and college leaders from places that do not have a mayor, and we were talking through and working through some of these issues so that we can get that right.

There will be some trailblazing areas that are more ready to go, and that want to join things up more in a holistic way with, as I say, their back to work programmes. In other places, it will be about supporting local areas to influence and shape the provision in their area in the meantime. That might be more akin to your area. It is not about creating a postcode lottery; in fact, I think it gives us a fresh opportunity. As the Committee will know from its report, there is not really that kind of universal offer in post-16 at the moment. How do we set out some of the national expectations and outcomes for what our young people should get between 16 and 19? The Milburn report that is coming soon has some new ideas about that as well.

Caroline Voaden: The vision sounds good; are you confident that there will be a national accountability mechanism if mayoral strategic authorities are not doing a very job, areas are falling behind and young people are not getting the opportunities that they could or should be getting?

Lucy Powell: That is what we will wire in, and that is what we are working through at the moment. We have a White Paper on devolution and rewiring the state coming later this year. We are working across Government and with local leaders, mayors and others on how we get that right. You will know from your inquiry that it is currently a very patchwork, fragmented system. That also makes it hard to fully devolve. This gives us an opportunity to be really clear what the offer for young people is and what they can expect, and we will absolutely make sure that there are clear lines of accountability that sit alongside that for local areas but also, beneath that, for providers.

Caroline Voaden: Is there going to be any increase in funding for post-16? We know that further education colleges are desperately underfunded. Teachers are lower paid than they are in the school workforce, and it is really hard to recruit the staff needed to provide the vocational and technical courses that we all want to see more of. Will there be an uplift in funding to further education?

Lucy Powell: I completely agree with you. We will see this in the Milburn review, which is due out very soon. He has looked at the international comparisons, and countries that invest heavily in their equivalent of further education or technical education have a far lower number of NEETs, so to speak, in their systems. We have to learn those lessons. For further education, there are additional pressures because the bulge in the birth rate is now coming through into post-16.

There are funding questions, but there are also some measures questions in respect of what we are asking of further education. I have been working closely with college leaders, the AoC, sixth-form colleges and others about what we can do to lower some of the barriers that stop them doing what they want to do well, and about how they are measured and how their funding is distributed. One of the things we have talked about is this endless cycle of resits in maths and English, which is not delivering good outcomes for those young people at all and is putting real strain on post-16. It is not just a funding issue, but I very strongly hear what you are saying about that.

Chair: On further education colleges and funding, you know that the Committee has strong views about VAT and the current uneven playing field for further education colleges compared with schools. We have read your correspondence with us on the matter, and correspondence from the Treasury, and it is our view that the Government are approaching this from the wrong direction. The Government are saying, “Well, the rules are drafted in this way, and further education colleges fall outside the rules.” We think that, when there is such an imbalance for parts of the state education system that are often delivering the same qualifications, and they face such an inequality with regard to taxation, the Government should be working to change the rules, not simply telling us that the rules say it cannot be done. What representations are you making to the Treasury that the rules on VAT, as applied to further education colleges at the moment, are really not justifiable and that it needs to work with you to find a way to change them?

Lucy Powell: Obviously, VAT and taxation are matters for the Treasury, as you know. I have ongoing, regular dialogue with the Treasury about a whole range of issues. You can rest assured that I make my representations very strongly.

Manuela Perteghella: Good morning, Secretary of State. I would like to talk about funding for higher education. I was a university lecturer for many years, so this is something quite close to my heart. The Government have repeatedly said that they have taken steps to put higher education finances on a more stable footing, yet universities are continuing to make redundancies among staff and are closing modules and courses. Are you happy with the approach that universities are taking?

Lucy Powell: Thanks for raising that; it is a really important issue. As you know, universities are autonomous organisations that have their teaching funded by tuition fees, whether from domestic or international students, and then they receive research grants. I keep a close eye on the financial situation of every single university, and it certainly isn’t a uniform picture. It is a very un-uniform picture, and it is not necessarily the institutions you would expect that are in a more precarious position than others. Part of that has to come down to how some institutions have been led and managed over a long period of time—not just in relation to Government policy.

As you know, one of the recent pressures on universities has been the sharp decline in international students. We are working with the sector to help with that. You will have seen in the Budget last week that we announced that the graduate visa will now be extended to two years for, I am hoping, most of our universities. We think that will help in that regard. We are also working with universities to make sure that tuition fees keep in line with inflation to help to meet their costs.

It is very distressing for students and staff when universities go through a closing of departments and a big restructuring—obviously there are a few high-profile examples at the moment. It is not something I want to see, and I obviously speak to the trade union groups about it as well. We are playing our part in making sure that the overall framework and policies that impact on universities mean they can be viable but, as I say, it is not a uniform picture.

Manuela Perteghella: In its report on higher education and funding, the Committee recommended an early warning system and a special regime to manage insolvency. At what point and in what ways will the Government intervene to prevent a disorderly closure by a university?

Lucy Powell: These are challenging questions. As I say, universities are autonomous. They are charities. Lincoln Bishop was recently in this situation. The Government try to ensure that there is continuous study. There is a real role for universities to play in their place, as part of the Prime Minister and the Government’s agenda to create growth in every postcode and have a whole new strategic approach to place—the Prime Minister and many others call it the triple helix. There is a real role to be played there. We do not want to see institutions closing. We have some powers and some capacity in that regard, and we will do our best to help to broker those situations where they arise. I did read the Committee’s report on that.

Manuela Perteghella: You mentioned the impact of the decline in the number of international students; how confident are you that the Home Office has the same commitment to the international education strategy as your Department? Shouldn’t the Home Office be made a co-owner of that strategy, since it has oversight of student visa rules, for example?

Lucy Powell: We work very closely with the Home Office on these issues, and there is a joint approach to that. I have spoken to the Office for Students, Universities UK and others recently about this. There is an onus to make sure that we weed out some of those—to say they are abusing the system would perhaps take it a bit too far—where we are seeing high numbers of international students with low prior attainment and low levels of English coming in, especially through the franchising model.

Collectively, we have a duty to make sure that the Home Office, as well as everybody else, has confidence in the student visa route in this country, so that we can do what we are great at: we have fantastic universities that people around the world want to come and study in. It is one of the things we are best known for around the world. It brings great advantages to this country, as well as to the students themselves. We have a job to do, to give the Home Office confidence in the wider system, but we do work incredibly closely with it.

Manuela Perteghella: So you are confident in the Home Office in relation to international students.

Lucy Powell: The Home Office has its agenda, and we have our agenda, but I think most of that meets in the middle, to be honest with you. We all have to give the Home Office the confidence that the student visa route is not being used in a way that it should not be and that everyone has confidence in that. The vast majority of our brilliant universities, which are known around the world, do meet their requirements, and we need to ensure that is the case. As you probably know, we are looking at how we can bring in some requirements on English language for students to get student loans as well.

Sureena Brackenridge: Secretary of State, you have made it clear that you would like to see further changes to Ofsted inspections, particularly to ensure that the context in which a school operates is truly recognised. Teaching unions have raised concerns about the “secure fit” grading approach and whether it allows sufficient professional judgment. We have heard that schools in deprived areas and those with higher proportions of disadvantaged students and SEND students are more likely to receive harsher grades in some of the criteria. We have also heard concerns about inconsistency and extreme workloads for staff affecting their wellbeing. What are the next steps for the Government’s reform of Ofsted, and how would you like to see the inspections change?

Lucy Powell: You absolutely hit the nail on the head there. That is why I have been talking about this in relation to Ofsted. It is not any particular person’s fault; it is just sometimes how these things come out of the wash, if you like. What has happened under the new framework, with “secure fit” and the way that it is being judged, is that some schools in our most challenging areas or with high numbers of children with SEND, high rates of disadvantage and high rates of low prior attainment are being disadvantaged.

Where there is really good progress and good practice and schools are doing their best for the children, we want to see that being recognised by Ofsted. That is why I have said publicly and privately that I want to see context and inclusion being recognised in a different way by Ofsted under a reformed framework. I am having ongoing conversations with Ofsted, which is working very closely with me on how we can do that, so that we can get trust back into the system. Accountability is a really important part of the system. Ofsted’s regime has over time helped schools to improve, but we want to get to a system that is more peer-to-peer and self-improving, with the Ofsted badge of judgment on it. That is why we are working closely with Ofsted, but there are also changes that we need to make in the Department for the consequences of Ofsted.

It is not, as some have tried to say back to me, that context is an excuse—far from it. As you rightly say, what has actually happened is that context can be a penalty, rather than what it should be, which is a professional judgment understanding what a school is trying to do. The idea behind the framework originally was to create more consistency. I think the balance between creating some consistency in judgments versus it being entirely data-driven and not having the inspector judgment on it is not right at the moment. That is what we need to get right. I will of course let the Committee know as soon as we are in a position to come forward with some new proposals on that.

Sureena Brackenridge: I appreciate that you are not able to divulge the details of the conversations you have been having with Ofsted, but I have to return to the “secure fit” grading. Is that on the table? Are you addressing that?

Lucy Powell: All these things are in the mix. You are right. What I hear from heads, multi-academy trust leaders and others, as well as trade union leaders, is that “secure fit”, as designed, is leading to an entirely data-driven approach or a sense that that is the case—that before an inspector even comes into a school, there is a limit to what that school can achieve because of the data and “secure fit”. This is complex, and there are other factors within it, but you are absolutely right that we want to see, as I say, a much stronger balance between having more consistency in Ofsted judgments, so that everybody understands what is being looked at and how judgments are made, and professional judgment being applied by inspectors who really understand the school, the context and what is going on. That balance is not right at the moment, so you are definitely on to something, but I want us to be really clear about what the proposals are going to look like as a package when we have that all finalised—which won’t be too long.

Sureena Brackenridge: There has been a clear breakdown in confidence between the teaching profession and Ofsted, and that is very much driven by lessons not being learned from extreme stress and how it impacts on the wellbeing of school leaders and all the staff who work in the school. We have to keep in mind the tragic death of headteacher Ruth Perry. What assurances can you give that things truly are going to change and be done differently?

Lucy Powell: Again, you are absolutely right. Nothing is more to the fore of my mind than headteacher and teacher wellbeing, the tragic case of Ruth Perry, and all the other cases and feedback that we hear all the time. I have met Ruth Perry’s sister and discussed these issues, and I meet the trade unions, especially the headteacher unions, to discuss them regularly. We also need to make sure—this is what I am really focused on at the moment—that we do not just change the balance and the dial of what is being measured, but look at the culture, how we can make sure that is delivered over time and what accountability loops we can put in place to ensure that Ofsted as an organisation is really delivering that. One of the things that we will be judging that on is the rebuilding of trust with headteachers and the sector. I have been clear about that.

Sureena Brackenridge: Thank you. Moving on, when will the Government publish their legislation on reforming the system to support children with SEND? I ask because yesterday I saw reports that further delays are likely.

Lucy Powell: The first thing I would say about timing is that we are taking the time that this needs. This is a once-in-a-generation opportunity to reform a system that really is not working for parents or children. You have done all that detail work. When I was on this Committee, we definitely did a big report on SEND, and that was a good eight or so years ago. You have also done one.

This is something that has dogged successive Governments. We are, I think, doing this in the right way—working with the sector, working with parents, working with schools, working with you, working with parliamentarians and working with others to really get these reforms right, so that we get buy-in, people are happy that they are going to be effective, and we can get that early help in sooner. On day one, when I came in as Secretary of State, I said that the worst thing we could do in this regard would be to produce a piece of legislation that is not quite right and that people do not have trust in from the day it is published. However it long it takes to get that right is what we will take.

At the moment we are looking to publish the Bill very early in the new year, but I can tell the Committee today that, alongside the Bill, we will publish a full policy response to the consultation. It was a huge consultation process; we are still working through those representations and issues. Alongside the Bill and that policy response, we will publish the national inclusion standards and the specialist provision packages so that parents, schools, local authorities and everybody else can see the standards that are expected and what support they can expect to get for their child. That is why we want to take the time to produce it all together.

Darren Paffey: Good morning, Secretary of State. To continue the point about trust and confidence, your predecessor as Secretary of State recognised that the key to the success of the reforms, as with any reform where there is lots of unease, is to gain the trust and the confidence of everyone. You have already recognised that. Can you say more about the approach you have taken with stakeholders, since you took on the role, to ensure that that trust is there, particularly given the threat to that trust and confidence from any delays? As you know, that will always damage people’s trust.

Lucy Powell: Timeframes are our own expectations, so I do not think “delay” is the right word. We are working very closely, night and day, with our trusted partners, with parent groups, with our expert panel and with others to get it all right. Just producing the Bill on its own, without the context of the understanding of the specialist support packages and the national inclusion standards, and without a proper policy response to the consultation, would be a mistake. That will help to build trust because everyone will have all the answers they need, rather than being left with questions, which would be the worst case there.

You will know—you have met her—that we are all very lucky to have Minister Georgia Gould leading this work for us. Just this week I joined a trusted panel of advisers who have been working very closely with her. Every single one of them had huge praise for the work and the energy and detailed knowledge she had brought to it. We have some great officials in the Department and really good people who represent parent groups, local authorities, schools, and so on, working closely with us to get the reforms right.

When it comes to trust, the other really important thing to say is that change is happening already. People are already starting to see some of those changes in terms of the experts at hand and the huge recruitment we are doing there to get specialist support into more schools, along with early help, support for teachers and the extra 66,000 specialist places that we are creating through a big capital programme. We are also rolling out the teacher training work. We are already doing a whole range of things; we are not waiting for the Bill itself. That is critical to building trust.

The other thing is that, for me, the SEND reforms sit in the wider piece of education reform that I am embarking on. I want to see no child left behind; every child really does matter. The view of education that we have had for the last 15 years—a narrow view of what good is, with a one-size-fits-all model—has left too many feeling that they are outside the school system and that the school system does not suit or serve their child’s needs. The SEND reforms are part of that process, as are the curriculum reforms that we are embarking on with the new qualifications—technical, vocational and creative—and the changes that we are making to Ofsted. This is all part of a bigger package, so that more parents can have confidence that the school system will suit their child, whatever their needs.

Darren Paffey: Are you confident that that approach, the team, the measures and the early implementation of some of those things will be enough to ensure the buy-in from all the stakeholders, or do you recognise things that we need to do more on, be that x, y or z?

Lucy Powell: Yes, we are getting that buy-in. One of the areas the Committee has talked about a lot—it was in your report, and we have heard about it in Parliament—is making sure of this. I have had direct conversations with my counterpart, Yvette Cooper—other Ministers have as well, and other officials—because obviously this is a joint endeavour with the Department of Health. Parents want to be sure that the Health Department is fully on board, which it is, and that some of the accountability that is needed will be in the system too. We are working through those issues at the moment.

Mark Sewards: Thank you very much, Secretary of State—

Lucy Powell: Lucy, please.

Mark Sewards: Let’s go with Lucy then. One of the complaints about the SEND reforms has revolved around individual support plans—what parents can do if they have complaints about ISPs. At the moment, what should the right of appeal be if a parent has a complaint about an individual support plan? If it is taken to mediation, but it is not resolved in mediation with the school, what should their right of appeal be?

Lucy Powell: Obviously, that came through in the consultation as well, and that is the nub of what we are working through at the moment and what we will set out in the Bill and in the wider proposals. Accountability and recourse are a really important part of the system. We need to get that balance right. That means more support earlier on, more help and not needing to have diagnoses in order to get the support that the child evidently needs.

We are already seeing some good results. Rochdale is piloting this at the moment, and we have seen an 80% reduction in speech and language referrals, because the help was in Rochdale schools earlier on. That is the model that we are trying to drive to. That is why it is so important to get the early help and support in first. Essentially, we will run both systems at once before the reforms come in, to give everyone confidence that this model really can deliver better outcomes for children and families and be much less adversarial and challenging than it is now. You are right that that is a core issue.

Mark Sewards: That is what parents in Leeds South West and Morley come to me with, especially those who have children with SEND. From your answer today, can they take it that that is being looked at?

Lucy Powell: That is obviously a key part of what we are looking at, to make sure that those mechanisms are in place. The idea is—I think the early evidence shows this—that far fewer families will end up needing to be in that position, because there would be clarity not just about their individual support package, but in the specialist provision packages in their area and what they can expect to get, depending on need. We will publish all that. Of course, EHCPs will still be in place as well to give that.

This is about full transparency. We are working with local authorities, which are producing their SEND plans. Once they have produced those SEND plans to a quality that we expect—we are also working with them to reduce their deficits, which is an ongoing process—we want to make sure that the health boards, the ICBs, are also playing their part in that provision in the locality. There has to be some grit in the oyster to make sure that these things happen, but we are making considerable progress in having those things in place for schools and local areas long before the Bill has been published.

Mark Sewards: That all makes sense. For what it’s worth, in the roundtables that I have held in Leeds South West and Morley, parents have welcomed early intervention because it resolves problems that come up later on. I suppose, though, that you will still have a number of parents who will not be happy with the individual support plans and then will not resolve it through mediation. Is there a danger that those with the means to do so might go to the High Court and seek judicial review if there is no right of appeal?

We know that hunger does not stop at the end of a school term. In fact, the reality is that, with the challenges around disadvantage, it can be exacerbated during school holidays. What consideration is the Department giving to making all children on free school meals eligible for the HAF programme?

Lucy Powell: You are right to say that the expansion of free school meals this year—the biggest expansion in a generation—to include households that are just above the threshold and in receipt of universal credit is a massive boost for those families. It is a huge cost saving and, alongside our new food standards that we published earlier this year, it will ensure that children are able to concentrate and have better behaviour, because they have a full, healthy meal every day.

It has been a huge, significant increase to the budget for free school meals. If we had passported everything across, that would have significantly increased the costs. We felt it was important to give those families on the borderline that extra support and, critically, to make sure that those children had a healthy, full, hot meal in the day. We will always keep these things under review, but they do come with significant costs when we then start passporting everything else across.

Sureena Brackenridge: I understand the significant cost, but there is significant value as well. When we talk about the educational attainment gap between disadvantaged young people and their peers, we have to look at the school holidays as well.

Lucy Powell: I hear that.

Dr Johnson: Secretary of State, if I heard you right earlier, you said that you had exceeded your target of reducing the content of the curriculum.

Lucy Powell indicated assent.

Dr Johnson: You are nodding—that’s right. How is that a good thing? It is reducing aspiration, is it not? What about the more capable student or our competition in the world market for graduates? Why do you think it is a good idea to reduce the content?

Lucy Powell: It is still very hard. One of the big findings from the Becky Francis curriculum review, which I know you have looked at as a Committee, was that the current curriculum had too much content, and teachers were having to rush through lots of content, rather than allowing time for deep understanding of important topics, whether that is in maths, English, history or elsewhere, because it was an overcrowded curriculum.

It is very hard to do a review of the curriculum and get that balance right, because there will of course be things that people want to keep in. The approach we have taken is that this is about creating deeper understanding and time to really look at these things. It is certainly not a case of dumbing down in any way, shape or form. This is about creating time to have proper, deeper understanding, but also creating time across the curriculum from five to 19 for children to do more reading for pleasure and other things that are foundational and very important.

Caroline Voaden: Prior to you coming into post, your Department cut the adoption and special guardianship support fund, so that each child receives £3,000 per year instead of £5,000. The £2,500 allowance for assessment was removed, and the match funding for the highest-need children was stopped. These are some of the most vulnerable children in society. I have heard from families, providers and psychologists that this has had a devastating impact on the support they can give adopted children—either the support cannot last a full year, so there are breaks in support, which means different providers and breaks in continuity, or they just cannot afford the support they need. Is your Department doing a proper review of the real-world impact of these cuts? Can you give any indication of whether you will be restoring that funding for these vulnerable children?

Lucy Powell: When I was Leader of the House, this issue came up—it got raised with me several times in business questions for many months—so I know the strength of feeling about it. As you know, the Government have committed £55 million to the fund, and it has had a 10% increase. We will continue that fund next year and beyond, and there is a new funding pot for adoption. I will make sure we continue to look at these issues and make sure we are supporting those who take that step to adopt a child or more than one child.

Caroline Voaden: This support has a lifelong impact on these children.

Lucy Powell: Absolutely, and obviously, parents who choose to adopt are doing an amazing public duty—they really are—for all of us and for those young people and children.

Chair: I have a final question. Earlier, in our discussion about SEND, you said that the deficit changes for local authorities for producing their SEND reform plans are fully funded in the coming years, but the OBR’s economic and fiscal outlook says that while the Government have committed to reimbursing SEND spending deficits to the end of 2025-26, there is no commitment about deficits accumulated after that point.

Lucy Powell: I think I said the coming year, but we will take an equally proportionate approach to next year’s conversations as well.

Chair: Are you aiming in the SEND reforms that will be published to get to a situation of financial sustainability for local authorities, so that they are not continually in this cycle of accumulating deficits on SEND because the need exceeds their ability to pay for it within their current budgets?

Lucy Powell: We are absolutely trying to get to a sustainable system that works well for children and families, is deliverable and is getting those outcomes that we all want to see. That is not just for local authorities, but for Health and other Departments.

Chair: Will the statutory override arrangements be reinstated beyond 2025-26?

Lucy Powell: I will let you know about that, but we are going to continue to take the same approach to those issues as we have taken this year.

Chair: Thank you very much for answering our questions this morning, and we look forward to seeing you for future sessions.

Lucy Powell: Absolutely, I look forward to it.
