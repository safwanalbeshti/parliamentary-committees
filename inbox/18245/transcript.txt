Chair: Thank you very much. Dr Luke, could you introduce yourself and your work?

Dr Moore: Good afternoon. My name is Luke Moore. I am a consultant medic here in London at the Chelsea and Westminster hospital. In my spare time, I am an academic at Imperial College London, where I focus on antibiotic resistance and how we , as a global community, can address that. I am a member of various learned societies, and editor at the Journal of Hospital Infection, where we focus on how, as a globe, we can combat antibiotic resistance.

Chair: You are both very busy and very valuable people, so thank you for making the time today.

Elena, could you give us an overview of the current healthcare situation in Ukraine? Then I will ask Dr Luke to go into the antimicrobials. So just the general health situation, please.

Dr Moshynets: Let me try to guide you through the problems we have now with medical system care in Ukraine. I should start with the fact that Ukraine’s healthcare system is in a stage of reformation. This reform was launched in 2022, and its aim is to bring healthcare financing and service delivery closer to western standards. However, it began in the same year as Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine and continued despite the unprecedented challenges caused by the war. It is without adequate adaptation to the new realities or sufficient consideration of the rapidly deteriorating healthcare infrastructures, financing constraints and changing medical needs.

As a result, the healthcare system is expected to implement extensive structural and regulatory changes under wartime conditions without sufficient financial resources, institutional preparation or workflow development. In particular, Ukraine lacks a comprehensive system of national clinical guidelines for antimicrobial treatment, a sufficient number of trained specialists in antimicrobial resistance and infection prevention and control, and dedicated hospital funding to implement the new requirements.

I would say that infection prevention and control is particularly difficult to implement in those circumstances. Low salaries, staff shortages and extensive workloads have a severe influence and have resulted in the problems that we have now with infection prevention and control requirements. In some hospitals, one nurse is responsible for 40 to 60 beds, making consistent implementation of basic infection prevention measures extremely difficult.

I should also say that the further stage of healthcare reform has resulted in a consolidation of healthcare facilities, hospital mergers and the closure of additional institutions and hospitals that are unable to provide the expanded range of services required under the new system. Regional hospitals are particularly in trouble because they are smaller and cannot provide the full range of services. That is why they are considered for closure.

This approach gives rise to serious concerns in the context of healthcare-associated infections, and AMR specifically, because concentrating large numbers of patients in fewer hospitals increases the pressures on infrastructure, healthcare personnel and infection control systems, potentially facilitating the transmission of healthcare-associated infections. At a time when Ukraine faces a substantial burden of infection caused by multi-drug resistant bacteria, healthcare planning should prioritise improving hospital capacity, recruiting and appropriate patient-to-staff ratios, rather than further concentrating patient populations in a smaller number of hospitals.

Another problem we have now is access to publicly funded medical care. This access is deteriorating, particularly outside major urban cities. Hospitals face a shortage of antibiotics and other essential medicines, while regional and local authorities, whose budgets have been severely depleted by the war, are increasingly unable to compensate for those gaps in national healthcare financing. Consequently, regional healthcare provision is deteriorating faster than in major cities, creating growing inequalities in access to medical services and effective treatment.

This problem is specifically important in regions where war activities are happening. It also includes community-acquired infections, such as leptospirosis, Lyme disease, pyelonephritis and pneumonia. The further damage of energy infrastructures and other problems related to war just make the situation even worse because people cannot get access to specialised medical treatment.

Another problem I would like to mention is that treating infections caused by highly resistant bacteria is becoming increasingly difficult due to the limited availability of last-resort antibiotics. Some essential antimicrobial agents are either not registered or are unavailable on the Ukrainian pharmaceutical market, while others cannot be procured by hospitals because of insufficient funding. Consequently, even when microbiological diagnostics identify the appropriate treatment, clinicians may have no practical means to provide it. This creates a dangerous gap between identifying effective antibiotics and actual delivery of those antibiotics to the patient.

This is a very general overview. I just mentioned what is most important on my side, but Luke may be able to add some comments.

Chair: We will go into some of the details and issues that you raise.

Tracy Gilbert: Dr Luke, could you help us understand what antimicrobial resistance is? Elena touched on some of the reasons why it is a specific issue in Ukraine. Can you expand a little bit on that, please?

Dr Moore: Thank you very much, Ms Gilbert. Right now, there are more bacteria than there have ever been humans on the face of planet Earth. We reproduce, what, every 20 years? We teach our young. Our young are better than us. Bacteria do the same but, rather than having a generation every 20 years, they have a generation every 20 minutes. That means they have the ability to learn and adapt to everything that we are doing as humans in our human civilisation, including developing new antibiotics, developing new surgery and giving people chemotherapy for their cancer.

People often think about antibiotic resistance as being an issue for just an infection. You have a urinary tract infection, “Whoops, I cannot now give you a tablet antibiotic. I need to now bring you into hospital to treat.” Sure, that is part of it, but actually if you need a metal hip and you need surgery, if you need chemotherapy or if you have diabetes and get a manky foot, I cannot now manage that as effectively as I could have 20 or 30 years ago.

Just coming back to that time issue, the amount of money and time it takes to develop a new antibiotic I think is replete in the media—20 years, $2 billion. Yet, as I alluded to, these bacteria are reproducing every 20 minutes, and there are so many of them on each of us.

If we, as human healthcare, are trying to enable longevity, and enable health rather than ill health, we need to address how we enable the rest of healthcare to be undertaken. We need to support that with antibiotics. That is the status quo in a reasonably resource-replete healthcare organisation, which is national and is not in the midst of conflict and catastrophe.

Add into that conflict and catastrophe; huge internal or external displacement of hundreds of thousands of citizens; dissolution of the infrastructure where we actually provide healthcare—the organisations, the buildings in which we do it; and removal of healthcare providers, through casualties or displacement to other countries, and suddenly you take a severe issue for the whole of healthcare and compound it.

What we are facing now in areas of conflict and catastrophe such as Ukraine is an inability to treat infections with any antibiotic. We have increased spread of those resistant bacteria from citizens who have an injury because of the conflict to citizens in beds next to them, who may have come in for a pacemaker or a hip replacement. That exponential increase in the problem of antibiotic resistance undermines all of healthcare provision in Ukraine.

What we have then seen in the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Poland and Germany is movement of citizens from conflict areas such as Ukraine to our healthcare organisations, again with an exponential increase in the dissemination of those resistant bugs. We kind of understand borders; bacteria don’t.

David Mundell: Luke, can I ask how you, Elena and others are collaborating to meet the challenge of antimicrobial resistance that you have just set out?

Dr Moore: Elena and her team, and our disparate team here in the UK, have been collaborating for several years, most recently with financial support from UK Defence Innovation, which has given us an ability to form a formal network looking at conflict wound infections. That includes universities in the UK such as Birmingham, Imperial College and Strathclyde, and Great Ormond Street hospital, as well as Elena and the National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine. We have been building a network of excellence—as much as we can all be excellent together, but also, much more functionally than that—to look at the types of bugs, the types of drugs that are still effective, and how we can deploy diagnostics to improve in situ diagnosis of resistant infections as close to the frontline as we can. I hope, Elena, that that is of use on the shop floor, but it also provides us here in the UK with resilience, should we need it further down the line.

With that, we were able to roll forward to this UKDI grant. There are other pockets of UK money. I don’t know whether FCDO has any pockets of money to help us with Ukraine antibiotic resistance issues.

David Mundell: Do you find that the UK Defence Innovation fund is proactively supportive of this work?

Dr Moore: Unequivocally, yes, they are supportive.

David Mundell: We are very interested in the “transfer” of what previously were perhaps ODA funds into defence-related matters.

Dr Moore: Yes.

Chair: That is good to hear. Thank you.

Tulip Siddiq: Thank you very much for coming to speak to us. Elena, how easily available are antibiotics in Ukraine? The reason I am asking is that I want to know about the impact that that has on the prevalence of antibiotic-resistant disease.

Dr Moshynets: This is a very complicated story, and it has a few problems associated with it. The major problem now in Ukraine is the growing gap between the increasingly complex regulatory framework established by the Ministry of Health and the actual capacity of hospitals to comply with those standards. The healthcare facilities are expected to meet extensive clinical administrative requirements, but often lack the financial and material resources to do that. In practice, responsibility for providing appropriate treatment is increasingly transferred from Government institutions to hospitals and individual clinicians, even when the necessary medicines are simply unavailable.

This is really creating an extremely difficult ethical and practical clinical dilemma for clinicians. They may have to prescribe antibiotics that are available in the hospitals, even when those are not the most appropriate options for the treatment of this disease, or ask patients to purchase the required medicines themselves. The latter is particularly problematic because patients are officially promised the provision of free medical care. That means that doctors may be blamed for corruption when asking the patient to buy essential medicine. So that is really a problem—a psychological problem, even.

The consequences for antimicrobial treatment are rather serious because antibiotics may be selected according to what the hospital can afford or has in stock, rather than what microbiological susceptibility suggests should be used. In some cases, clinicians may even hesitate to request bacterial culturing and to provide the specific diagnostics, simply because they expect that they will not be able to treat the infections they may identify. In fact, sometimes they will miss the diagnostics because they expect a high resistance rate, which they cannot treat according to—I won't say “recommendations”, but let’s say the antibiogram. This creates a dangerous cycle, where inadequate funding and limited access to appropriate antibiotics compromise treatment, undermine antimicrobial stewardship and may accelerate the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance further.

The other problem related to this issue, which I would also like to mention, is the lack of a comprehensive, nationally co-ordinated and adequately implemented system of clinical guidelines for the treatment of infectious diseases in Ukraine, particularly multi-drug resistant infections. I should also mention that simply adopting recommendations developed by leading Western medical infectious disease societies is not sufficient in Ukraine, because those guidelines should provide not just an essential scientific foundation, but also treatment strategies that reflect Ukraine’s local resistance patterns, bacterial epidemiology diagnostics capacity and, of course, the actual availability and affordability of those antibiotics. That is crucially important.

Ukraine urgently needs evidence-based national treatment guidelines that are adapted to the epidemiological situation and healthcare resources. This is essential. Guidelines alone will not solve the problem; the Government must also ensure nationwide access to the required antibiotics, according to those guidelines. Decisions about which essential antibiotics should be available and publicly funded must be made at the national level. It should not be hospitals or just clinicians that develop the standard. There should be national expertise to offer protocols to clinicians in Ukraine, with all the recommendations being science based and practice based, but still rational in the reality of Ukraine. We cannot have national standards of care without national responsibility for ensuring access to the medicines that are required to meet those standards.

Chair: Thank you. Let me pause you there, because I think Dr Luke wants to come in as well.

Dr Moore: When I think of the antibiotic availability across the globe, including in Ukraine, there are issues with licensing and regulation. As Elena said, many of the reserve-line antimicrobials that we would use here are just not available in many countries, including in Ukraine.

Then you have issues with counterfeit and substandard medications, including because of failure of the supply chain—cold chain—and that is a particular issue in areas of conflict. So you have access to the antibiotic, but the antibiotic compound itself is degraded.

Then, as Elena said, you have issues with reimbursement strategies. We are very lucky here in that, at the point of care, nobody pays for medicine. That is not true in many areas of the world, including in Ukraine.

Then you have issues with abnormalities in the economics of a drug cost versus a diagnostic cost, meaning that, if you come to me with an infection, sometimes it is cheaper for me to prescribe you best-guess antibiotic 1, then 2, then 3, then 4, getting it wrong each time, contributing to antibiotic resistance each time, because that approach is cheaper than doing one diagnostic test where I give you the right drug at the first guess. Sorry, Elena, sorry to butt in there.

Dr Moshynets: Thank you so much for this clarification, can I just—

Chair: I think Tulip has another question.

Tulip Siddiq: You have touched on this already, but I want to ask about the implications for global health when there is a surge in disease from Ukraine. You did touch on this, but could you elaborate a bit on the implications for global health, please?.

Dr Moshynets: In Ukraine, many reserve antibiotics are definitely not registered. That is true. The other truth is that we have problems with those antibiotics that are not so expensive and not so innovative, let’s say. They are still unavailable because, as we mentioned, the pharmaceutical market is very small and vendors simply do not bring them to the Ukrainian market.

I have an example that explains how bad everything is in Ukraine. A few weeks ago, we had a new paper published by the Public Health Centre describing wound infections. They stated that almost every second war-related wound infection is a gram-negative multi-drug resistant infection. That means that, to treat those wounds, doctors need to prescribe reserve antibiotics. According to that paper, almost half of the antibiotics purchased in the medical system should be from this reserve group. In fact—I just specifically calculated this—based on the Ukrainian electronic procurement platform, which every hospital is obliged to use, in 2024 the total amount of reserve antibiotics purchased by hospitals was only 1.37%. This is mostly because of the price. This is a huge discrepancy between the cumulative resistance to hospital-acquired infections in Ukrainian hospitals and the antibiotics that are purchased and prescribed to treat those infections. That is the best way to illustrate the discrepancy between the reality and the affordability from the hospital side.

Tulip Siddiq: Anything to add, Dr Moore?

Dr Moore: Just zooming out a little bit to global health, in a reasonably resource-replete society, we focus a lot on non-communicable diseases—cardiovascular health and diabetes. In areas of the world where there are fewer resources, the weight of healthcare is still in communicable diseases. We are talking a lot about infections and antibiotic resistance, and that might not resonate so much here, but actually in areas of the world with fewer resources, infectious diseases still trump non-communicable diseases, and that is why this is so important.

Noah Law: Elena, could you please explain a bit more about the context of where we are now—the reforms that have taken place in the Ukrainian healthcare system and why those were necessary.

Dr Moshynets: Thank you for this question. I wish I knew the answer. It is true that I am not a fan of the previous system. There were a lot of problems with the previous system. I do not know the answer to the question of why the national medical system decided on this reform despite the war. I would postpone this. It looks like there was a strong recommendation not to stop the planned reform and to move it forward. This reform really changes everything, even the structure of the hospital, the availability of antibiotics, and the availability of the other medicines. Everything is absolutely different to what we had before.

The other question is why this reform was not planned for smaller regions and upscaled later if it was effective. I still do not know the answer. The real problem—observing the number of resistant strains, and really looking at the bigger problem with the medical system—is why this reform was not adjusted to the gaps we can observe now. That is the question. It looks like the reform was planned and there are no corrections being suggested to it.

What should I say? We understand now that there are a number of problems, and it looks like the Minister of Health is now trying to find a solution for a number of gaps. I recently went to a specific meeting dedicated to AMR in Ukraine, which was organised by the Robert Koch Institute and the Charité hospital in Berlin. It looks like a decision has been made to formulate a specific fund for reserve antibiotics, which could be donated by Ukraine’s partners and distributed through the hospitals. But that is the original system we used to have before the reforms. It looks like they have tried to compensate for the problems and come back to the initial state because, with the resources we have in Ukraine, this reform is absolutely impossible to implement.

It is not only about funds; it is also about specialists. The core idea of this reform is to introduce another system for infection control and prevention. That means every hospital must have specialists in infection control and AMR, and we simply do not have enough specialists in Ukraine. This reform starts without a financial background, and without the number of specialists that could be employed by the hospitals, so there are still many questions about this reform.

It is my personal opinion that the main problem is the availability of antibiotics and the cost of evidence-based treatment. That is the main problem now.

Noah Law: Are you saying that that payment dynamic has not really changed in recent years and that it is still exacerbating AMR?

Dr Moshynets: True. With this reform, it looks like the idea of distributing the funds through the hospitals has been inspired by the American system. The treatment of patients is covered by insurance companies. Our insurance is the state. But, in fact, instead of covering the whole cost of the patient’s treatment, the state decided to allocate a specific budget for every nosology—for every, let’s say, disease—but the cost does not correspond with the real expense of the treatment. That is why all those reserve antibiotics really cannot be covered by the budget. They are in there, but the money is simply not enough. That is the main problem. Now we are just balancing between the very limited resources for every disease and the real cost of those diseases.

There is nothing about the salary for the staff because, as I already mentioned, the shortage of nurses, clinicians and doctors has made a strong impact on the IPC—infection prevention control—situation. The fewer nurses we have, the less safe our hospitals are. I would say that there is a direct correlation between the salaries, availability of nurses and the quality of medical care.

Noah Law: What lessons do you think the WHO and its members could learn from the experience of some of these reforms that Ukraine has had?

Dr Moshynets: The reform was intended to bring Ukraine’s healthcare system up to the best western European standards. But, as I already mentioned, Ukraine was not prepared well for the system. I really support the idea of improving the healthcare system to a western standard, because that is much better than the system we used to have. But like I said, all these preparations should be done, and the transformation from the old standards to the current western European standards could potentially take 15 to 20 years. So it is not only about money; it is about time, and we didn’t have the time. This reform started to be implemented quite fast, and the requirements we have now do not match the dedicated funding, resources, personnel or anything, to say nothing about the war. Even without the war, it could be very difficult, because this reform really should take at least 20 years. So this is a problem.

I would like to briefly remind you that, first of all, there are established infection control teams. We need enough personnel to complete those teams, which we do not have. Another problem, which I should also mention, is something I have experienced myself. Part of this reform is to make prescription requirements for antibiotics stronger, to reduce the inappropriate use of antibiotics. That would work in western countries, but in the current Ukraine it is not adequately accompanied by measures to ensure access to medical consultations. On the one side, antibiotics are strictly restricted; on the other side, no medical assistance is available.

This is something that I saw myself during winter. There were blackouts and heating disruptions in Kyiv, and all those difficulties, and many people—specifically elderly people—were not able to visit the doctor. I have personally become aware of deaths in Kyiv reportedly associated with pneumonia. That is simply because patients are unable to visit doctors and get antibiotics that could treat the pneumonia. We do not have epidemiological data about that, but it is something that I saw myself and it is really very sad. Two years ago, those people could go to a chemist’s shop and buy antibiotics to support themselves. Like I said, that is not appropriate for western countries. In countries in need, such as Ukraine, perhaps priority should be given.

What should I suggest to the WHO, if I may of course? First, this healthcare reform cannot simply transfer western European standards into countries with very different financial, institutional and security conditions. Secondly, new standards must be accompanied by realistic funding, workforce development and the resources needed for implementation. That is super-important.

Of course, thirdly, reforms should be flexible. That is again very important to understand. Those reforms should be introduced progressively and tested before nationwide implementation. That again was not done here. The impact of those reforms must be continuously monitored, with the possibility to adjust when problems are noticed.

My final conclusion might be that international organisations, such as the WHO, must listen to healthcare professionals working on the ground in Ukraine. I do not observe that a lot. It looks like we have been told what to do, instead of us discussing what to do. Again, that is my personal opinion; I might be wrong. It is just my feeling, so maybe someone would not agree with me.

Chair: Thank you very much. Your personal opinion is valuable, and I think those points were well made.

Stepping back from specifically focusing on Ukraine and looking more globally, I think about 130 conflicts are happening around the world at the moment. I know that AMR infections and resistance are ever increasing. What would your advice be to the World Health Organisation when it comes to dealing with the consequences when you have mass casualties and AMR? But, also, how should countries around the world insulate themselves from the risks of AMR?

Dr Moore: Yes, insulate.

Chair: Sorry, a snappy question.

Dr Moore: I guess there are several different ways to think about that. Way back in 2015, which seems many moons ago now, the WHO launched its global action plan on antibiotic resistance. It said that all 200-ish member states should write their own national action plans. Fast-forward to now, and about 150 of the 200 have done that.

I have spent most of the last three years analysing the different 150-ish national action plans that are out there, trying to find what good looks like. I have learned several things from that. There is no good. There are different elements that work for different countries. Some countries, in their national action plans, appear to have lifted en masse things that have worked elsewhere, thinking that this would be a good idea, and have tried to plug those into their own. As Elena said, it does not work.

So the No. 1 piece of advice to WHO is, “Thank you for commissioning all 200-ish member states to write their own national action plans. Please now review that and have a think about how we can all learn from each other’s national action plans. That does not mean copy. That means learn from each other’s plans.” That is point No. 1.

Point No. 2 was that, in that analysis of how different people are trying to address antibiotic resistance, some have tried to run before they can walk. Again, that resonates with what Elena was saying. There is no golden answer to antibiotic resistance; the answer does not have to be, for any given country, an expensive, all-singing, all-dancing solution. There are some examples in different countries’ national action plans that are really low cost and have worked far better than some elements of our own national action plan to address specific areas of antibiotic resistance. What a shame that the WHO isn’t facilitating that learning between countries. That is point No. 2.

Point No. 3 is around resilience, technology and AI. Now, I am not clever enough to know anything about AI, but it seems to me that we have huge numbers of data streams in healthcare in general, and in civilisation in general, so we can use other things, that are perhaps better than human brains, to elicit answers from to feed forwards into antibiotic resistance. In certain areas of the world—such as us, such as much of North America—we have huge electronic healthcare records. Why are we not leveraging AI in that format to extract ways to improve non-communicable health? I know that certain elements of AI are doing that for diabetes and renal disease. That is lovely, but where is that for antibiotic resistance? If the WHO has flagged AMR as a global silent pandemic, where are we with leveraging technology to help with that?

In my research group, I have the most fabulous research fellow I have ever come across, who has travelled up mountains in Greenland that have never been climbed before, gone to Antarctica and shrunken technology to be able to diagnose pathogens and antibiotic resistance genes in areas of the world in a way that you would never think was possible. That is lovely, but where can we do that en masse? Where can we do that at scale? If there is a conflict in a cold area, or a catastrophe in a hot area or a remote area, how can we deploy diagnostics to improve infection management—and AMR, but infection management in general—in those remote areas?

That is a kind of a high-tech specific funding issue. As I said, there is no single answer, Ms Champion, but it ranges from policy all the way through to R&D. In the UK, we have expertise. We have leadership, with Sally Davies in the field of AMR, but we need to live up to that and work where we can to feed up to the WHO that we can help with this.

Chair: I really like that you say “antibiotic resistance”. It makes it a lot easier to understand. Secondly, what I think you are saying is that there is a role for the WHO to provide that global guidance that then becomes tailored at a country and situation-specific level, and you are not seeing that happen.

Dr Moore: Correct.

Alina Belobra: In general, this year, in comparison to previous years, is discussed within the humanitarian sector as an exacerbated protection crisis. Last year, we were talking about it, but this year we are seeing increased numbers of civilians who have been targeted with different weaponry—with longer-range missiles and longer-range drones. Basically, the monthly number of children killed this summer—this July—is the four-year highest we have faced in Ukraine. Chair: Tell me what it is like to be a woman with two children, both at school, whose husband is on the frontline? What does my day look like? How is the war impacting on my life?

Alina Belobra: The first change that you will observe is the change in the air raid alerts. We no longer call it resilience; we call it adaptation to the point where people do not react to the air raid alerts, because they last for 24 hours.

The underground facilities are becoming targets as well. If I was a mum with two children in a frontline area, presumably my kids would go either into an underground facility to study or stay with me at home, trying to study online. We hear different concerns about both approaches.

On top of that, the situation would also limit my economic income. My livelihood would depend on how much I earn while my husband, in the scenario you gave me, is on the frontline and is conscripted. The healthcare and the caregiving burden are the two main challenges, including the education for kids who cannot attend the schools, if the underground schools are not available or if the air raid alerts and attacks by drones are so consistent that you have no opportunity to bring your kids to a school.

Chair: Can I feed my children?

Alina Belobra: Markets are functional in frontline areas, but access to markets can vary. Honestly, it depends on the city. Let me give you an example. Chair: Human safaris?

Alina Belobra: Yes.

Chair: What is that relating to?

Alina Belobra: Let me explain this terminology, which was used in one of Human Rights Watch’s reports.
