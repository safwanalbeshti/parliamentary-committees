Chair: Welcome to the second panel in today’s hearing on export support. We thank our witnesses very much indeed for coming along and giving us a perspective on what the Government need to do more of to help our larger exporters to export more. Steven Lynch, give us a sense of what your members think the Government need to be doing more of.

Steven Lynch: The Government’s ambition for trade and the rhetoric about growth being the No. 1 target of the Government are really welcomed. There is a genuine challenge around delivery, and we are really seeing this, especially from our small and medium-sized businesses. Put bluntly, you can either tax more or trade more, and I think the biggest opportunity is around trade. That is the lifeblood of growth, and there is so much latent potential in UK businesses that want to do more. It is the practical mechanism that we need to support them more on. We really welcome the fact that there is a trade strategy and an industrial strategy for the UK. If you break that down, the trade strategy says, “This is where we need to go,” and the industrial strategy says, “This is where we are globally competitive.” It is the delivery space that we are really lacking.

Chair: Once upon a time—I am old enough to remember this—the export support contracts were in the hands of the nation’s chambers of commerce, and it struck me that they were doing a pretty reasonable job on the whole. Then we arrived at a moment in history where those contracts were put on a very short-term basis and it was very difficult for chambers of commerce to plan. And then the whole shebang was pulled in-house. Would it not make more sense for us to put export support back out to the chambers of commerce?

Steven Lynch: We would absolutely welcome that opportunity. We have a ready-made platform: we have 51 chambers here in the UK and 75 chambers internationally—we are the trade network. On average, about 10% of British businesses export; 45% of our chamber members export. We know the trade support system. We know what businesses need. And what is really key is that you put the private sector at the heart of this, in regard to both delivery and design. Businesses are people who trade. This is the opportunity for the public and the private sectors to work a lot closer together, and again, we have a ready-made platform; we do not need to create something new.

Chair: Mr Reynolds’ new Department is having to take huge headcount reductions—at least 20%—over the next two to three years. Much of the burden of those cuts is going to fall on export support staff. Isn’t this the right moment for Government to revisit what has worked in the past and put the export support contracts back out to the nation’s chambers of commerce?

Steven Lynch: We know that this is a challenging moment for BIST. They are pulling back resources, centralising and digitalising. At the same time, we are moving towards more devolution and regional growth. That cannot happen without international trade and investment. So this is the piece that we would actively call for—reinstating and restoring support for our regions, especially, here in the UK. Fifty per cent of the UK does not have a mayoral combined authority, and we do not want trade to be a postcode lottery, but many of our members are saying that it is. So that is the opportunity.

Chair: The Prime Minister has said that he wants to see “growth in every postcode”. To deliver that, we are going to need exports from every postcode. Do you think that chambers of commerce could better deliver on that ambition than the current arrangements?

We have been working with the UK Government: BIST and the Foreign Office. It is private sector funded but Government backed. That is the opportunity to get our businesses actively growing, and all delivered around outcomes—not targets or KPIs, but genuine outcomes for business. We have proved it with Singapore and NatWest. We now have a big opportunity to scale that and replicate it right the way across the globe.

Chair: And that is rooted in the chambers of commerce.

Steven Lynch: It is rooted in the chambers of commerce.

Steven Lynch: First and foremost, there are some really valuable services currently in the system. The Department is doing really strong work, whether it be training or guidance. We have seen goods trade basically flatline, whereas services trade has improved. The big opportunity I would go back to is that we are a service economy—80% of our economy is services. There is a lot more that we could and should be doing on services.

The big opportunity for the UK, if we were really to prioritise, is in the SME space—the mid-market businesses worth £5 million to £50 million where we will get the biggest return on investment. They could and should be doing more; they just need a little bit more support on that. There generally are some great services at the moment; there is just a bit of a challenge connecting it all in together.

Antonia Bance: Quite often, when you talk to businesses about any of the support services available for them—we have had this conversation on the Committee a number of times—they talk about a single front door. Steven Lynch: To your point about which countries are doing this right, we can point to the Germany example, where their chamber is built in with their department for business and their foreign office. It is a different economy—more manufacturing—but that is an example of getting things right. It is really about connecting the private and public sectors a lot more. Japan is a great example of that. It is maybe not fully understood, but if you look at China, they bring the public sector into a lot of bilateral and multilateral conversations. Now that economic security is a huge part of the jobs of our fantastic trade commissioners and high commissioners, we should bring the private sector into a lot of these conversations. Especially if we are looking at third market collaboration and financial professional services, it is a big opportunity.

We do not need to look to other countries. We are actually doing a lot of good things at home. The east midlands is a perfect example of that. We have three chambers working with the combined authority to deliver exports. There are great examples in Wales and Scotland, where there is really focused and sharp prioritisation. This is where we need to get quite realistic. We need to focus and prioritise on specific sectors and markets. We could get a lot more growth if we got really targeted.

Chair: The money that we are spending on things like trade fair participation is basically a legacy of the past. Do we need to look again at where we are spending that money to make sure that it is being spent in the right place?

Steven Lynch: We want to avoid the cobra effect, where we put incentives in place to try to solve the right problem, but they actually make things more challenging. Again, if you look at a trade fair or a trade mission, the idea is to get more businesses out to market, but what is the genuine outcome? Are businesses actually exporting on the back of that trade mission, or have they just gone to the country?

I think that you will have a much better return on investment, especially for the money put in. The return on investment on the outside is outcome-driven.
