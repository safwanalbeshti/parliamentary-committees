Jonathan Leape: Yes, tough act! I am Jonathan Leape, executive director of the International Growth Centre, which is based at the London School of Economics, founded as an initiative of DFID in 2008, with the mission of promoting inclusive and sustainable growth in developing countries through path-breaking research partnerships.

A key design feature of the IGC has been to be there. We have had resident teams in countries across Africa, south Asia and the middle east. The idea is to enable us to have quite a deep view of both cogeneration of research and localisation of research. It starts with the agenda setting, and that is done very much jointly with policy leaders. We engage typically at the Minister of Finance level, sometimes Head of State. We agree what the priorities should be in terms of where IGC can add value to the policymaking process around growth, and then we organise our research around four themes of what we see as key drivers of growth in developing countries.

Very briefly, those are macro stability and state capability; trade firms and structural transformation—the whole transformation of the private sector—energy and infrastructure, including cities, which we see very much as engines of growth; and, finally, labour markets and women’s economic empowerment, meaning both labour markets generally but with a particular focus around issues of female labour force participation.

Just to make things complicated, we then have four cross-cutting themes that do not fit in any one box. One of those is gender. Gender issues run across all these areas. Another is data and AI. That is increasingly transforming the policymaking environment. We are working with Governments to understand how they can make policy more effectively with data and AI. Partly it is just getting access to their own data. Governments worldwide sit on masses of administrative data but do not get access to it, so data and AI is the second one. Political economy and geoeconomics is the third, and climate is the fourth. Those are things that we think need to run all the way through the DNA of our organisation, but with those four core pillars of research.

Very briefly, how we work is to combine both commissioning at the local country level and global commissioning. The global commissioning is driven by country priorities. We work with our country teams, which identify what the key priorities are in that country, and then we put those questions to a global audience of researchers. One of the things we do is create a level playing field across researchers in the global south and the global north. The idea is to try to get the best minds to think about these problems.

Going forward, we will have 90% of our research projects involving researchers from the country itself. Right now, we are at 70%, but we want to push that further. Our ideal is to have projects that involve local researchers and international researchers, because we think there is huge cross-fertilisation in that process, and to have projects that make a major policy impact—we track that very carefully, and I can talk about it—and an academic impact. Of our published articles, 20% are in the top five journals of economics internationally.

Q8 Dr Sam Rushworth: Lots of NGOs and multilateral institutions do partnership work in the global south and have partners on the ground. Is there anything specific about a research partnership and the knowledge that is co-produced that made what you are talking about more impactful?

Professor Martin: One key thing is that the UK has very successful universities with very strong expertise. That enables us to bring partners around the table—not to bring our expertise to bear on problems like that in a top-down way, but to convene and to get things going.

An example of something I am peripherally involved with is the Norwich Institute for Sustainable Development—which has been in part a product of ODA funding, but is now funded through private sector foundation trusts—and its work on agricultural index insurance in Uganda. It is a big problem that small farmers around the world cannot get insurance, and that stops them being able to invest in their farms and to be more productive and so on. Research-based partnerships are essential at the start to understand the problem and to be able to invest in the research, which develops the tools and the methods for addressing that. After that, once you have proof of concept and so on, then other partners might pile on board. Now there are 900,000 farmers who are insured who were not before, and that has a big effect. We hold that capacity, which is trusted by a lot of key players overseas who therefore want to be involved.

Q9 Dr Sam Rushworth: In your experience, has the GCRF funding increased research capacity in partner countries in the global south?

Professor Martin: I would say it could have done better. It has done an awful lot to increase UK research capacity by making interdisciplinary connections across a university like UEA. We have done our best also to ensure that that builds capacities overseas—and it has, in tandem with scholarships and so on. Q12 Dr Sam Rushworth: Thank you very much. Jonathan, could you briefly outline the research partnerships that the LSE International Growth Centre has been involved with?

Jonathan Leape: Our core model is to base a team of economists with an experienced policy lead as country director. Then, they develop the partnership with Government. Our core model is to be developing a partnership with the economic policy cluster in the Governments where we engage. What we have learned over the years is that this has to be cradle-to-grave for it to have the impact—the cradle being building relationships over time where policymakers trust us, in part because of the neutrality and independence, in part because of our authority and access to high levels of expertise, in part because we have delivered in the past so they believe we will deliver in the future. With that trust, they are more candid with us about what their challenges are and that is the whole start of the process. They help us set the agenda that might make a difference for them.

On that, someone once said that one of IGC’s great virtues is that it is ignorable. The point was that the World Bank is not ignorable because you need the lending, the IMF is not ignorable, and so on. Countries value expertise that comes independently to them. They can ignore it and we could be wrong, but they have it. That then means that we get access to decisions that others would not because we are not threatening. The idea is to start with the agenda and then—

Q13 Chair: Could you go back one step before you get to the cradle? How are those relationships determined? Do Governments come to you? Do you see a weakness in Governments and you go to them?

Jonathan Leape: It is a mix. It is always a country where FCDO—previously DFID—is active, so it can leverage the benefits of what we discovered through our research. In terms of our ability to engage, we need to have a Government who are committed to reform and to using evidence as part of the reform process. We pulled out of two countries seven years ago: Tanzania under President Magufuli, and Zambia because the President there was rotating Ministers in a way that made it impossible to engage. We then went back into Zambia when Hichilema was elected. A crucial thing is having a Government who want to reform and to engage with evidence as part of that. Of course, within that—as I am sure we will all say—identifying policy champions is crucial, because you need somebody who can navigate the political economy, the coalitions and all that stuff, which we can never do. The cradle is the agenda.

Then we work with them, and this is very much a back-and-forth process on what specific research question we should focus on because we, as researchers, know what is known and what is not known. Sometimes it may just be surveying existing evidence, and sometimes new evidence is needed. Then we put that out to our global commissioning platform, or they might put it locally. We have those two possibilities. Crucially, we then keep policymakers engaged throughout the process. One of the things I think researchers often forget is it is not just your findings that are useful; your baseline—what you learned when you tried to describe what the situation was—can be useful. We try to have an ongoing interchange with policymakers that also builds ownership on their side, so that when we release the findings it is more likely to be brought into policy decisions.

Q14 Dr Sam Rushworth: What do you do to ensure that those research partnerships are equitable and responsive to local needs?

Jonathan Leape: That is a good question. We have done various things. Most recently we have established formal partnerships with local universities. We have a lead university in every country where we work and that is quite a broad relationship—it is about not just producing policy-focused research but developing research talent. We have mentorships, fellowships and other sorts of offers. We offer quite a bit of online instruction as well. We are trying to build those partners. We work with other non-university partners, think-tanks, where that is appropriate. As I said, we are currently at a position where 70% of the research we commission has local researchers as PIs. We are going to move that to 90%. It is tough because our niche in the market is at the upper end—very high-quality research—which is why we have developed the partnerships with universities to help with the research training and to help them to strengthen their research training programmes.

Q15 Dr Sam Rushworth: My experience in developing countries is that often universities do not even have access to the journals that we would have. Are you opening up access as well? Is there a transfer of tools?

Jonathan Leape: There is very much a transfer of tools. We do not have a lot of money, especially after the cuts, so we are not offering very much to universities in the way of money. We are trying to offer in-kind things that we think are of benefit. So 70% of our international researcher network is interested in mentoring, which can—we have piloted this over several years—create real value for the local researchers at low cost.

Journals is not something that we are able to do. What we find most powerful is when local researchers—and we do a lot of work with early career researchers—join a PI team that has researchers from the UK or elsewhere, from research universities, because the transfers that happen there are particularly valuable. You can watch the best online course in the world and not absorb very much of it, whereas if you are working side by side with a leading PI, then you learn from her. You understand, “Okay, this is how she thinks about the problem; this is how she thinks about research design”, which is the key core challenge. We see there that we end up building repeat offenders—someone who comes in as a junior member of a PI group then later applies to us for funding in their own right or as part of another team and so forth.

Q16 Dr Sam Rushworth: This is my last question, Jonathan. This research is producing knowledge, but how successful is the FCDO at that point in taking the knowledge produced by these research partnerships and turning it into sustained, scalable programmes?

Jonathan Leape: I think they regard us as being responsible for doing that, not them. I will give you a couple of examples. In Sierra Leone, we have had an engagement since 2018, initially with the very capable mayor of Freetown, about introducing property tax. That had to address a whole range of problems, from how you value property in that context—and we had to have a simple, credible, transparent system—and how you get people to accept the idea that they are going to start paying a new tax, which involved visible increases in the level of public services, to how you manage billing and so on. Over a space of eight years, we built a property tax system with them that doubled their revenue base as a city and has the potential to increase revenues fivefold. That is now being taken to four other cities within Freetown and is being implemented in a way that will have a significant fiscal impact.

Q19 Tracy Gilbert: I will come to Jonathan first, but if others want to join in, I would be interested to hear your views. The Government are increasingly looking to private investment to fill gaps created by the ODA cuts. What are the risks in relying on private capital in development research, particularly where the commercial returns are uncertain but the development needs are very high?

Jonathan Leape: It is a very important question. There is no question that private capital will play a major role in development going forward. There is no question that that is essential. That is both domestic private capital, so mobilising savings domestically, and attracting foreign capital. There is no question that those will be needed to secure the growth that these countries need.

The question is: are we doing enough to support the institutional capability that Governments need to create the right framework for that investment, domestic and international? Governments need to have strong regulatory frameworks that are enforced, that ensure that this private investment, when it occurs, is aligned with national priorities and with broader social benefit. There is enormous potential benefit from investment in the grid, from investment in new renewable sources, from investments in high-level services, which will be very important for structural transformation. There is enormous potential.

However, left to their own devices, as we know in all sorts of different spheres, they will pursue private returns and maximise private returns. Often in weak enforcement regimes, that involves increasing private returns at the expense of the social return. In Zambia, for example, the Government have now been quite aggressive in trying to fight fraud in terms of mining companies exporting excessive uncontrolled quantities of ore for processing elsewhere. Governments need to have both a regulatory framework and the ability to enforce it. That is part of their growth strategy, that they need to be able to manage the private capital, both domestically and internationally, and encourage it there.

Some years ago, we were working in Ethiopia. One of the striking things that we learned was that the biggest source of domestic investment from foreign investors was not new stuff coming in, it was the new investments of existing investors. It is not enough to have tax holidays or one-stop shops or that sort of thing. You have to address the issues on the ground. What makes a firm want to expand on the ground? If you do that, you are benefiting your domestic investors at the same time as your foreign investors. There is a lot to be done, but it is a challenge.

Professor Baggs: My concern with private investment is that there is a risk that it narrows the scope of development, and there is a risk that some areas, like mental health or non-communicable diseases, for example, are wiped off that funding map. There is a risk of investment into a narrower range of countries as well. It has its place, but I don’t think it can replace ODA funding.

Professor Martin: Building on exactly that, it clearly has a role in my area of environment. It has a role in paying for carbon forestry, for trees to be planted, for the value of the carbon, and in lots of other areas. However, it is not going to replace the role of public funds and public agencies, which are often funding the bits we cannot do. The values of forests that cannot be monetised and provide returns is important. There is the equity element—ensuring that the more marginalised and vulnerable groups are the ones who benefit from that investment—and lots of other things like regulation. Gene editing is a big part of what we are doing now. The UK is emerging as a key driver of regulation in that field, as well as others, and that requires oversight and long-term vision beyond the annual returns and so on. Yes, things that it just cannot do very well.

Q23 David Mundell: Liz, to come back to another element of the issue that I raised before, in providing opportunities through the Commonwealth scholarships and other such arrangements, what do you see as the balance between the benefit that the individual gets compared with delivering what might be the UK’s international development objectives?

Professor Baggs: The benefit for the individuals is that they tap into that scientific excellence that we have on offer in the UK and all the associated environment with that, so that culture around research, the infrastructure around research, the values and so on. They join programmes that have components of them with a particular lens on LMIC challenges or they undertake specific PhD projects that are very much aligned to their future ambitions for going back home. They take all that back to their home countries.

During the scholarships, they have the opportunity to network. A network is a group of scholars, but also individual. If they are masters students on programmes, there are often networking opportunities within those programmes. There are obviously then networking opportunities when they undertake PhD study. They take those connections back home with them and they can then leverage those. Harry Anderson: I would probably agree with what we have just heard, as well as with what the previous panel said. There is clearly a role there. Q62 Chair: Harry, on the international education strategy, we have heard criticism that there was a very limited focus on development objectives within it. Would you agree with that criticism and what would you—or rather, your members—like to have seen included?

Harry Anderson: We are very supportive of the international education strategy. It has been refreshed a number of times, but most recently at the start of this year, which is the first time since the change of Government. We would say that the international education strategy has responded to a lot of the concerns that our members would have, in that it is broader in focus. There was a previous concern, if you were to look at the previous iteration of the strategy, that it was overly focused on export earnings and that that was quite problematic when you were going to meet other Governments, other universities in other countries, because it quite frankly could look exploitative.

What we have seen therefore is a welcome move away from just focusing on an export target to the broader contribution that international education can make. I think that reflects the breadth of contributions that universities can make. It is not just about international student recruitment anymore, it is about international research partnerships and it is about TNE, as we have just been hearing.

The opportunity that we have is that the FCDO has joined as a co-owner of that strategy. Previously it would just have been the Department for Education and DBT, as was. That is where we would always encourage the Government to think of universities as pieces of critical infrastructure of global development. Wherever the UK is looking to talk to other Governments around the world, our members will more than likely have a partnership or expertise or support that they could utilise in that sense, so I think there is a real opportunity there.

The challenge we face is that we have seen reductions in the headcount for what was previously the Department for Business and Trade in its colleagues based in-country. Q63 James Naish: Harry, coming to the reach of UK universities, about 650,000 students are located overseas, and UK universities are working and supporting them. How do you think that that infrastructure could be used more strategically as part of our development offer?

Harry Anderson: This year I believe we crossed over a quite important tipping point and threshold. I do not think this will be reflected in our submission because the data only came out very recently, but we have now reached a point where there are almost 700,000 TNE students—UK students—studying for a UK degree overseas. Given policy in the UK, we are seeing a decline in the number of international students coming to the UK, so those two figures have now crossed over. The short answer is that we are seeing a huge renewed interest from UK universities in establishing TNE partnerships. Probably a number of vice-chancellors and senior leaders within universities are looking at TNE in a far more strategic way, and I think the international education strategy helps with that.

What we are looking to see, and what is often happening is that the breadth of TNE provision is huge. It is not just campuses. Overseas campuses are often what attract the attention, but I think they are, off the top of my head, less than 1% of all TNE provision. There is validated provision, franchise provision and other types of provision, so there is a huge opportunity there for universities to be working with partners overseas to meet the local skills needs and local development needs of those institutions in-country to make sure that the offer is responding to what is needed locally. That is the opportunity we have. That is what I was talking about before, why overseas Governments often come to the UK and want our UK universities to help with setting up campuses and joint provision with their local universities.

Q64 James Naish: I will come to you as well, Anu. What explicit role could TNE have in our aspirations around soft power and international development?

Q66 James Naish: While we have you here, Harry, I will push you on something that is not related to international development but is very much university-related, which is what came up on 30 September, around the MI5 warning universities about China using a public company to front some of its intelligence operations. Harry Anderson: First, universities take incredibly seriously their responsibilities when it comes to national security. This topic has increased in salience and has established itself very much as a board-level issue within UK universities. Looking at what has happened over recent years, institutions have strengthened their due diligence, risk assessment processes and research security processes. My organisation, UUK, has been instrumental in helping to set up the research collaboration advice team—RCAP—now within Government, and which is there to support universities when they are looking to set up new partnerships.

We are always going to be a target for this, first because of the quality of our institutions, the sort of research that we are producing and the collaborations that we have, but also because of what I said at the start, which is that we are an inherently open and internationally facing sector and we are therefore always going to face these challenges. The question therefore is about how we mitigate those challenges. I think we have had a very constructive and good relationship when it comes to working with the Government on this. RCAP is one example. We had the director general of MI5 come to our annual conference to address our vice-chancellors and highlight that this is a major concern that institutions need to be aware of.

There is always more to be done, but this is an issue that universities take very seriously.
