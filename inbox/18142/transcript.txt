Q1 Chair: Welcome to this session of the Public Accounts Committee on Monday 19 October 2026.

Digital identity underpins how people prove who they are, demonstrate their eligibility, and access public and private services securely. Since this Committee announced our inquiry on digital ID, the Government have dropped their plans for a national digital ID scheme. However, the underlying issue remains. As more services move online, questions about how the public can conveniently and securely prove who they are remain highly relevant—they are certainly not going to go away.

The National Audit Office Report found that the UK already has many of the components associated with digital ID, but that the responsibility has fragmented across Government, with more than a dozen organisations and no coherent strategy for how they should work together.

Today, the Committee will examine how DCMS is organising its new responsibilities in this area, whether the Government have a clear long-term approach and accountability arrangements, and how they will address the practical challenges around legacy systems, fragmented data, privacy, security and public trust.

Natalie Jones: Yes. On digitising services, it has always been a requirement for people to prove who they are for certain things. Q24 Sarah Olney: When you say “once”, do you mean once for all time or once a day, once a week—because I am not accessing all those services continually?

Natalie Jones: Not all Government services require you to prove your identity. It is based on the sensitivity of the data or whether the thing is particularly tied to you. Obviously, for a driving licence, it is really important that you are who you say you are, but if you are paying your car tax, it does not matter who you are; you just need to have the means to pay. So the first time that you as a user create a One Login and access a service that requires you to prove your identity, One Login takes you through an identity verification process using things that you already have—if you have photo ID, you can use that, but if you do not, you can prove who you are using other means. Once you have done that, that verification of your identity is stored in One Login, so that if you go to another service that requires identity, you do not need to do it again—I would say once and for all, but there are some caveats.

One of the things that we have discovered as we worked through One Login with the bigger Departments is that it is not ideal to identify someone and not to look at their account again for a decade or two, because accounts can be compromised and it is hard to know when the person behind the keyboard is who they were originally. One Login therefore creates an account for you and curates that account, so it looks out for signs of attack or fraud. For example, if we could see your credentials accessing eight services in the same minute, we might think, “That’s a bit weird. Is it really them?”, so we have fraud analysts who would look at the telemetry that comes off the back related to fraud and investigate for you.

In an ideal world, you would prove your identity once, your account would stay within normal parameters of activity and therefore you would not need to prove your identity again, but in some scenarios—if perhaps we thought your account had been compromised—we might ask you to re-prove your identity at that point. We do not expect that people will have to do that routinely every six months, every year or anything like that.

Q26 Chair: This may be the time to ask you this, Natalie, but maybe Susannah wants to come in on it. There is one word that I think is very important in all that, and that is trust. Because of dramatic stories of hacking and so on, the public are very concerned about the roll-out of digital and the Government holding more and more data on them. How are the Government going to overcome that?

Natalie Jones: I can talk about a practical example with One Login. I can talk you through the security model, but at a principal level, One Login holds the bare minimum of data it needs to operate, so it is not a big data lake. The amount of information it holds about individuals is a very small number of data items. The first thing to say is that it is not a big target in that respect. That data is also kept in disaggregated places, so again, if you wanted to get hold of that data, you would need to break into a number of different places in the system before it would be of any use to you.

It is also built with privacy-enhancing principles in mind. One Login effectively works a bit like a security guard on a building, so when you want to access a service, it says, “Have you got the right pass?”, and then it checks that the pass has your face on it and it will let you in. That security guard does not know where you go in the building, what conversations you have or what data you access and neither does One Login, and that is a conscious design choice. One Login does not know what you do when you get into a service. It knows what time you have accessed the service and what time you have left, but that is it. It also checks your credentials as you go in to be sure that it is you. That is useful for things like fraud. Like I said, if you were accessing eight services in the same minute, we would think, “Hang on. That’s weird”, and it would trigger an alert, because we might think that your account had been hijacked, and we would send you a message to that effect.

Similarly, with verified credentials, One Login works like a perimeter fence for all Government services, and it will also protect the gov.uk wallet. Although it will know that you have downloaded a credential, it does not know the contents of that credential.
