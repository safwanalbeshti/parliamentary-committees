Q61 Chair: Welcome to the Public Accounts Committee on Thursday 15 October 2026. Today is the second instalment of our farming week, in which we are examining the resilience of UK food supply to disruptions.

On Monday, we heard from expert witnesses on topics such as the preparedness of the farming sector for major disruptions, as well as the Government’s engagement with food retailers, manufacturers and farmers about food supply and resilience. Today, we will explore how DEFRA and the Government are organised to improve food supply chain resilience, assess DEFRA’s preparedness for a severe supply chain disruption, and look to understand how the Department engages with industry and learns from other sectors and countries. We will also take a local angle on this issue by questioning DEFRA on the level of local and societal preparedness for food supply disruptions.

May I thank all our witnesses very much for coming? As is our usual practice, it would be helpful if you introduced yourself and said how long you have been in post.

Paul Kissack: Thank you, Chair. My name is Paul Kissack. I am the permanent secretary at DEFRA and I have been in post for almost exactly one year.

Emily Miles: I am Emily Miles, director general for food, farming and biosecurity. I have been in post for two years, and before that I ran the Food Standards Agency.

Helen Dallas: I am Helen Dallas, the deputy director for food preparedness and response. I have been in that role since April but have been working in this area for coming on for five years now.

Q62 Chair: Thank you all very much. Before I ask the first question, I should declare my interests as a practising farmer and chartered surveyor.

Paul, farming and food resilience are two sides of the same coin. In 2023, we had a major disruption to the food chain, so you had plenty of warning about what actions DEFRA needed to take to ensure the resilience of our food supply chain. This summer—this year, really—saw major problems for farmers, with a spike in input prices. We have now seen the whole debacle of the SFI, as Harry Metcalfe told us about on Monday, with five hours to get applications in. As if it was not enough for the price of fertiliser to increase by 30% to 40%, there is now a new fertiliser tax, imposed by the Government on 1 January. It does seem to me as though, in both instances, DEFRA is behind the curve. What can you do to make sure that DEFRA is more prepared for these quite seismic world events, which affect the resilience of our food and farming?

Paul Kissack: I would not recognise your characterisation of DEFRA as being behind the curve, but I absolutely recognise your characterisation of the challenges facing farming at the moment, and particularly over the last couple of years. For your assurance, nobody in DEFRA at official or ministerial level is under any illusions about how difficult it has been over the last year or two in particular for many farmers. As your witness on Monday pointed out, it varies by sector and by area of the country, but overall it is definitely the case that farmers have had a particularly difficult time over the last couple of years.

On how that plays into questions about the overall food system, perhaps I could start by offering a reflection on the Report itself, which is not about farming but about the broader food system. We absolutely recognise what the Report sets out, and I think we share its analysis almost entirely. It is very helpful and very well timed from our perspective, as we ramp up a programme of work. It sets out a food system that is extremely resilient, and probably one of the most resilient food systems in the world, in its ability to respond to some of the acute challenges it has faced over recent years, be that as a result of geopolitical events, the pandemic or the changes that came with the exit from the European Union. We have discovered that we have a very resilient food system led by the private sector. There is a role for Government, but the resilience comes from a sophisticated private sector food chain.

When it comes to farming—we absolutely recognise that domestic production underpins that food system—the Government have set out, I think unusually for Government, a clear commitment to at least maintain, and ideally grow, domestic production over the coming years. That was set out in the land use framework earlier this year and also in the farming road map, which I think is the first time a Government have set out a long-term vision for farming in quite some time. That is underpinned by sector growth plans—we are currently developing two sector growth plans around poultry and horticulture, and we published the organic farm plan yesterday. There is a lot of work going on to try to build the growth potential of the food system as a whole, both from domestic farming and from other parts of the food system, including manufacturing.

On Government funding in this area, it is worth recognising that we are now coming to the end of what has been at the very least a seven-year transition away from basic payments. Some might argue that that transition has actually been going on for decades as we have moved away from unlinked payments. Government funding to farmers is now focused broadly in two areas. One is the sustainable farming incentive and ELMs more broadly, to support farmers to move to more resilient farming. That works alongside food production, so that is good for food production and good for long-term resilience in terms of addressing some of the climate issues that you just referenced. The second type of funding is more in the innovation and technology space, to try to move to higher-productivity farming. That is how the Government now spend their money—or taxpayers’ money—on farming, rather than on the unlinked payments we had previously. That is a huge change for the sector.

As I say, we are coming to the end of that enormous change that has taken place over the last seven years. We are now seeing record levels of demand from farmers for SFI schemes—maybe we will come on to the SFI particulars you mentioned. This year, for example, we have committed close to £800 million of new funding for farmers to support them to move to more resilient systems over the coming years. That is in addition to pre-existing commitments that will be out there through ELMs and so on.

To stick with the farming element for a minute, I am glad that you cited the land use strategy. Savills estimates an average loss of farmland of 26,000 hectares every year since 2000. Paul Kissack: I heard you use those figures earlier in the week, and we did what good people do when they hear something and think, “Is that true?” We went back and looked at some of the data, and I think that is a UK figure rather than an England figure. As the DEFRA perm sec, I am better placed to talk about the England figure.

The amount of farmland in England is just shy of 9 million hectares. That is about 68% of the total land in England. That is by far a larger proportion of our landmass than in any comparable country. If you look at most European countries or at the States, it is much closer to about 50%. We are very unusual in having quite so much of our land under agriculture. I do not say that as a good or bad thing, just as a statement of fact. In terms of where we might have been 10 or 15 years ago, we now have 1% more land in England under agriculture than we did 15 years ago.

Q64 Chair: I would like to examine that in greater detail; we will do some work on that.

On food resilience, there has been quite a lot of criticism, as you will have seen from the NAO Report, that DEFRA has worked up some plans but has not worked them up well enough in concert with the private sector by testing them out and discussing them with the private sector. It is all very well working up plans, but if you do not have the buy-in of the people who have to operate them, they cannot be as effective as they should. What do you plan to do to change that?

Paul Kissack: That is a very fair analysis, so I completely agree with it. To clarify, the reference in the NAO Report is to our playbooks and the fact that we have not shared them. I would not share those playbooks because I do not think they will add any value for the people we would share them with. It is basically a playbook of how we stand up particular operations within the Department: how we set up teams, how we communicate with Ministers and with the press office, and so on. They are very much internal manuals for DEFRA to make sure that we are equipped to set things up fast. We similarly have a playbook on a national power outage—which we all hold in hard copy, as you can imagine—for what happens in a national power outage. They are internal manuals for how we respond quickly in the event of certain crises.

That said, the NAO is absolutely correct to identify a gap in not having a shared plan with different stakeholders for different events. We are working towards exactly that. We had a first meeting of a new food readiness group—I think it was last week or the week before—through which we are bringing together people from right across the food chain, including logistics, the cold chain, retailers, and a lot of the local responders, be they LRFs or the voluntary sector, to try to form a clearer national framework that will run through various different scenarios that we all need to collectively respond to, and to have clearer agreements on the triggers and intervention points, and the roles and responsibilities. That will be a shared plan, both in its development and in who can access it.

Q65 Chair: A final point from me—I also raised this on Monday—is about what the NFU calls, in its submission, multiple and compound threats to our food resilience. You have a major climate change problem, as we had this year, and you then get a massive disease outbreak somewhere else. In your discussions with the private sector parts of the food chain, did you discuss the whole issue of compound and several different disruptions occurring at the same time?

Paul Kissack: I have a couple of points on that. The short answer is yes—that is exactly the sort of thing we want to talk about with that group, which will meet on a regular basis.

To distinguish a bit between the different sorts of risks we face—I think the NAO Report helps a bit with this—when we say that we think the food system is pretty resilient and has demonstrated itself to be resilient, we are primarily talking about acute risks. We know it is resilient because we face a number of acute risks and we have therefore stress-tested it to real effect.

I am more worried about two different sorts of risks. One is chronic risks, the most obvious being what we are seeing in the changing climate and the more extreme weather we are facing. As I talk to farmers, that is increasingly the thing they are talking about. To be honest, they were talking about that before summer, heat waves, the droughts and the wildfires. That is a systemic challenge that we face, both in this country and globally, in respect of the changing nature of the climate and the loss of biodiversity. That is absolutely something we are focused on. To my earlier points, that is why we are investing in the environmental land management schemes to help farms to become more resilient.

The second sort of risks I worry about are what we call the catastrophic risks. They are untested. It is a good thing that they are untested—we do not want to have to face catastrophic risks—but the changing geopolitical circumstances mean they are becoming more apparent. A national power outage is a particular risk that would be very challenging not just for the food sector but for many critical national infrastructures. It is about understanding not just the compounding effect of those different risks but how you respond to different varieties of risk over time.

It is worth noting that DEFRA has moved over probably the last five years—so mainly not under my watch; it started prior to me but I have continued the process. Six years ago, when we went into the process of some of the EU exit planning and particularly the pandemic, we were not set up in the way that you might expect a national security Department to be set up, in terms of having a central professional cadre with clear lines into Cobra and the national security apparatus. We very much are now—over the last five years we have built up that capacity—and there is a particular role for that central resilience unit to deal with concurrent risks and ensure that we are moving resource and capability around the Department to do that.

Chair: I am sure other Members will wish to probe other questions. This is not totally a matter for your Department; there is an inter-dependency with other Departments and it is all to do with critical national infrastructure planning as well. I am sure Members will wish to probe that.

Q66 Rachel Gilmour: When will you actually have tangible plans and targets to support your objective to improve the resilience of the food supply chain?

Paul Kissack: We already monitor a very wide range of data on exactly that question. Every three years, as required by statute, we publish a food security report. I cannot remember which chapter it is, but I think it might be chapter 3 that is specifically about food chain resilience. That sets out a range of information. It is not targets and metrics in that sense, but it is a range of information, including data, that we track to see how we are doing on the overall state of food resilience and security in the country.

Alongside that, last year we produced the 10 “Good Food Cycle” outcomes, which are referenced in the NAO Report. One of the criticisms we have faced is that we have not produced metrics underpinning those. We are in the process of producing those metrics, and I expect us to be putting them out relatively early next year, all being well. Of course, there is a range of other public data and statistics that come out—further statistics came out today—which we can put together and use to judge the overall resilience of the system.

I do not think there is a single metric you can use. The NAO Report includes the production-to-supply ratio. That is quite a useful metric, but if I am honest, I do not think it is that useful a metric when we are talking about food security and resilience; it has quite a lot of flaws. It needs to be used alongside quite a lot of other things, so that we have a basket that we can assess.

Q67 Rachel Gilmour: I do not know whether you picked up on the news on Monday of a severe problem in France, where refrigerators were hacked and the temperatures were increased, which of course had disastrous outcomes. With that sort of thing in mind, where have you got to with the planned update to your departmental outcomes framework? Do you have anything that reflects complex hacking and incidents like that?

Paul Kissack: If I may, I will hand over to Helen to say a bit more about the work we are doing on cyber specifically. The case on Monday was interesting. I do worry about cyber. When I think about the things I worry about most, I worry about the chronic changes around climate, I worry about things like a national power outage as a catastrophic but hopefully very low risk, and I worry a lot about cyber.

The reason I particularly worry about cyber is that the risks are getting greater and the focus on critical national infrastructure is getting greater, as the chief exec of the National Cyber Security Centre has set out publicly. Aspects of the food system, from farm to fork, are connected to the internet to a degree that people are not necessarily au fait with. Who would have thought that French fridges would be the subject of a cyber-attack? There are lots of areas that could be susceptible to that kind of attack.

One of the characteristics that makes the food system as a whole quite different from some other critical national infrastructure systems is that it is very dispersed. Unlike the energy sector, which has a relatively small number of big critical assets, or the transport sector, where you can talk about airports and so on, it is very dispersed; there are tens of thousands of firms out there. To some degree that is a vulnerability, because you would have to guarantee the cyber-security of all those tens of thousands of firms. On the other hand, it is a strength, because if one of those firms goes down, there are substitute firms that will step in. Even recently, when we saw a couple of supermarkets subjected to cyber-attacks, the overall food supply for the country was fine.

Nevertheless, the growing level of risk is something that I am concerned about. A couple of years ago, we would not have had a particularly strong understanding within the Department about the cyber-security risks facing the sector, but since then we have made quite a lot of progress. Let me hand over to Helen to say a bit more.

Helen Dallas: We obviously learned a lot from the attacks on the retailers last year. One of the key things is the relationships across Government—with the National Cyber Security Centre, across the Cabinet Office and with us, too—that need to step into place when those sorts of attacks happen. We have been doing a lot of more intensive work at the retail end of the supply chain, including some benchmarking exercises on what their cyber-security levels are looking like and using the cyber assessment framework, which is a National Cyber Security Centre tool.

Because, as Paul says, there are tens of thousands of businesses, getting that intensive reach is incredibly difficult—well, it is not possible—so we also have things like the PAS 96 guidance, which has the title “Food Defence – Protection and Prevention from Deliberate Acts”. That provides a wealth of guidance to businesses, mostly aimed at SMEs, about how to protect themselves against these things. The French supermarket attack is a really good example: when we heard that news, we quickly stepped up the National Cyber Security Centre and were in touch with our retailers and checking with our stakeholders to see whether there were any impacts here.

Rachel Gilmour: So there was a lot going on behind the scenes when this happened.

Helen Dallas: Absolutely—always.

Q68 Chair: One of the Committee’s continuing themes is that the Government have an awful lot of legacy equipment. DEFRA has a lot of it, including in its ALBs. They are much more susceptible to cyber-attacks. Is it a real priority for DEFRA to do something about that?

Paul Kissack: For its own systems?

Chair: Yes.

Paul Kissack: Yes, it is. Cyber is always at the top of the Department’s risk register. There are one or two other things there as well, like the current position of the Weybridge site—the National Biosecurity Centre site—but cyber-security is basically at the top of the list, and we devote quite a lot of time to it as an executive committee, to understand where we have got to in terms of our overall position.

You are right that we have a very large legacy stack. The NAO identified a few years ago that we had what I think was the second largest legacy stack in Whitehall—I have always wondered who has the largest. We thought that about 300 applications in that legacy stack were potentially vulnerable. We have remediated over 200 of them, and we continue to work through that, so we are in a much stronger place now.

There is also quite a strong cross-Government drive, now led out of DCMS, to ensure that all Departments are applying a clear framework that regularly tests our cyber-security. Within the Department, we have also identified a small set of business-critical systems on which we want to do even more work just to double-check their cyber-security.

The biggest cyber-security challenges, both for a Department like ours and for the sector, are sometimes not the most sophisticated cyber-attacks but the relatively unsophisticated ones, as well as human behaviour in large organisations that makes them open. Some of this is also about training our staff to guard against that.
