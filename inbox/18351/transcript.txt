Examination of witnesses

Neil Ross, Chris Southworth and Ana Peres.

The Chair: Good afternoon, everyone, and thank you to our witnesses who are joining us today to help us understand better the ramifications, opportunities and areas for extra scrutiny for the e-commerce agreement that the UK has just recently signed up to. I am extremely pleased that we are joined by Neil Ross, who is head of tech, media and telecoms at Public First, Chris Southworth, who is joining us virtually and is chair of the International Centre for Digital Trade and Innovation, and Ana Peres, who is assistant professor in law at the University of Sussex.

We thank you very much for your thoughts. Please be as broad as you can be in your answers. If you think that there is anything at the end of this discussion that you would additionally like to send us for our evidence-taking, we would also be grateful for that. I call on Baroness Bi to ask the first question.

Baroness Bi: Welcome. I start by asking you a very open question. In practical terms, what new opportunities, obligations or constraints do you think the agreement creates for UK businesses? I am very happy for one or more of you to respond to that.

The Chair: Just to say, we have another session later with some officials from the Department for Business and Trade, so some of the government elements will be covered later. We are particularly keen to understand where we are from a business point of view. Neil, perhaps you can start and Chris will come in after you.

Neil Ross: Digital trade provisions, largely speaking, allow you to take advantage of non-discrimination clauses, electronic signatures, e-payments and various other digital systems to sell goods and services abroad. The good thing about this agreement is that it replicates some of the best provisions we have in other agreements—it is similar to those we have with south-east Asian countries, the European Union and others—and expands these to a whole host of markets where we do not currently have such agreements, most notably the Gulf states and China. That is the main innovation of this particular agreement, not least because of the geopolitical dynamics, which I am sure we will come on to later, that led to its slightly later adoption and the slightly odd format in which it has appeared.

That is the real benefit: it allows businesses to take advantage of those provisions. When I think about digital trade and international trade, the advent of AI and AI agents being able to much more effectively streamline those paper and compliance processes means that smaller businesses in particular can take advantage of these provisions. That is the real win here.

Ultimately, however, the big issue with digital trade agreements is that they are rarely heavily taken up by the business community. Most businesses tend to look at international trade and think, “It’s not really for me; this will be too difficult”. The Government and business groups will need to do a lot of legwork, similar to what we did with the European agreement when it first went through and some of the other agreements that we struck, to really raise the opportunities and profile of this agreement with a wide range of businesses.

Baroness Bi: Just to follow up, if it is your sense that it may be difficult for small businesses to see the benefit of this to begin with, do you think that big businesses will take advantage of this agreement?

Neil Ross: Big businesses will largely have their own agreements. One of the big countries that we have expanded these digital trade provisions to is China; many larger businesses will have found ways of getting around that. It is largely the smaller and medium-sized businesses that are the ones most likely to find an opportunity here. The question is whether we can help them to find it. It is often the case that businesses just do not think that it is an option for them. That kind of information-sharing, and spreading it among the business community, is an area that the UK has often been relatively weak on relative to other countries. The EU puts a lot of effort into socialising its trade agreements with its businesses, which can sometimes be more effective than what we do here in the UK.

Chris Southworth: Sorry about my camera being off; I cannot figure that out all.

First of all, this is a good balance between liberalisation and regulation. It reduces some of the impacts of things like localisation; in other words, it makes digital trade easier, more predictable and more secure for companies in general. When it comes to the broader question of whether businesses will use the agreement, that issue is much bigger than the WTO agreement. Ultimately, what we are doing here is making a big transition away from very traditional paper-based processes to very different ways of doing business that are much faster. That needs a behavioural change, a mindset change and a process change. To do that, we need to have some much stronger incentives. The Government have to do that alongside all the digital economy agreements and in what we are doing internally within the UK as much as the ECA.

I would say that the WTO agreement raises the baseline for a large swathe of the world—all our trading partners in various forms—which is a good thing. It will never meet all the objectives and ambitions of the UK, or any other G7 economy for that matter, but it pulls up that bottom baseline. Let us bear in mind that, prior to this agreement, we were operating on 1992 rules. That was the same year Google was created, just to give you an idea of the gap that we have had. This is a step forward, but it is only step 1 and they are already talking about version 2. This is a process over the course of time.

Lord Hannay of Chiswick: Can any of the three of you give a rough order of magnitude for the trade that is being done now through e-commerce as a proportion of overall world trade? If I am correct in guessing that the trend is steadily upwards and that e-commerce is growing, could you give an order of magnitude of that growth in recent years and its likely continuation?

The Chair: Chris, do you have any thoughts on that? We are happy for you to send us something.

Chris Southworth: I do. We have to be focused here on practical trade. Digital trade is a huge area, so if you discount anything that is outside of the movement and transaction of goods, that is the easiest way of looking at it. This is about buy-ship-pay processes. There has been progress, but it is slow, and much slower than everybody had anticipated, particularly after the Electronic Trade Documents Act in the UK. Only a fraction of companies are leveraging the agreements and leveraging the laws, policies and frameworks that we have in the UK, so there is a lot of work to be done.

I can give you a sense of some of the progress. To take the bill of lading—one of the documents in shipping—for instance, the usage of electronic bills of lading has gone up from an average of 1% to 2% a few years ago to 15%, and is climbing like a hockey stick. There is a much larger adoption rate in commodity bulk trade, which is up around 60% to 70% across the biggest shippers. That gives you a flavour of the movement that is happening in the global community, but there is an enormous amount of work to do to raise awareness of how companies can trade digitally. We have got to do that together as the public sector, private sector and the Government.

Importantly, from a government point of view, there is a need to incentivise that change. There are not enough strong incentives and demands in the marketplace to push companies to start to move towards digital trade. Customs is one area: the Government could be moving much faster on digitising customs. They could leverage public procurement for electronic transactions. These are the sorts of incentives that would help push the market to adopt. Once it gets going, the market will look after itself, but we are not quite there at the moment.

Lord McDonald of Salford: I think our guests have already answered the first half of my question, but I will persist, and there is a second half. What benefit to businesses does a global digital trade book carry? I think we are hearing quite a lot in terms of ease of business, especially for smaller businesses. Also, to what extent does this agreement mainly consolidate existing practice? What is actually new in this agreement?

Ana Peres: The main benefit here is the participation that we have in this agreement. The UK already has free trade agreements and digital economy agreements with selected partners but no agreement that reaches this scale of participation, where 67 countries have accepted the final text of the agreement and the interim arrangement.

For businesses, we have here an expansion of the countries where they can make business and where it would be easier for them to enter into contracts and to cut the red tape. For digital trade law, we have two broad areas to regulate. The first is the facilitation of trade through digital technologies and the other one is the regulation of the trade of digital technologies itself. This agreement focuses on the first area; it is about facilitating trade and covers how to use digital technologies in contracts with electronic signatures and electronic documents, and also how to cut red tape in customs procedures. For businesses, it will be easier to find partners in different markets where they are not yet present.

Chris Southworth: This agreement is, as I said, a very low baseline for the UK. It by no means goes anywhere close to the UK-Singapore Digital Economy Agreement, the UK-India digital economy agreement or the UK-GCC digital economy agreement. However, even if you put that aside, I can absolutely tell you that there are at least 50-plus case studies and use cases of companies that are transacting digitally, and the results are dramatic. We are seeing 80% reductions in transaction costs; 80% reductions in cross-border processing times, which means fewer delays at the border; 60% workforce productivity benefits; and, to give you an idea of the speed, we have slashed the transaction time from an average of two to three months to one hour. This is consistent, over and over again.

We are now looking at how we can slash the costs of things such as customs declarations and customs documents. To give you an idea of the difference here, an average customs declaration costs £48 to business, whereas the data transaction cost is £0.04. There is a £3.3 billion opportunity here to reduce the costs for business and consumers. There are benefits to Governments and to business, and we are seeing that consistently. The challenge is to scale those benefits across all businesses so that everybody can benefit.

The Chair: Can we go on to the next question with Lord Anderson? You are next because Sandy is not here, so we have jumped a question.

Lord Anderson of Swansea: This is an agreement of the willing. It could have been incorporated into the WTO, but there were objections from five countries—India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, South Africa and Indonesia. Were those objections in principle or to particular elements of the agreement? Is there any prospect of movement on the part of those five countries? Given all the advantages you just outlined, why did the UK think it proper to join this partial agreement?

Ana Peres: Actually, the list is longer than that—you can add to it Brazil, Indonesia and the United States. We have different countries objecting to this agreement and I would group those objections into two different groups. The first is a matter of institutional and procedural concerns, mainly from India and the United States.

For India it is a matter of principle; it objects to open plurilateral agreements at the WTO in general and thinks that we should pursue the multilateral route, that we have the 1998 declaration on e-commerce and that the discussions should be held in that venue. The US has, more recently, also voiced a list of concerns: the involvement of the secretariat, the role of the new committee, the relationship between this new committee on e-commerce and the existing committees in the WTO, budget issues, and the role of the WTO director-general as a depository of the ratifications of this agreement. We have here lots of institutional questions about the relationship between this agreement and the WTO.

The second group of concerns are about substantive matters for those concerned: which rules we should have, who benefits from those rules and what type of policy space Governments should have. Brazil, Turkey and Indonesia, for example, are voicing concerns about the development dimension of this agreement. They feel that the agreement does not do enough to promote the digital economy in those countries that are still lagging behind, and that there is a disproportionate balance here favouring those countries and big companies that already participate in the digital economy. They also have questions about the permanent moratorium on customs duties for digital transactions.

The second part of those concerns comes from the US as well, and it withdrew support in 2023. Until then, the US participated in the negotiations of this agreement with substantive proposals, but since 2023 the US has changed its position. The US Government believe that they need to preserve policy space to regulate pressing issues such as AI data, algorithms and source code and that these issues should not be discussed in the WTO, but rather they prefer instead to do that domestically while they reconsider the best approach to address them.

There are different objections and concerns being expressed but, for the UK and other members participating in and supporting this initiative, it is important to show, and there is value in showing, that this is just a stepping stone to something larger and broader, and also that the goal is still to incorporate it into the WTO and make it a WTO agreement.

Lord Anderson of Swansea: Thank you. That answers the gist that there is not one concern but a multiplicity of various concerns. This suggests that it will perhaps be very difficult to make the move to incorporation within the WTO. Is that fair?

Ana Peres: Yes. The agreement is exciting for us trade lawyers because it basically raises questions about how to negotiate agreements in the WTO and what the legal outcomes are that we should expect from now on because of the geopolitical scenario: should we still wait for traditional treaties as we know them, or should we focus on creative solutions, both in procedures and legal outcomes? It raises a lot of interesting questions that we unfortunately do not yet have the answers to.

Lord Hannay of Chiswick: To what extent do any of the three of you believe, and this is a judgmental question, that the objectives of those who do not want to sign up are simply attempts—fairly classical in the WTO, given the unanimity requirement—to achieve leverage to shift their position? Also, to what extent do you think that some of the objections will be susceptible to evidence that traders and business in their countries would benefit from joining this? Do you think a lot of the objections will simply be washed away by the evidence that e-digital trade is good both for sellers and buyers?

Chris Southworth: Those are really good questions and I want to be really clear about this. I was there in Cameroon at MC14 when the whole deal was, effectively, torpedoed by the US and India. It was super interesting on one level but very frustrating on another. In many ways, this has nothing to do with digital trade. Ultimately, the bottom issue here is the lack of reform at the WTO. The electronic commerce agreement is interesting because it is the first time that we have seen a large group of countries, in this case 66, saying, “Enough is enough” about the constant vetoing and blocking. All the countries you named are promoting digital trade outside the WTO, so this is purely about WTO politics. It is completely absurd that you have countries such as India—some of the most advanced in digital trade—using the electronic commerce agreement as a tool to get what they want out of the WTO, which is largely stockholding. It is all about compromise and the WTO’s inability to now find a way through.

My takeaway from Cameroon was that we simply will not be able to move forward on any sizeable agreements until the reform question is addressed. From a business point of view, it was brilliant that 66 countries were brave enough to stand up and say, “Enough is enough. We have to find a different way of doing this”. I would look at it in that context. The question is: what happens next and what role can the UK play? Its officials have been very good in the background, facilitating, mediating and brokering. They were literally running between rooms in Cameroon. We need that coming from the UK, Japan, Australia, Canada and all the other mid powers that can do this.

It is really important that we find a way through, but this is absolutely WTO politics. What we cannot do is to have no agreements, and we simply cannot put off digital trade any longer. Technology is moving so fast and the rules are so far behind the market that it is ridiculous from a business point of view. Time is not on our side. We need to make progress. This is one small step in the right direction but there is an awful lot of work to do on the reform agenda.

The Chair: Chris, can I ask a follow-up question? Some of the measures in the e-commerce agreement are highly practical, such as those on customs documentation. Are India, Brazil, Turkey and whichever other countries did not sign the e-commerce agreement implementing e-commerce customs documentation, et cetera? I assume they are.

Chris Southworth: They are.

The Chair: So does it make a practical difference that they have not signed the e-commerce agreement? It strikes me that everyone is heading in the same direction, with inkless signatures and so on, and the main bulk of this activity is well covered. Or is that not the case?

Chris Southworth: The countries to think about are the less-developed countries. The biggest beneficiaries of digital trade are small companies—SMEs—and the least developed countries are the smallest. Think about the Pacific, the Caribbean and a lot of the Commonwealth countries. Those are the ones that we should care about, and it is not fair for the bigger economies, especially the G20, to prevent their ability to participate properly in a digital trade environment. What we are seeing now is the big economies, G7 in particular, charging ahead and leaving huge swathes of the rest of the world behind. The WTO scoops up at least a chunk of those and enables them to participate, which is a good thing. Going back to the reform agenda, we cannot allow one thing to be played off against another, because it is simply not fair for the smallest—the SMEs and the least developed economies which need this more than anyone.

The Chair: We missed a question because our colleague was not here—perhaps some of your colleagues will want to come in on it—in relation to the CPTPP and other multilateral and bilateral arrangements, such as the UK-Singapore digital deal. Does this overlap on top of them? Is it necessary between countries that already have digital agreements? For example, does this make a difference to our relationship with Singapore on digital trade? Neil, would you like to answer that question?

Neil Ross: I do not believe it does. The key point that we are trying to get at here is that trade agreements are really messy. If you end up in a trade negotiation with another country, there is compromise in various different places, and sometimes you do not get everything through. A few years ago, I spent a lot of time working on the digital provisions of the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement, and there was no guarantee, until right at the end, that we were going to get non-discrimination, data flows agreements and various different things. All of those go a lot further than what it is in this agreement, but there is no guarantee that you will necessarily get them in a negotiation, so the real benefit of WTO-style agreements—this kind of agreement—is their breadth, and expanding those still quite basic provisions to a very broad range of countries.

Lord German: I wonder whether we could take this discussion further. In general terms, does plurilateralism mean that we are going to give up on multilateral approaches—in other words, move out of the current system into a new one—or should the search go on to try to find a rules-based approach that applies to everyone? The reform agenda that Chris talked about earlier would, of course, require unanimity in order to be able to change. With all those headings and gale winds in your face, do you think we have reached the end of the road for multilateral approaches, or is there still room for them? I do not know whether you want to go from academic to practical.

Ana Peres: No, it is not the end of the road, but it shows us that we need to change how things are done in the WTO. In the WTO, we have the rule of consensus, which—sorry to be pedantic—is slightly different from unanimity. Consensus basically means that no country can veto the decision, so it does not require all members to approve it. It is technical but we can differentiate from other contexts where we have just one country vetoing the whole process.

In this sense, we have plurilateralism in the WTO. We have the telecommunications reference paper, we have the more recent services domestic regulation and we have the ITA. We already have different agreements in the WTO that can be implemented just by a change to the schedules of commitments of the WTO members—they can modify the commitments in their schedules either in goods or services and implement the rules.

The difference—the big novelty—with this e-commerce agreement is that it has rules on both goods and services, and those rules imply bigger changes than just changing singular entries in members’ schedules of concessions. They require institutional arrangements and co-operation. They are changing the regulatory environment here, so for that we need deeper integration. We cannot rely on past experiences to show us the way ahead, so we need to try to find creative solutions to overcome these challenges.

Neil Ross: One of the interesting things is that this agreement came about at all. Two years ago, when we were thinking about the logical conclusion of this process, you would have thought it would end nowhere, and the moratorium on customs duties on electronic products would lapse. But we have now found that a range of countries in this agreement and in the parallel US agreement have been able to find a way around it. It is certainly not the end of multilateralism. We are obviously going through a bit of a trying time, but it has been very interesting to see this group of countries come up with what I think is a relatively innovative solution.

Lord German: Would it be fair to say that the fact that the director-general of the WTO is being used as a repository for these agreements is an indication from those who are participating plurilaterally that actually they would prefer to have a multilateral approach, and others can join in at will if they so wish? Would that be a fair reflection?

Ana Peres: Yes, I would say so. The position of the director-general is that there are no rules in the WTO agreement that preclude her from accepting that role. That is true. The countries participating in this agreement maintain that it is a WTO agreement because it was conceived, negotiated and concluded in the WTO. They are trying to preserve as many links as possible with the WTO to support this position.

The Chair: I am sure that Chris will want to come in but, Lord Hannay, would you like to ask a question?

Lord Hannay of Chiswick: I have listened with great interest to your answers, which seem to be very positive about this agreement, and convincingly so. Should the UK be switching the way it deals with digital commerce in bilateral negotiations and simply say to the partner it is dealing with, “Either you sign an e-commerce agreement or there is no agreement”, rather than trying to cobble together a separate one, as we have done, I think, for Singapore, India and others? What are the pros and cons of simply using any bilateral dealings we have with trading partners to put real pressure on them to join this agreement?

Chris Southworth: I would like to come in on that. I shall take those in order. First, on multilateralism, one set of rules for business is always the best option. What we are seeing now is just a reflection of the fractured politics in the world. There are just countries with wildly different views, values, sets of systems and ideas, and there is nothing we can do about that at the moment.

Plurilateral is the second-best option. As many countries as possible—a coalition of the willing—is the pragmatic approach. The way I look at it is that we are better making progress than being dogmatic and not making any progress, because we have not done so for years. Are plurilaterals ideal? No, but they are better than bilaterals and better than no deal, so we just have to keep pushing for a coalition of the willing. In today’s politics, that is the only way we are going to make real progress. If there is a multilateral deal up for grabs then we should go for it, but those are scarce.

It is not just the WTO; it is every global forum. We have just seen the same with COP—deals collapsing because countries simply cannot agree. I do not think it is fair to blame the Secretary-General, to pick up on that point; she has done a good job. She has a tough job in trying to bridge all the differences of opinion and she gets a hard time for it because you have to be bold. That is good leadership.

In terms of the ECA, deal or no deal, that is not completely fair. When it comes to countries like Singapore, India, Australia or the US, we are always going to want more. I would frame it in the way that Singapore once framed it to me: look at the multilateral and plurilateral deals, get them as high as you possibly can, depending on the grouping of countries—CPTPP is very different from other parts of the world, to give an example—and then go higher in either your bilaterals or your plurilaterals. I do not think you can force it on countries. We just have to look at the WTO as the broader community and then look at CPTPP, Europe or other parts of the world as different types of communities, and always go to the highest standard that we can possibly get everyone to. That is just the way it is at the moment.

The fewer bilaterals and the more plurilaterals the better, because that is less complex for business. It is really tough for businesses at the moment, with 250 regulatory changes a day, to give you an idea of the kind of stuff that companies are dealing with. If you are an SME, that is almost an impossible ask.

The Chair: That is a significant burden on businesses.

Chris Southworth: We have to bear down on that burden and do the best deals we possibly can.

Lord Anderson of Swansea: I fail to understand whether the current situation is fairly static or whether there is a certain positive dynamism arising from, as Lord Hannay mentioned, the lobbying by individual companies of those Governments who have not yet prepared to withdraw their objections.

Ana Peres: Do you mean dynamism domestically?

Lord Anderson of Swansea: As a positive movement, from the sort of position that Lord Hannay mentioned—pressure on those defaulting Governments from their own business.

Ana Peres: The Brazilian context is a good example of that. We have lots of initiatives from companies in Brazil pushing for a digital economy and digital tools. Brazil has created, for example, an important online payment system. However, the problem is the geopolitical scenario. It becomes a matter of sovereignty. These companies and the Brazilian Government regard policy space as a matter of sovereignty against the big companies from the United States, so we also have to factor in geopolitics and rivalry. These technologies are becoming more and more important so we have to ensure the supply of critical minerals. Yes, for the companies inside those countries it is quite dynamic. Indonesia is also trying to develop its digital industry and to process critical minerals in-country so it can get more benefits from digital trade. We have these movements inside those countries, and the bigger picture—the bigger scenario—becomes more complex because of these geopolitical considerations.

Baroness Anelay of St Johns: A short while ago, Neil, you referred to the fact that countries around the world are becoming less multilateral in their mindset, and multilateralism is under threat. The UK has continued with its allegiance and commitment to that, and we hear that when statements are made in the Security Council in the United Nations. Statements have also been made there about our continuing commitment to rules-based trade; that was said only last week. Against that background, since we are now among the 66 or 67—I am getting confused about what the actual number is, but I know that Ana Peres says 67, so I will take that—what are the risks for us in ploughing ahead with something that is not multilateral and is not necessarily the normal rules-based trade version of the WTO system?

Neil Ross: There is always a risk that you upset some of your diplomatic partners and you roll back discussions in other areas. The UK, because of the structure and nature of our economy, and particularly because of our very services-focused and quite internationally-outlooking way of doing things, needs to fully embrace a rules-based order. The more consistent the rules that we can apply across the world, the better for our economy. Digital trade has been one of our real success points, and we have been able to do it not just in this forum; we got better terms than we expected with the EU, we did well in CPTPP, and we have also been quite clever to move and join the US parallel agreement to this to make sure that we are kind of operating in the round. The UK, as Chris was pointing out, has been effective in these forums, trying to bridge the gap between lots of different countries. We all hope that at some point we will get back to a more multilateral way of doing things, and the UK’s role in that process is potentially very important and something that we should be staying committed to. While there are obviously some risks because of the structure of our economy, where we are positioned and how we generally do business, we have to lean into the positives and the upsides.

Baroness Anelay of St Johns: If you were someone wanting to trade at the moment and you knew there was some kind of risk, what do you think would be your percentage of success? Where is the level of risk low enough to let you go forward?

Neil Ross: I think most businesses will be happy to get whatever easing of process that they can get through. A business will probably not think about the diplomatic arrangements; it will just think, “Oh great, I can probably save a bit of money off my margins here if I can adopt this system or do this kind of thing”. Businesses will take what we can give to them. The key aspect of domestic implementation is that we have a very low uptake of digital technology among our smallest businesses, particularly at the micro and small end, and it is probably through domestic policy that we will see the biggest gains by encouraging those companies to digitise.

The agreements that we have struck around the world give you the channel through which to sell things, but most businesses might not know that it is there or how to make full use of it. I would say, when you are speaking and engaging with the Government on this, yes, focus on the agreement, but also think about how they plan to operationalise it, because that is often something that we do not spend enough time on when we talk about international agreements.

Lord Hannay of Chiswick: In comparing the plurilateral and multilateral approaches, surely if we were to give absolute priority to a multilateral approach or only to a plurilateral approach that was anchored in the WTO—not like the present agreement, which is not anchored in the WTO—we would basically be validating the blackmail that is being performed on us by those who are refusing to join. We would basically be helping to keep the amount of digital e-trade down from what it would otherwise be. The approach that we are looking at in this agreement seems to be a way round that. Have I got that wrong?

Neil Ross: No, I agree with that.

Ana Peres: Me too. There are risks, but we need to weigh them against the risk of doing nothing, and the risk of doing nothing is way higher. The UK can promote or advocate for some guardrails to ensure that this process continues to be transparent, open and inclusive so that any WTO members can join and influence the process at any point.

We should also make sure that this is just a starting point. We still need a broader, more ambitious agreement to cover issues such as data, AI, data transfer and data localisation. A truly global digital agreement without addressing those issues would be quite deficient and limited. There are things that we can do to try to protect this agreement to make sure that all countries can still benefit from it.

The Chair: Chris, I know you want to come in on some of these points, but I have two questions. This is clearly important for us, but it is not about us having to make significant changes to our legal system—we are already ahead of the game. It is about getting other countries to fit in line with us. If they do not, I am afraid that they will just remain isolated. I would have thought that they are incentivised to do this. That is one brief thought.

My second question is a bit deeper, and about the moratorium. How serious would it be if people started trying to charge customs duties on e-commerce activities? How would that work? We are led to believe that this is a very significant issue. This agreement parks that with nearly all the key economies with whom we do business. I have always slightly wrestled with why it is such an important issue and considered to be so deadly for our economy or the future of the world.

Chris Southworth: I will work backwards on a couple of those points. First, the moratorium allows a free-for-all. There is no established implementation mechanism for applying customs duties on electronic transmissions. “Electronic transmissions” is effectively anything digital. It is not just videos and web content but email, design, IP going through email and everything. Anything digital can be taxed. The question is how you would do it. From a services economy point of view, for us that is a huge risk, but it is a big risk for others, too.

There are two lines of thinking on this. Generally, we all agree from a business point of view that this is a terrible idea. There are also groups of people out there who think, “Well, go for it. If you really want to go and make yourself uncompetitive, then knock yourself out”. But in general, we need to stand on the point that this is not a good idea and, as always, the ones who will be penalised are the poorest and smallest. I do not think that that is a good idea.

On the point about our role, we must never forget that the UK has an incredibly unique role out there in the world as a bridge between the global South and North, as well as between East and West. These days, it is a mid-power broker. I would argue that we have always been that in history, but we are even more in that space now, with the likes of Canada and others. It is down to us to facilitate this. We need to lean into this because it really matters to our economy. Of the G7, we are the most impacted by all this disruption and bureaucracy going on. Anything that reduces bureaucracy is a good thing, but we have got to focus on our own self-interest in this as well, while doing it in the right way. That means we must be absolutely active. If you have not seen the recent Heywood Foundation report, it is absolutely spot on. We need to strike a balance between being super-active as a facilitator and broker, and looking after our economic interests.

Just to give you a number so that you understand what I am talking about in the context of this bureaucracy, it is generally accepted that the overall prize globally if we digitise trade is a minimum $10 trillion. Let us just look at that figure. Effectively, that is a reflection not of the prize but of the inefficiency tax that business and consumers pay for all the inefficiency. In the UK, that amounts to £224 billion minimum. That then gives you an idea that, if we just move quickly towards a digital trading system and support others to do the same—we cannot do it on our own—the win for our economy in terms of growth, competitiveness, agility and resilience is unbelievable. To me, that is the answer. It is a complete no-brainer.
