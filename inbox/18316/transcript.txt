Gerald Jones: Thanks, all, for being here. I will start with a question around local policing models. Will you talk about what policing model each of the forces operates under, and whether there are any operational trade-offs associated with each model? As an aside for Mark Hobrough—it is good to see you, Mark—the PCC for Gwent talked about the fact your force had “significant performance improvements” when it switched from local units. Will you talk a little bit about that, and what evidence there is for that improvement?

Mark Hobrough: To start, there are a multitude of different operating models across the country, let alone across Wales. Some of those, such as Gwent’s—as you alluded to—are functional and some are geographical-based. There are nuanced differences between those, and benefits and disbenefits to each of those models. I think it is fair to say that there is no one-size-fits-all model that all police forces and police chief constables would agree was the best deliverable for their local community.

In the role of chief constable it is your job to provide the best service that you can for your local communities based on what you see, what your data says and your feedback from your local communities. When I transferred into Gwent six years ago—I was formerly a South Wales officer for 25 years—it was really apparent to me that Gwent had a local policing area model whereby it was separated into boundaries and it felt very much like there were different forces operating in different ways. It also did not have specialised functions, so omni-competence was expected of our officers from the very start, when they went on patrol and dealt with an incident, right through to submitting files to courts and attending courts themselves. It felt like there were opportunities to really specialise things and improve performance.

I will give you some things that we did. I brought in a functional model for Gwent two and a half years ago, when I became the chief constable. About two and a half years ago we were towards the bottom end of the 43 forces across England and Wales on metrics for responding to 999 and 101 calls. We are now consistently, over the last 12 months, in the top five forces—we are sometimes in the top three and we have been top—and have consistently delivered.

We declare that we will answer emergency response calls within 15 minutes and priority response calls within 60 minutes. Two and a half years ago we were hitting metrics of about 45% and 48% success in those two arenas. Since changing to a functional model, we consistently deliver on those metrics over 80% of the time. Our community satisfaction levels have gone up. I link that back to my opening remarks about our mission statement as an organisation: to improve trust and confidence in Gwent police. For the first time, in the recent public survey that we undertook, 70% of those surveyed from our public said they have confidence in Gwent police. That has been an inclining metric since we brought the functional model into the force. I mentioned that engagement is an important part.

In a functional model—just to explain it—rather than having geographical bases, I have three distinct pillars: an operational pillar, a crime pillar and a bespoke neighbourhood pillar. It is not going away from neighbourhood policing; it is investing in a chief super who leads an entire department focusing on neighbourhood policing. I think that we are also one of the only countries that has a roads policing specialist ops capability. That capability is attached specifically to that neighbourhood policing pillar. They are not just answering grade 1 and 2 calls as a primary function—yes, of course they do that—but actually getting upstream to tackle the issues that are important to our communities and for which they have specialist vehicles and training. Those issues include dangerous dogs, speeding vehicles, off-road bikes and all the things that we and you know are regular complaints from our members of the public. What I find from that is that the functional model fits the force. I know that colleagues in the room operate a different model, but for me, on the metrics I see and the feedback I have, that is a model that fits Gwent.

Jeremy Vaughan: Basic command units are our model. For us, effectively, that crosses over Cardiff and the Vale of Glamorgan; the old Mid Glamorgan, which is around Rhondda Cynon Taf, Merthyr Tydfil and Bridgend; and Swansea, Neath and Port Talbot. Basically, we have those three core units. The local level sitting below them are local policing areas and local policing inspectors. I think about 80% of England and Wales now operate on a version of the BCU model.

For us, the basic command unit model works because it strikes the right balance between local oversight and accountability, and local delivery. People want to identify with their local police as much as they can, so the local policing area inspectors ought to be the immediate contact for local policing priorities and local partners, and then they feed up to a senior management team that sits above them. That is the way that we have organised our local policing model in South Wales.

I have experienced other models, and I am aware that others are favoured, but for us the BCU model seems to strike the right balance between the local and force levels. Typically, the local BCU commander will have quite a strong relationship with the local authority chief executive and leadership. For us, that cross-cuts either two or three local authority areas.

Amanda Blakeman: In North Wales, we have three basic command units: Eastern, which has the current local authorities of Wrexham and Flintshire in it, with four policing districts sitting under that; Central, which has Conwy and Denbighshire, with three areas underneath it; and the Western side is Gwynedd and Anglesey, with three areas in there. Each of those has a superintendent to lead it, with the chief super overseeing all three districts. In a very similar way to South Wales, we have very much tried to make sure that our resourcing and staffing levels across areas reflect the population and the crime levels that we see in those areas. We have tried to make sure that each has a strong local identity and that there is visibility and accountability to local communities within each. The boundaries align to strong partnerships with local authorities, with much of the work that we do that delivers the feelings of confidence to local communities.

We find that, for that deliberate balance between local accountability and operational effectiveness, a BCU model works for us. In our areas, given the size of them, we find that the main strengths lie in maintaining visible local leadership, strong community relationships, effective partnership working, and responsiveness to our communities. In much the same way as my colleague Mark identified, we have strong performance in responding to the public, in terms of response times and in our emergency contact centre. We have a command that has all that within it, including our police dogs and our roads policing, which is particularly important to us with the A55 being the gateway in, from the point of view of Merseyside and Cheshire. For us, the model has strengths, and it works across north Wales.

Ifan Charles: The operating model in Dyfed-Powys is similar to that of North Wales and of South Wales, in that we have four basic command units, which reflect the local authority areas: Ceredigion, Carmarthenshire, Pembrokeshire and Powys. The leader of an area is a superintendent. As Amanda mentioned, the expectation on that leader is that they are accountable for the performance in the area, and for building strong relationships with key stakeholders, as well as the local authority and health board chief execs, so that they can deal with challenges as they appear.

Under the basic command units, you then have the criminal investigation department, response policing, roads policing, offender management and neighbourhood policing. They all come under that person, but with a clear plan of what the priorities are for them, which is reflected in our performance framework for the force. I think that provides some real clarity, ownership and local accountability, through the individual being there and the way that we have set it up.
