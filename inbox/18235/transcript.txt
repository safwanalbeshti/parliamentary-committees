Jim Dickson: Do you see any risks attached to the wider public finances by devolving quite a lot of potential into the future revenue-raising capability and spending capability locally, taking it away from the Treasury?

Tom Josephs: Again, it very much depends on the final details of this. If you look again at the literature on fiscal risks associated with devolution, there are definitely potential benefits from local leaders and local areas being able to better target spending in their particular areas, but there are also risks around the potential for tax competition, a race to the bottom, competition between different regions and more complexity in the tax system.

There are definitely risks around revenue volatility at the local level. A narrower tax base potentially means more volatility, and that can be a challenge for local administrations to deal with. Again, that all very much depends on the final design of the system.

Jim Dickson: We know roughly that the likely components are business rates, income tax, a visitor levy and those sorts of things. Do you have your eye on any of those as particular potential problems going forward or, on the upside, drivers of better local economic decision-making that drives growth at all at this stage?

Tom Josephs: I would not say so at this stage. As I say, we will look at the final details, the overall package and how it all fits together to make that sort of assessment.

Chair: You talk, quite rightly, about how you have a position that, until it is actually happening, you cannot really score it, but there is expectation that there will be position papers coming out and a 10-year plan; there is “Rewiring the State”. How far, though, does that need to go before you can score it in the next forecast? What will you need to see from Government?

Tom Josephs: It varies from policy area to policy area, but typically we would need to see firm details of the rates that will be set, the actual amount of funding that will be devolved and the final details of any revenue equalisation type arrangement that the Government are considering. Those kinds of things have currently only been spelled out at the high-level framework type detail.

Chair: Of course, a lot of how it will be spent and the effectiveness will depend on the individual combined authority, and sometimes the individual mayor.

We heard very strong evidence yesterday from witnesses about the lack of accountability at the moment. We do not know what will come out in the paper. Is there any message you have for Government about how that will help you assess whether every tax pound is being spent as well as possible?

Tom Josephs: Our job is not really around the accountability in the system; that is more an NAO area. Our assessment will be on any fiscal and economic implications.

I would definitely emphasise the point that you are making. This will be very much dependent on final decisions that are made at the local level. A good example may be devolution of tax powers to Wales and Scotland. It took around nine years from the original announcement of devolving tax powers to Scotland before Scotland actually took some decisions to change tax rates.

Chair: So it is quite a long way off, potentially.

Tom Josephs: In Wales, it was about seven years before they got the powers, and they have not used those powers yet. If we had tried to make an assessment back when those original announcements were made of what the impact would be, you can see that it would be very difficult to do.

Chair: It is a slow burn. Dame Harriett mentioned earlier about the bank surcharge. There were a lot of assumptions about what might happen, but the big new bit was that it was only for 15 months, so it will come to an end. Do you think that makes a difference to the way that banks behave? Did you model that they might, for example, rebalance their profits over that period of time in order to mitigate against the tax?

Tom Josephs: This is in terms of that fairly narrow behaviour that I was just describing around potentially shifting profits overseas.

Chair: I do not necessarily mean overseas, but just reprofiling how they—

Tom Josephs: That effect would be lower for a temporary increase than it would be for a permanent one.

Chair: You have taken it as Government policy that it is only 15 months. Did you do any modelling about it going on for longer? Sometimes temporary policies have—

Tom Josephs: No, we just assessed the policy as the Government put it.

Chair: I figured that was the case.

Yesterday, we heard some very trenchant evidence from Helen Miller at the Institute for Fiscal Studies about founders’ relief. She described it as a dreadful policy. I am someone who represents a lot of founders who have then sold out. Some of them have stayed around, but some have sold out to big American companies and made some good money out of it, sometimes founding another business, I should say. We can see that there are challenges with how you do this.

What is your assessment? Have you modelled the responses to this new policy? Do you have any assessment about whether you think it is going to grow businesses, or do you agree with Helen Miller’s analysis?

Tom Josephs: As you know, it is not for us to comment on the merits or otherwise of the policy.

Chair: Indeed, no. It is just on the modelling and what you think might happen.

Tom Josephs: For us, this new founders’ relief is a fairly small measure. Across the forecast, we estimate that it only costs around £200 million or so. It is not a huge change and therefore is not one that we assessed in detail in terms of the economic impact that it would have.

Chair: How are you going to assess it going forward? You are saying that it is not material at this point.

Tom Josephs: It is obviously an important policy for those people who are directly affected, but in terms of the overall fiscal forecast, it is not a huge policy and therefore not one that we looked at in detail in terms of the economic impact.

The Government also announced a reduction in the allowance for the BADR relief, which has a roughly offsetting cost. There was more than one CGT measure announced.

Seema Malhotra: You have mostly addressed this point, but do you assess that, with this measure and other measures, there is a contribution to a more encouraging environment for entrepreneurship that has any positive impact that you might consider?

Tom Josephs: As I say, at this Budget the measures were relatively small-scale in terms of the overall fiscal impact. Therefore, we did not make a detailed assessment of the economic impact that they would have. That is not to say that they will not have important impacts for those people or businesses that they are particularly targeting, but from a macroeconomic perspective—it is our job to look at the macroeconomic and fiscal impact of these measures—we did not make that kind of assessment for these measures.

Dame Harriett Baldwin: One of the revenue-raisers in this Budget was the enthusiastic backing of HMRC to get more money from taxpayers. One of the ways that that is happening is because more and more people are having to fill out self-assessment forms. There has been a 17% increase in people who are having to fill out self-assessment forms.

I have asked some parliamentary questions that have highlighted that a lot of the people who are being asked for the first time are over 80 or 90; I had some people over 100 recently. They are suddenly being sent these self-assessment forms for the first time in their lives. How realistic is it that £3 billion of extra money is going to be raised by HMRC resources being boosted?

Tom Josephs: It is definitely an area that we highlight as very uncertain. There is quite a lot of risk around the estimates that we make of the additional revenue from compliance measures and measures that aim to reduce tax debt. We have quite a lot of analysis in the book about the way in which we assess individual measures of that type to try to ensure that the estimates are central.

We do a lot of work with HMRC to think about, “Okay, this measure will target this non-compliant section of the taxpayer population, but that population might move on to find different ways to avoid tax”. We adjust the costings for that sort of thing.

We also look top down at what each measure means for the overall tax gap—the overall difference between the tax that HMRC collects and the tax that it could theoretically collect.

Dame Harriett Baldwin: It is an enormous number.

Tom Josephs: The difference is the tax gap. That has been relatively stable in outturn over the past few years. Our forecast assumes that it will fall because of all the additional resources that HMRC is putting into tax compliance. It will fall to a level that is not unprecedented. In the recent past, we have seen it at the levels that we are projecting that it will get to in the forecast. We are reasonably confident that that is a central forecast, but, as I say, there is definitely a lot of risk around that. Those measures are particularly difficult ones to cost because they rely on assumptions around how people will respond to the measures that are very uncertain.

Bobby Dean: Beyond closing the tax gap via extra resources, there seems to be quite a bit of money coming in from almost moving tax receipts around. Is that right? The power to directly recover from people’s bank accounts and the fact that people are going to be submitting their tax returns more frequently means that you are going to be able to drag money forward into the forecast period that would otherwise have appeared later. Is that the correct way of interpreting it?

Tom Josephs: The measure on self-assessment payments being made in a more timely way in the year, rather than with a lag, as is the current approach, is the one that raises the most in this Budget from 2029-30 onwards. Most of that additional revenue is based on analysis that suggests that, because people are paying in a more timely way, they are less likely to build up tax debt than is the case when they are paying with a big lag.

Bobby Dean: There is a proportion of money that might be considered new as opposed to just moving around.

Tom Josephs: Yes. As you say, there is also a bit of an effect from tax being dragged from the future into the forecast period, but most of it is around those assumptions about having lower debt and more recovery of debt.
