Examination of witnesses

Doug Chalmers, Baroness Stuart of Edgbaston, Vijay Rangarajan and John Pullinger.

The Chair: Good morning and welcome to this meeting of the House of Lords Constitution Committee. I welcome our witnesses: Baroness Stuart of Edgbaston, the First Civil Service Commissioner; Doug Chalmers, chair of the Ethics and Integrity Commission; Vijay Rangarajan, chief executive of the Electoral Commission; and John Pullinger, chair of the Electoral Commission. You are all very welcome this morning. I remind members of the committee to declare any interest relevant to this inquiry or today’s meeting the first time they speak. I have no relevant interests of my own to declare. I remind everyone that we will pause the evidence session shortly before 11 am to observe the two minutes’ silence of Remembrance Day, the beginning and end of which will be marked by the ringing of Division Bells.

We are doing an inquiry into the constitutional safeguards in the United Kingdom. We are looking at a whole variety of things. We will have former Cabinet Secretaries and existing ones. We are looking at some of the organisations that make up the safeguards of the British constitution. However, we also very much wanted to hear from you about the role you play in protecting the UK’s constitutional democracy. Do you consider your organisation to form part of the UK’s network of constitutional safeguards? Although there are four of you here, you do not all have to answer every question, but I will ask Baroness Stuart to start off on that broad question, which you might all want to comment on.

Baroness Stuart of Edgbaston: Thank you very much, Chair. I regard the Civil Service Commission as an integral part of the constitutional framework of this country. It arose out of Northcote-Trevelyan in the last century, and I think we have now existed for 150 years. We are now within a statutory framework of the Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010—CRaG. The reason why it is an important element is that we now regulate and advise on the entire career cycle of a civil servant. We regulate entry into the Civil Service, either by chairing competitions, ensuring that the process is open and fair and decisions are based on merit, or by auditing departments on that process. We are the final arbiter of any complaints under the Civil Service Code. Since October last year, we have taken on the role of advising civil servants and special advisers of a certain pay grade when they leave the Civil Service on business appointment rules. The overall oversight, assurance and education from our role are really important for the constitution.

It may also be useful to say at this point that we regard our role as a regulator as well as an educator. What we are driving at is that we should spend as much energy on preventing wrong decisions being taken, ensuring that the system works and educating everyone on what is expected of them to ensure that the system functions properly.

The Chair: We had a lot of evidence early on about the Civil Service, and one witness said that what the Civil Service really needed was more responsibility and more accountability, which would make it react more beneficially to the issues that were facing us. Do you see that as part of the problem that needs solving, and do you play a role in that?

Baroness Stuart of Edgbaston: We do not play a role in that. However, the current Cabinet Secretary, Antonia Romeo, gave a speech at the LSE last night in which she emphasised that that kind of decision-making by civil servants should be effective and improve things. She has led by example; I think she is the first Cabinet Secretary who has made public her own performance criteria for what should be expected of her. In that context, she is showing leadership. That element of leadership, which my colleagues will have much more to say about, is important, in the Civil Service as in public life in general.

Doug Chalmers: We as the Ethics and Integrity Commission are slightly different. I was the chair of the Committee on Standards in Public Life before I became the chair of the EIC, and I will come back to its remit shortly. To build on something that Baroness Stuart said, we do not do casework, so I am not a regulator, but I would say that I am an educator and an adviser.

To take us back to first principles, why do we as a standards body have a part to play in this constitutional work that you are doing now? I think it is because standards do matter. Expectations among the public—having looked at some of the previous evidence, I know that might come up as a question—definitely have risen over time about those who are elected or appointed into public office. We are a country that should be governed without bias and the rest of it, and we still tax and police by consent, so that trust and linkage to consent remain very important.

We as standards bodies are focused on ensuring that that power in public office is exercised fairly, consistently and without favouritism or bias. The EIC, built off the CSPL, is an umbrella organisation. We do not regulate per se and we do not do casework, but we look over the standards landscape and then advise. We make policy proposals over time, just like the CSPL did in times past, that have led to much of the landscape that there is now. In fact, the team to my right are a result of a CSPL recommendation in the past.

The EIC is growing and has been given a few extra remits, one of which is to keep on doing what the CSPL did; another is a more formal element on engagement—that comes back into the “educator” word that you heard both of us talk about so far already. We are also to become a centre of excellence in codes of ethical conduct, which we might explore a bit further later on. I write an annual letter to the Prime Minister, and I chair a network of those who are in the regulatory space.[1] Baroness Stuart, Vijay and John are part of that network, so we get to know each other quite well from there.

Our remit is focused on England but we have quite strong relationships, and we are building strong relationships, with Scotland, Northern Ireland and Wales. They are all looking at the same problems and address them in different ways; their contexts are slightly different but we need to learn from them and share. We also look at other European countries, as well as countries like New Zealand, Australia and Canada, which are more aligned, about how they are addressing similar challenges. The challenges that we are talking about here are not unique to us; others are addressing the same challenges.

John Pullinger: The answer to your question is yes, we are definitely part of this system. That is what the CSPL recommended and was taken up in Parliament: that there should be a body that has the duty to give independent oversight to elections across the UK. We have a lot of elections. We have had general elections in Wales and Scotland this year, and for the last couple of days Vijay and I have been in Northern Ireland overseeing the preparations for the combined elections that are taking place there next May.

We have also had a lot of local elections in England and a whole string of by-elections recently. All those are part of our constitutional settlement, giving democratic legitimacy and giving those in power accountability and, using Doug’s word, consent—ensuring that there is popular consent for the system. So we would say we are absolutely at the heart of this constitution.

The Chair: Are you confident that what you do supports what Mr Chalmers just talked about: trust in the electoral system, without which the whole thing collapses like a pack of cards?

John Pullinger: Absolutely—the whole emphasis is on free, fair and trusted elections. We start from pretty strong foundations. There are high levels of public trust in the smooth running of elections. We survey this after each election, and we have a statutory duty to report within six months of an election. We have just done the reports for the May elections and, across the board, we have high levels of trust. That is down particularly to the efficient running of elections by local authorities up and down the country.

We also have high levels of compliance with the political finance regime. We work with all political parties and, to follow the conversation earlier, we try to create a framework that supports political parties to comply but of course calls out non-compliance. We have statutory authority to call out non-compliance and to fine people. To that extent, the system does well.

We have a whole range of threats coming down the line. If you think of the electoral system as part of critical national infrastructure, which we do, we need to renew our investment in it if it is to keep the strengths that we have had in the past into the future. I do not know whether you want me to go on and name some of those threats.

The Chair: We might come back to that, because it would be very interesting to hear what external threats you feel exist to the electoral system. Mr Rangarajan, do you want to add anything to that?

Vijay Rangarajan: I will add two things to what John has said. The first is that the part of the process with the lowest trust is the transparency of political finance. The current Bill, which you will all be involved in scrutinising, will help significantly on that. But it is a very complex system and one area where we think there needs to be some significant improvement.

The second area, which you may want to touch on later, is education; we have already touched on it briefly. Because of votes at 16 coming in, we are running a major programme to get materials to help young people understand not just the system—why politics matters to them and the issues that they care about—but where to get information from and, frankly, what information not to trust.

The third is: how to argue with a politician or a candidate whom you may agree with but may strongly disagree with as well. This is partly to tackle one of the fundamental threats that we see: the increased abuse and intimidation of political party candidates across the country. It is rising steadily over time—we track that—and that is a significant worry in the longer term. If people are not standing to be candidates, that is pretty fundamental.

So we are running a big programme of education. We are now in about 500 schools testing out the materials we have produced. We want to go to all schools in the course of 2027, which should help some of the underlying trust issues and expectations.

The Chair: Will this again be England only?

Vijay Rangarajan: No, that is across the whole country—it is UK-wide. We are starting in England and Northern Ireland at the moment, and we had a fantastic session in Northern Ireland with a whole lot of youth voices, networks and our key partners on Monday night. It is England and Northern Ireland because that is where votes at 16 is coming in immediately, but we will expand that. We have already done quite a lot in Scotland and Wales, where they already have votes at 16. But our new material is getting a very good reception. So we will go across the UK.

The Chair: It is helpful to confirm that.

Lord Murphy of Torfaen: Lord Strathclyde mentioned that a very big part of our inquiry is about the safeguards to our constitution. Can you tell us a little about how effectively you think your organisations are able to fulfil your roles as constitutional safeguards? What factors, if any, constrain your ability in so doing?

Doug Chalmers: Shall I lead off, as the umbrella, if I could describe myself as that? It was useful for me being chair of the CSPL and then coming to the EIC, because we have taken the literature that the CSPL did, the evidence-based inquiries and the recommendations that have been made from there, and we have moved them through. As you know better than I do, sometimes recommendations for action are not linear. They do not go in an algebraic way. You put something into the mix—into the system—and it takes a while for the system to truly absorb it before it finally comes through.

I have been a key advocate of much of the stuff that the CSPL did. Strong recommendations were made on things such as local government. It was great to see some of the stuff about finance in the Representation of the People Bill that we had put in before. So we keep on advocating that. The metric I judge by is: do we do high-quality, evidence-based reports that pick up well, with external credibility, as well as being tested by those involved? The other side of it is how the Government enact them and take them forward. Both are really important because there is an external credibility element, which also helps influence the Government and how they pick it up. But, for me, it is how the Government pick those up.

I watch quite carefully, and my two concerns are: how our reports are picked up and enacted by the Government over time; and whether I have the resource to deliver the quality and evidence-based work that we do. As I mentioned, the EIC has grown quite a lot and, if we get given additional tasks, then obviously my ability to match resource to task to get the quality that we are after can come under stress. We did a report on lobbying, business appointments and disclosure, which was asked for by the Government, and it stressed our system to get that in. We might want to come back to that later.

So we have had a good effect, and we have talked about some of the recommendations creating other elements that help guide and guardrail life in public office. But for me, as I look forward, it is: are they being picked up and enacted, and then how are they being done and in what sort of time?

John Pullinger: We are pretty well placed, but, going back to the threats that Vijay hinted at, the Electoral Commission is part of the electoral system, which comprises a whole range of people beyond us. The critical factor is how those bits of the system work well together. So I will just run through a few things.

The toxic information environment is an absolutely critical threat at the moment. Trying to get a handle on how foreign actors and others are manipulating the information space to confuse voters and destabilise campaigns is becoming a massive issue. The Electoral Commission can call attention to that. In the elections in May 2026, we ran a deepfake detection tool that threw out a whole range of issues there, including 15 cases where we thought there was a threat to democratic integrity. We are not a technological deepfake detection organisation, and we think that the state should have some systematic monitoring of this so that we can call out bad behaviour. That is number one.

Secondly, as Vijay referenced, the increasing levels of abuse and intimidation of candidates are putting people off and degrading our political capacity. The Speaker of the House of Commons has taken a lead on how we tackle that and ran a Speaker’s Conference last year. There is a critical role for the police here to step up and understand that they need to step in when there is unacceptable illegal abuse of candidates. Some of the police forces are very good, but some have been hesitant—how do they navigate the boundary between freedom of speech and harassment? It is really hard in the political space.

The third one, which Vijay referenced, is political finance. The public have very low levels of trust, even though we do a pretty good job of making public where the money is coming from. The Representation of the People Bill should help, but there is serious public disquiet about money buying influence, which we need to tackle. I am nearly getting to the end, Chair.

The next one is that British law around elections is so complicated that it is becoming very difficult for those involved—voters, candidates and electoral administrators—to manage. Much of it is very out of date. It is becoming inconsistent, because we get one layer over another, particularly for voters in Wales and Scotland. The complexity is enormous and, although there are very many good things in the Representation of the People Bill, it will add another layer of complexity. Amendments have been tabled to the Bill in this House, about to be debated, on making some progress with simplifying and consolidating electoral law. We think that is very important. It is one of these “boiling the frog” issues: it gets more and more complicated until one day it is so complicated it collapses under its own weight.

The final issue that is a constraint on us is the pressure on local government. Our elections are run by local authorities up and down the country. Returning officers have personal responsibility for running them and they do a really good job. We set and monitor standards, and we call them out if they fail to meet the standards—and a few do not—each time. They desperately want to do a good job but they struggle to get the staffing and resources that enable them to do it, given the complexity of the task that they have to undertake. So there is a range of things there and we are well placed, but there is significant work to be done between now and the next general election.

The Chair: You would not change it from local authorities?

John Pullinger: No, not at all. That is a real strength of our system.

The Chair: So you are saying there is a potential weakness there but you would not change their role?

John Pullinger: No. We know that in many fields local authorities are stretched.

The Chair: So it is basically about money.

John Pullinger: It is about ensuring that they have the ability to give the right level of resourcing to this job.

The Chair: Okay, I have got that. You said there was public disquiet about people buying influence with money. I completely understand about external people putting money potentially directly into trying to speak to Ministers or Members of Parliament, but your disquiet was wider than that, about the generality of spending around a general election campaign, for instance. Do you feel that you have a greater role in deciding what passes muster and what does not?

John Pullinger: Vijay may wish to add something, but at the moment we get input from the public about the things they are happy with and the things they are concerned about, and the lowest ratings of public trust are in the transparency of the political finance regime. I am saying no more or less than that. The public also want political parties to make sure that they are well informed about the issues they are going to be debating, and that costs money. So we are drawing attention to that disquiet.

At the moment we are running a review on the spending limits that political parties have at election time, and we are taking evidence from all political parties about how those should be set. This is quite challenging territory for us—we had a discussion of it at a board meeting yesterday—but it will enable us to put an evidence base before Parliament about what those spending limits should be. That will help to inform this debate in a much better way than maybe it has been in the past.

Lord Murphy of Torfaen: That is a fascinating account, and the question of resources is critical. Basically, if local authorities do not have the money, they cannot run electoral services properly, but there are other things too.

The Chair: Lady Stuart, do you have something you want to add there?

Baroness Stuart of Edgbaston: Yes. Just as the Electoral Commission has expressed its view on the plethora of legislation, which has layer after layer, at the other extreme we are the beneficiaries of CRaG, which is clear, precise and enabling, so legislation, when well drafted, can be really helpful.

It is worth putting on record that, in the four and three-quarter years that I have done this role, there has never been a moment where on either the political or the Civil Service side I have felt there was any undue pressure. However, if there were to be any, the political pressure would probably be the one that was slightly more difficult.

That leads me to the search for my successor. I believe I am the first former politician to fulfil this role in 120 years. I suggest—it is for others to decide, but I offer it as an observation—that it may not be a bad idea to have this role filled by a former politician. That also leads me to the observation that my term of office comes to an end at the end of February. It is a complicated process: PACAC has to approve and the First Ministers of Scotland and Wales and opposition leaders have to be consulted. The role was advertised in the summer but interview dates have not yet been set. I urge anyone who may be taking note of these proceedings to ensure that we do not end up with an interregnum. In regulators or any other organisations, if you do not fill vacancies in a timely fashion and do not provide the budgets, you weaken those organisations. You do not destroy them but you weaken them, and avoiding weakening them is really important.

Other than that, I would say that the Civil Service Commission has a long history. It is important and, as long as they do not weaken this organisation and allow the line management of the civil servants who serve on those organisations to be properly managed, these things can work really well.

Baroness Hamwee: This is to the Electoral Commission. In your written evidence, you said that the UK’s paper-based voting system is an important safeguard. I was a member of the Greater London Authority. It was immensely frustrating as a candidate, and then it provided quite a lot of work for me as chair, to be involved with the automatic counting. It is hard to think that there might not be a pressure for more automatic counting, the way the world is going, so I wondered whether you might want to expand on that.

Vijay Rangarajan: You are right, Lady Hamwee, there is pressure to use technology more extensively to make the systems simpler and better for voters, and better for candidates and administrators. The reason why we think that paper voting—you can use a pen or a pencil, I hasten to add—is important is because it is so transparent and scrutinised by agents, candidates and everyone in counts; you can track it every step of the way.

Internationally, there is a massive level of cyber attacks on counting systems around the world. We have it in the UK; we get between 30,000 and 50,000 cyber attacks on the Electoral Commission every month. That cyber threat to our systems means that it is important for voter trust so people can be absolutely clear what happened and it is scrutinised every step of the way. I argue that it is even more important for candidate trust. If you are a losing candidate, you really want to be sure that the system worked. For us, that emphasis on losers’ consent is very important. It is very public in the UK, and we think that is a significant trust.

That is not to say that we cannot strengthen the systems electronically in other ways. The biggest one that would help is automatic voter registration, because it is a real barrier that we have up to 8 million eligible British voters not on the electoral register. We very much welcome the provisions in the Bill that are going to bring that in. The Welsh Government did pilots in four local authorities last year; we evaluated those, and they went well. Again, as long as we get the cyber defences right for that and the law is very clear, I think that will hugely strengthen the system.

There are also experiments going on where, when you walk into a polling station, you can be ticked off on an iPad, and that is working well in many polling stations around the country. Again, that speeds up the process and makes it easier for local authorities, as we have touched on. Fundamentally, though, there is an element of voter trust and losers’ consent in having a clear, open and transparent paper-based system.

Lord Beith: I will move on to a more general question. Perhaps each of you could briefly say whether there is something that would make your organisation more obviously independent of the Government, and not perceived to be in any way dependent on the Government’s good will to do your job?

Doug Chalmers: Can I follow up, if I may, on the previous question? As you know, we were asked by the Government to do a review into lobbying, business appointment rules and disclosure. With the dissolution of ACOBA, we looked at that and identified exactly what you described. Civil servants were fine and Ministers went to the independent adviser, but military officers and Crown servants, such as in the agencies, did not fall under Civil Service rules. Previously, ACOBA dealt only with the top end and the departments always dealt with the lower level. We found that there should be better Cabinet Office corralling of the people who are delivering the Business Appointment Rules, because they are still owned by the Cabinet Office.

We also felt that the Business Appointment Rules themselves needed to be updated, because some of the mechanisms are quite old. I really welcome some of the review that Baroness Stuart and the Civil Service Commission has done on their side of it; there are many lessons in there that will, I hope, be translated through the Cabinet Office to the business rules as a whole. Therefore, all organisations should be done.

We also made a couple of observations about things such as national interest and who should be the judge of national interest, to protect against some of the concerns that you have. I realise that I say that as a retired general, so I feed that into the mix.

On your question about how important it is that we are independent and how we can be independent, we take that very seriously. I have just gone through the MoU exercise with the Cabinet Office to make sure that we are independent in our output, the choice of what we do and how we do it. But I have to rely on the Cabinet Office for support.
