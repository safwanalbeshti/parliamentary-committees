Q34 Chair: Welcome to this second panel in today’s opening inquiry into entrepreneurship in Britain. Chris Lewis, we are going to kick off with you. Welcome back to the House of Commons. The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor report that was published this morning shows that it is getting harder for entrepreneurs to get the start-up and scale-up capital that they need. Is that your sense? Tell us what is going on and what is going wrong.

Chris Lewis: You are right. There is a continued increase in supply of entrepreneurs, but they need access to capital. We heard it earlier in the previous session. I work in industry. My full-time main job is running a venture capital trust, and we see it every day when trying to match entrepreneurs with capital. There is so much demand and we are limited in the amount of capital that we have. We would like to be able to raise more.

The biggest challenge we are currently facing is the unintended consequences of last year’s Budget, where the income tax credit for VCT investment by UK taxpayers was reduced from 30% to 20%. That is having a very significant impact in terms of the reduction of fundraising for this year’s fundraising, which runs from now until the tax year end.

Q35 Chair: How significant an impact has that tax change had?

Chris Lewis: When we first spoke to Treasury about the impact of this decision, just after last year’s Budget, we started to model a reduction in fundraising of 20% to 40%. Our current level of budgeting and modelling goes beyond that, so it is having a serious impact. We are perhaps one quarter of the way through the fundraising cycle so far, so it might be early days, but all the indicators, for all the VCTs that are currently open for fundraising, are that it is markedly down on last year. If the rate of fundraising does not improve, we hope that, with this Committee’s help and with Treasury, we will be able to revisit it together, collectively, so the Government can take action before it is too late.

Q36 Chair: Can you give us a sense of how many millions less that means flowing into equity investment in start-ups?

Chris Lewis: Let us say that the VCT industry raises £900 million a year, which is what it has done broadly for the last five years. If we go on a 50% reduction, that will have a direct follow-on impact on to the amount of investing that we do. It is already down by half for next year, and the year after, and the year after.

Because VCTs are long-term patient capital, we do several rounds of investing. When we have existing companies on our portfolio, you will appreciate that every year, or every two years, subject to milestones, we do another round of investment. The companies that we support need that continued support. Whatever cash we have from fundraising every tax year, we owe it to them to continue supporting them further with advice and capital. By doing that, there is a big imbalance, therefore, in terms of the amount of free capital or free cash that we have to invest in new businesses, which obviously reduces as a result of having reduced the amount of overall funding.

Q37 Chair: We could be talking about a half-a-billion-pound impact on early-stage venture investment.

Chris Lewis: You are looking certainly in the hundreds of millions of pounds of reduction, both into follow-on and new portfolio companies. Those new portfolio companies will not exist every year for the next few years. There is a whole—I will not say a generation—cohort of entrepreneurs we will not be able to support quite as well as the ones we have managed to support over the last 30 years.

Michael Moore: You can think back to the ambition that the then-Chancellor, Jeremy Hunt, put, along with the City of London corporation mayor, Nick Lyons, and the previous Chancellor, Rachel Reeves, and a later Lord Mayor expanded with the accord to look at infrastructure and real estate. If we look back to that original stuff, for the UK start-ups that should become tomorrow’s scale-ups and the next generation’s great businesses for the UK, 16 times as much international pension capital is funding the UK compared to UK pension capital funds.

Q67 Chair: The Mansion House reforms have stalled, have they not?

Michael Moore: I am trying to be generous. We work very closely with them. It is clearly not fast enough. The idea that, instead of it taking five or six years to get to the target, it might take 15 or 20, is obviously not good. We will continue to work with the pensions industry on that.

Q68 Chair: What does the Chancellor need to do to speed this up?

Michael Moore: We have seen reform in the sense of the Pension Schemes Act in the last parliamentary Session. That is leading to a lot of consolidation. There is pressure in the conversations that we all have to speed up that consolidation, making sure that there is the expertise available to actually get that investment delivered and that, in terms of people being told about their DC pensions for the future, they can see the diversification of returns they will get from spreading a bit of that into these businesses that are in their own backyards. We think that there is a really strong case for saying that these are UK businesses that could go from Birmingham to be world-beaters, but they will not do it without capital.

Q69 Chair: That is an issue we are going to return to.

Chris Lewis: VCTs are genuinely, I believe, in the national interest. They fuel growth in every region with patient capital and advice.
