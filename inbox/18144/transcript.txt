Helen Waite and Shazia Hussain.

Q1 The Chair: Good morning. Welcome to the first oral session of the inquiry into children’s social care, particularly looked-after children in the system. I am Keith Bradley, Lord Bradley, chair of the committee. This is being live streamed; I just make you aware of that before we start.

I welcome you both, Helen Waite and Shazia Hussain, who will be answering our questions today. They will briefly introduce themselves at the beginning. Also, as you can see, we have three members joining us remotely, so that you can catch them as well. Thank you again for your attendance and we will hand over: is it Helen who is going to speak first?

Shazia Hussain: I was going to speak first, if that is okay. I will start with the introductions. Hi, everyone. I am Shazia Hussain. I am one of the directors in children’s social care in the Department for Education. I cover the earlier bit of the system, help protection and the social work workforce.

Helen Waite: I am Helen Waite, also a director of children’s social care in the Department for Education. I cover the care end of the system. Together we should have all angles of children’s social care covered today.

Q2 Lord Johnson of Marylebone: Good morning. Good to see you both. Thanks for coming in. I want to start with a question about outcomes for children. How well is the children’s social care system meeting the needs of children in care currently? How should we define a good outcome for a child in care? What evidence do we have that the system is delivering good outcomes, and how do we measure that over time?

Helen Waite: I will start, but we will probably go at this one together, if that is okay. We are on a significant journey of reform in children’s social care. Much of this has stemmed from the independent review of children’s social care, which was led by Minister MacAlister, who is the current Minister for Children and Families, which presented numerous recommendations in relation to the way that the system was currently not working, frankly, and the outcomes for children are not good enough. We are on that journey of reform at the moment.

To get those outcomes in a much better place, you have to start at the start in relation to prevention and the investment that we are putting in that space; we can talk a little bit more about that as we go through. In terms of the current outcomes, we know that children are much more likely to not be in education or employment. The Milburn report that is due out is obviously very relevant for us, and we have been inputting into that in relation to the support that care leavers receive. We know that stability is a crucial factor: children being in homes or foster care, where they are persistently moved, where they are not placed within their communities, means that they have not got stable lives, they have not got the relationships around them to support them to thrive. They may not have stable educational attendance, which also impacts on their educational outcomes. The outcomes are not good enough at the moment.

We have a package of reforms that we would love to talk to you about today that would address some of that stuff. This summer, some of you will have seen that we published a review of care leaver deaths—a tragic and hard read—which really spotlighted the fact that if you are a care leaver, you are more likely to die sooner. That also presented some opportunities for us to think about transitions and how the system operates as children come through the system.

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: I wanted, though, a definition of a good outcome.

Helen Waite: Sorry. A definition of a good outcome is that outcomes for those children should be as equal as they are for other children who are not in the care system. I think government, through its corporate parenting duties, has a responsibility to ensure that children ideally are supported not to come into the system and are supported to stay with their families and thrive. Where they do come into the system, we should look after them, support them and invest in them through all agencies and public services which should wrap around them to provide the advocacy and support to get better outcomes.

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: Outcomes need to be delivered within a budget and in the context of constraints in public spending. Children’s social care now accounts for 27% of local authority spending, up from 18% in 2011-12. We have heard that spending has risen much faster than the number of children needing care, and the average cost per child in care has risen by a third in real terms since 2015-16. Could you help us understand what is driving this increase in costs, and how can we judge whether local authorities are getting value for money from this expenditure? Finally, how do you define value for money and what is the baseline and counterfactual that you are using to measure that from?

Shazia Hussain: Should I start? As you said, the overall number of children that are receiving social care services has remained broadly stable over the last decade, but the number of children entering care has risen, and that is where a lot of the cost in the system is. A child going into care incurs foster care costs and residential care costs, and in particular, the costs of residential care have been rising. That is in part due to the complexity of the children that now present. Children are presenting at a much older age than they would have done, as a result of prevention spending over the last 10 to 15 years declining because local authorities sought to protect their statutory spend. Following the care review, we have made a £2.4 billion investment in prevention so that we can build back some of those preventive services. We have our Families First pathfinder, now a national programme, trying to get to children earlier. A key part of our strategy is to keep families together. We think that is a key outcome of some of the reforms that we are doing; where they cannot do that, it is to stay within their family networks. That is both better for some of the outcomes we were talking about earlier, but will also help reduce the flow in. I do not know if you want to touch on some of the measures around the care system.

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: How do you measure VFM? What is an acceptable amount for a local authority to spend to achieve a good outcome for a child in care?

Helen Waite: It comes back to what we invest in prevention. We saw local authorities whose preventive services had frankly all but disappeared. Therefore, if children are reaching the care part of the system and coming into care, as Shazia has said, with much more complex problems, not having the right wraparound support around them from Health, from Justice, from other agencies, means it is much more difficult to achieve an outcome when a child arrives in that position. By bringing support up front, we have a much better chance of ensuring they come out with a better education, better support and more stability.

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: I am not sure I am hearing you explain how you evaluate VFM in the context of achieving a good outcome. What is a reasonable amount for a local authority to be spending in a given circumstance?

Helen Waite: I do not think we have put a number figure on that. The way that we are evaluating the Families First programme is that we expect to see a reduction in the number of children entering care. That is through additional preventive support and we are also diverting numbers into residential care through a much greater focus on kinship and a much greater focus on fostering and increasing the number of foster care places available. Children that do come into the care system are more likely to get a better outcome if they are in a family environment.

The Chair: Can I just check, Lord Johnson, that you have no interests to declare?

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: No, no interests.

Q3 Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson: I apologise to the committee for arriving late. I wondered if you could just push a little bit on the preventive measures. I am particularly interested, following Lord Johnson’s themes about value for money, on whether we know what works if you are a local authority and you want to invest in preventive measures. You mentioned Health wraparound and Justice wraparound, but what evidence is there, for a local authority, about where the best place is to put your marginal pound and what support makes the biggest difference? Or does that evidence not exist?

Shazia Hussain: We have got pretty good evidence on the preventive end of the system: evaluated programmes on specific issues. The Supporting Families programme is one of the main ones, which showed that, for every pound spent, you get £2.40 back. That described the practice that works with families: having a key worker that can help you navigate all of the wider public services that you might need. That is a key plank of what we are now saying that local authorities need to do in practice, which we have codified in our programme guide that we tell them to follow. There are a number of other similar kinds of What Works evaluated programmes. We are managing it as a government major programme. We say, “Here are all of the things that have an evidence base”. They are a mix of interventions: if this family has a specific issue, try this type of intervention, and general practices of working with local authorities. That is codified in a programme guide. We have quarterly monitoring with every local authority in the country—how they are performing against those things. Where we are worried about local authority performance or practice, we have support mechanisms in place with experts that can go and help them transform their services. It is a richer area of understanding what works. I would say less so than in the care system. We have got a decent evidence base.

Helen Waite: I would add that, on the £2.4 billion that we have injected into the system over the period of this spending review, we have put it in through the local government funding settlement and have ring-fenced it. Although ring-fencing fell away in the past, it has broadly been welcomed by directors of children’s services, who have struggled in more recent years to hold funding in the prevention end of the system. As we have learned over time, when prevention spend falls, statutory spend goes up exponentially, so we are injecting this cash. We are effectively having to dual-run slightly in this rebalancing: we are having to put more money into prevention until we can bend the curve to improve the outcomes we are looking to see in the reduction in numbers of children going into care.

On where we have built the evidence base, we have worked with foundations—they are our equivalent of a What Works organisation—and this is focused on different aspects of support for parents. In designing the programme, we worked with 10 pathfinder areas, which were 10 local authorities around the country. We required co-design with not just the local authority; we required health and police to be at the table. How do you design services that are fit for a local community? What are their needs? Yes, you take on national evidence, but how do you make it bespoke in a way that meets the needs of those particular families around that set of schools in that particular locality? How do you support a family to navigate it? As Shazia alluded to there, we have a family help worker who will co-ordinate everything—the multi-agency response that is required around that family. A multi-agency person will stay with that family as needs ebb and flow, so families do not have to persistently come back in and tell their story time and time again, become frustrated or feel stigmatised. We want to remove the stigma so that prevention really makes a difference.

Shazia Hussain: I have one final point: the place where we have the strongest evidence base is on family group decision-making. For every £1, I think you get a return on investment of £3.80 back, but it is also proven to reduce entry into care. We therefore legislated for that, and we have put quite a lot of prescription around how local authorities do that, because we are so confident in the evidence base.

Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson: I have no interest to declare.

Q4 Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: I have no interest to declare, either. But I am very concerned and was shocked to read that, two years after leaving care, nearly a third of care leavers are homeless. I understand this focus on prevention at the beginning of life, but what can we do to improve that urgently? We know that, once people are homeless, all the data shows us what a disaster it is.

Helen Waite: Yes. Through the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act, we legislated to ensure that local authorities are publicising the support that is available for care leavers, but of course that is not enough. Again, we start slightly in front of a child becoming a care leaver, with all the things we can do in advance of that. We published a paper called the Enduring Relationships document, which is really designed to think about children in care and how we build those really important relationships with people that will stay with them when they leave the care system and become adults. That is not about just being the personal adviser at the local authority, who obviously should help the individual to navigate whatever support they need, but how they keep contact with their auntie or cousin—with the people in their community who will stay with them and provide that safety net, effectively, around them. It is really centring around enduring relationships.

On support for care leavers, local authorities should absolutely be publishing their offer. The Act also intends to expand corporate parenting responsibilities significantly, with all agencies across government, all schools and the health service having corporate responsibilities and duties to ensure that those in care and care leavers are supported. That is an expansion of the current arrangements.

We are also going to be rolling out Staying Close, which we legislated for: that is targeted support for those most likely to be at risk of homelessness. It provides supported housing and the wraparound care that stabilises the individual in that housing, supports them to live independently and secures ongoing tenancies beyond that. We will be rolling it out across every local authority across this spending review period. We have been working with 47 local authorities so far. We have slightly refined our expectations based on what we are learning of what works. We are going out to another 15 local authorities at the moment, and we will expand to all areas after that.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: I can hear the optimism, and I am really hoping that it is proven to be right. But I am aware that there are people of 20 who have been in care who go to A&Es across the country and report that they have nowhere to live while they are being dealt with for whatever they have gone to A&E for. Will the statutory duty then be on hospitals to ensure that they do not go back on the street after that attendance? I can hear how you are working with local authorities; I am frustrated with the other end—and I am, by background, a mental health nurse, just to declare that.

Helen Waite: There is probably more to do in the way that the broader system comes around an individual, in taking those corporate responsibilities seriously and acting locally to bring about change. We see a lot of variation in the way that housing teams within local authorities interact with the care leaving teams—you would think that would be an obvious connect, but it can be disparate. There is more to do there. The Prime Minister has stood up and clearly said that rough sleeping is top of the agenda, and we are working really closely with MHCLG to improve action on rough sleeping and homelessness more generally, with a focus on care leavers.

Q5 Baroness Redfern: Thank you for coming. I, too, have nothing to declare. My question is regarding Staying Close support, as you have just mentioned. The main sources of support for carers often hardly know the young person and are often unaware of the young person’s history and context. I wonder how that can be improved.

Helen Waite: That is interesting. That finding came through in the care leaver deaths review, titled You Really Are on Your Own. It really shone a light on the fact that too many care leavers really are on their own, that they do not have the support and that people do not understand them. There are those transitions, particularly on health, if I am honest, around moving from children’s support, where they may have finally got some mental health support; we had cases where children had diabetes and died because their support was not transferred over into the adult system. There is much more to do to make sure that those transition arrangements are right—but right from a multi-agency perspective, not just reliant on an individual within a local authority.

We are definitely in the foothills of what we are learning on Staying Close. We have redefined the model so that it is much more about the home itself and the support that comes with it. When we initially set off on the Staying Close journey, we were a bit more fluid about what might work for an individual, and we have realised we need to be tighter to get the outcomes and support that are needed.

Shazia Hussain: The only other thing I would add is that, at every step, we are trying to get a further step back: on working with children when they are in care and making sure that you are finding the people who can build that network around them. As Helen said, that sort of ambition is set out in the enduring relationships strategy. Once you have lost those opportunities, it is really hard to build people up to have a good transition out. Those things need to happen together, because it is quite difficult to find relationships for a 20 year-old: they are less likely to be engaged with their personal adviser and the local authority. You need to be starting that much earlier in their care journey.

Baroness Redfern: Would you think it is about building trust?

Shazia Hussain: Yes, but it is also about preparing them for the fact that the professionals will not be there for ever. We have had such a focus on building relationships with social workers and personal advisers, but, at some point, those people disappear. The shift in focus that Minister MacAlister has driven is towards finding the people who are in their wider networks—the people who they grew up with and around them who are safe—who will support them through that, so that, as professionals step away, they have a wider kind of family to be part of.

Helen Waite: On the reform journey, that is almost question number one when local authorities are engaging with a family. When there are early signals that things are starting to go wrong, one of the early questions is, “Who’s your support network as a family? Do your neighbours help? Have you got an auntie around the corner?” If you route in right from the beginning that you need a network around the whole family, and if things then go wrong, that network has already been contacted. They are already aware, so it is not a crisis phone call at the point at which a child may be going into care.

Q6 Lord Mohammed of Tinsley: Chair, I also do not have any interests to declare. I want to talk about another area of outcomes, or the lack of them: NEETs. What sort of interventions do you think we need to do? Clearly, that cohort of young people, when they leave us as corporate parents, are more likely to be NEET than your average young person. What sort of things can we look at pushing to ensure that they get the best start in adult life?

Helen Waite: These children, more often than not, have been through something fairly traumatic, so we should ensure that they get the right support at the right time. As we have said, stability is critical, but we see too many cases of children being moved placements—sometimes moved around the country, sometimes even to the other end of the country—so it is difficult. It would be unsettling for any of us to be doing that on a regular basis, but this breaks schooling arrangements and does not support children to have great attendance and therefore get a better education, which gives them the best shot at going on to further or higher education or into employment.

We have the virtual school head network. Every local authority in the country now has to have what is called a virtual school head. It is a bit of an odd name, but their job is to support all children in the locality who are looked after. They might be at a range of schools, but it is about making sure what is going on with their attendance and whether there is anything they need—again, being a sort of parental advocate to step in and see whether there are additional needs in place.

Children in care have a higher propensity to have special educational needs. Again, there is something in tandem with the reforms that are going on around special educational needs to ensure that this group of children, who do not always have the parental advocacy to push for the right support early enough, are seen, and that that support is put in place more quickly.

Coming back to your question, it is about supporting those children to thrive by creating as much stability as you can in the circumstances that exist. Wherever possible, that should be in a family environment. We are aiming for a massive expansion in fostering so that children do not have to go into residential homes but can be supported in a family environment. Those arrangements should be close to home so they can keep those enduring relationships and their schooling. Then, as they come into the care leaver system, we should ensure that we have great arrangements with DWP and that support is available for them to look for work.

Lord Mohammed of Tinsley: Can I press you on the support? There are a number of initiatives, such as the job guarantee, but the issue is that you have to have been unemployed for 18 months. That is quite a long time for a young person. Do you think there is an argument for looking at some of those national programmes and specific issues. Why can we not guarantee that on day one for people leaving care, knowing that they are going to be the ones who are likely to have all these other complex issues? “Why wait 18 months?” would be my argument.

Helen Waite: For some programmes that the DWP runs, there are arrangements that care leavers can access earlier. I know you are from Sheffield, and it is interesting that the South Yorkshire combined mayoral authority is running a programme with DWP at the moment that is really focusing in on NEETs, and a specific piece of their work is about care leavers. It will be interesting to see what we can learn from that and whether we can spread that learning.

The Alan Milburn review is under way at the moment, and he has made reference in the interim report already to the need to focus more on care leavers. The point that we have added to that is that, yes, we need to think about care leavers, but again we need to come back to children still in care. If you are going to push careers advice, work experience or whatever, how do we make sure that children in care are at the top of the pile for that sort of support to further reduce their risks of becoming NEET?

Shazia Hussain: I have a couple of things to add. We talked earlier about Staying Close. Stable housing is almost a precursor to being able to get a job and stay in that job. These things are complementary and we definitely hope that, if someone has somewhere to live, they are not worried about being kicked out of their tenancy and they are being supported to maintain that tenancy, they will be able to secure a job, keep the job and so on. We have examples of that, both through our Care Leaver covenant, where other employers can pledge work experience, internships and so on, and through public sector internships. The Civil Service internship scheme recently reached 1,000 care leavers having been through that scheme. So there is a combination of things, but your point about the mainstream is, as Helen says, one that we have been pushing at.

Q7 Baroness Nichols of Selby: I have a slight interest here, as in my other role I represent people working in the social care sector and have done for about 40 years. I was a bit anxious about the value-for-money element of this. For me, it is about how we can change young people’s lives, often from some really bad starts. Obviously society has changed over the years, and the needs of these young people are much more complex. We have to get to the bottom of that. The value-for-money element for me is in changing their lives, making them not go into shops and shoplifting but giving them employment so that they can then have a normal life. It is about support, I think. At one time, all care leavers were guaranteed an apprenticeship with my local authority, and that was a start. It went a bit haywire when the Future Jobs Fund was scrapped and now we have got the apprentices levy, but I do not think we use that as much. I do not know whether there is something that you can maybe tie in with that. My authority was a forerunner with the No Wrong Door programme, where it does not matter what complex needs you have because there is someone in these multi-agency teams now who can deal with everything for a family, rather than having four or five social workers or care co-ordinators going in to do that for them. Regarding value for money, if we support people and give them a good start in life, the health service will not need to give them as much support. Some of the issues for me have been about budget constraints over the years for local authorities and the move away from the looked-after bit and on to the foster carers. I worry that, because there are more complex needs, people cannot cope in their own homes. Those are all the things, for me, that we need to be sorting and being part of to make that better, because, if their lives are better, the lives of everybody around them will be better as well—and for the families.

Helen Waite: Yes, it is about the right support at the right time from the right professional and then working together, rather than families being left to navigate.

We are doing a piece of work to think about the purpose of residential care. We believe that the vast majority of children who come into the care system should be in a family setting. That is why today we have launched our first ever fostering national campaign. We are aiming to get 10,000 more foster places by 2029. That is pretty ambitious, but we feel that children are better supported within the family environment, and obviously that comes with the need for us to think about support for those foster carers with, as you say, a changing cohort of children who come in with differing needs. How we support those foster carers is one huge plank of our work in that space.

The other is that some children will still need residential care, so what is the purpose of that residential care? Who are the specific children who require that level of support? In providing that, and accepting that there is always going to be a cost to that, how do we make sure that we get the best value for money by providing the right wraparound support? That will often require complete integration with health services.

Again, we have just announced—we have lots of announcements at the moment—the Home Again programme. Our new regional care co-operatives, working with ICBs, will specifically target those children who are on the edge of a deprivation of liberty order due to their welfare needs, or indeed who already have a deprivation of liberty order against them. Those are the children who are most high cost and most at risk, who often are not in appropriate accommodation or getting support, and nor indeed are their families. How do you support the child but, importantly, support the family as well in order to navigate that child out of residential and ideally back to a family environment, be that their own family or a fostering environment?

Q8 Baroness Hollins: You have mentioned increasing complexity, and I wondered if you had an idea about the range of needs that children are displaying now. Sorry, I should say that I have no interests to declare, but I was a child psychiatrist and had a specialism in learning disability and autism for many years before I retired. I am interested in the range of different therapeutic support that children with complex needs might need, and whether the system is sufficiently joined up to enable that to happen. There is also the question of their continuing needs at 18, because a lot of these children are not going to be able to live independent lives. You spoke about fostering; should we be thinking about adult fostering? I am thinking about how complexity affects the picture that you are painting for us.

Helen Waite: We can talk a bit about rising demand and where we are seeing the shifts in that, which Shazia can talk about in a second. When it comes to complex needs, through the Home Again programme we have commissioned a specific tool to assess the needs of a child much more effectively, with medical support, which then drives the right action, often medically related, around the child, with the anticipation that that could mean medium-term or long-term support and intervention that stays with the child whenever they are in residential care, but also backs out of the system once they go back to families. I do not know if Shazia wants to say a bit more about that.

Shazia Hussain: I said at the beginning that over the last 10 to 15 years, because of the reduction in prevention activity, we have seen children presenting later, so the harm has been happening over a long period of time. We have not got in early enough when they are five, six or even younger, so that, by the time they eventually present, those needs, both in the family and for the child, are quite entrenched, and it is a much harder and therefore more expensive type of placement to do the right kind of work with the child and their family.

We have to shore up the family environment so that the child can return, which is important, but we have also seen a change in the harms that children experience. The change in age range is also linked to a rise in extrafamilial harm—not just online but grooming gangs, county lines and so on, so we have not really had to deal before with the things that we are dealing with.

Helen talked about us having to redefine the purpose of residential care. We have had a system that was designed to deal with children who struggled to stay in foster care. The next step was residential care. Things like the Home Again pilot are really starting to do exactly what you are asking: what are the needs? How many of those are mental health needs? How many of those are SEND/autism related? What is it about the environment? When we have really expensive placements, the ones that cost half a million pounds a year, that is because we do not yet know what the right treatment is, and we have high staff ratios to contain children because we have not quite worked out what the prescription is. That is what the pilots are designed to do.

Helen Waite: Yes, we have pilots doing that, and the review on the purpose of residential is under way. We are trying to home in on which cohorts of children are best supported in that environment and what training and support the workforce should have in residential; it is a workforce that has not been reviewed for many years. So this is a real opportunity, a moment in time, for us to understand what we want from our residential system and change the way it operates.

Q9 Baroness Pidgeon: I do not have any interests to declare. It is interesting hearing you talk about the work on prevention and what you are doing in that space. You have touched on the issue of residential care. Clearly, that sort of care is the right intervention in some circumstances, and it is important to recognise that. It is not always safe for some children and young people to be at their home. You have also talked about complexity and older children, and I think that is something we will want to explore further. Could you expand for me on what assessment you have made of the residential care market?

You have mentioned regional care co-operatives, which, I have to say, before preparing for this meeting, I had not particularly heard of. Could you expand a bit more on that and what role you think they will be playing in this area?

Helen Waite: We honestly think that the market is not functioning in the way that it needs to. It is no secret that expanding costs mean that the system has moved to the point where about 83% of it is private sector and the rest is public sector and not-for-profit, and there is significant geographical variation in relation to supply and demand. One-quarter of our provision is in the north-west, but one-quarter of the children who need that provision are not in the north-west. We do not have enough provision in London or the south-west. That is why we end up in a situation where local authorities are scrabbling around for the nearest provision and seeing if it is good enough? Does it meet the needs, potentially on a Friday afternoon, when they are trying to find an urgent placement for a child with complex needs, where their existing placement has broken down or the child is at significant risk at that point in time? Crisis decisions are being made where ideally you would not make them in those circumstances.

To go back to the outcomes point, in no world is it good if a child is living in London but gets sent off to the north-west. That is nothing against the north-west; you want children to stay in and around their communities to be supported. Stability is really important.

So we are rolling out regional care co-operatives. They are designed to provide some economies of scale around local authorities and to improve commissioning arrangements. Often, we find that local authorities are doing spot purchasing when they are in those crisis situations. They do not have the economies of scale themselves to be able to drive a market that is shifting significantly at the moment.

We are seeing new private providers coming in all the time. Again, some of those will be brilliant and some of them will not when it comes to their ability to decipher what good looks like in terms of cost, value for money and, importantly, quality when it comes to the changing dynamic of that cohort. For the regional care co-operatives we have had two pathfinders, one in Greater Manchester, operating across the Greater Manchester combined authority footprint, and then one in the south-east. Those are people from within local authorities who want to switch careers, as well as graduates. That is making a difference to sufficiency. In the latest numbers that I have, in 2025, we were at 36,200, which is the highest we have had since 2017, the first point at which we started recording. Vacancies are down 17% from 2024—we saw a huge improvement in 2025. Agency social work is down 17% from 2024 too, turnover has improved, et cetera. Across every metric that the department monitors, we saw a significant improvement.

As well as our workforce entry programmes, we took action on agency social work. We have talked quite a bit about some of the work we are doing on regional carers, which is much more complex, difficult and slightly more fledgling.

On agency, we did not use the law—we have now taken a power to put some of the work that we have done on a statutory basis—but worked to establish regional memorandums. Quite a lot of what was pushing the price of social workers up was that people were competing for them. Within localities, there are regionally agreed price caps; regions are required to report to us on a quarterly basis on whether anyone is breaking those. There are arrangements about post-qualifying experience: you cannot graduate into the workforce as an agency worker; you have to have three years’ post-qualifying experience in a local authority before you can move out to an agency. You cannot leave a local authority one day and appear in a neighbouring local authority as a social worker; you have to have a three-month wait. This is a set of things to constrain the agency workforce. At the time, the market that operates agency workers said that we would lose all these people, who would leave the profession. They have not: we have seen them return to the permanent workforce. We are much more confident in the supply side.

On development, we have talked quite a lot about the complexity that social workers are now dealing with. We are investing heavily in professional development. For quite a long time, we have had an early career programme, which is for a year, and we have just launched—well, we have just procured somebody to develop the curriculum materials for—a two-year early-career support programme for lead child protection practitioners. We are saying that, if you go into child protection, it is much more skilled and generic standards are not enough to do it, so this is how we will support you in developing that aspect of your career. There are other strands for people who are leading family help teams, et cetera, which will come on board later. It is worth saying that we did quite a lot of work with ADCS and LGA workforce committees on the things they were telling us social workers needed, such as more CPD, more support, more group supervision, et cetera.

The final thing on workload is this. We are doing two things. When I talked about the Families First programme and the evidence-based programmes that we are putting into prevention, quite a lot of them told us that, sometimes, the intervention works best when it is not with the social worker. The social workers carry statutory responsibilities and there is a lot of stigma and fear: families do not always want to engage, because they feel that that is quite serious. The earlier, preventive bit of the system, building on things such as the Supporting Families Programme and the family group decision-making model, all of which used non-statutory workforces, is investing in building up a multidisciplinary workforce. We do not yet have a rich picture of what that looks like; we have only just started collecting data on what sorts of workforces local authorities are building up. They might have a huge issue with domestic abuse or a particular clinical mental health thing, and they will have freedom to spend that money on a multidisciplinary workforce that sits within the local authority. That reduces the social worker workload—and they are not equipped to deal with those cases, so they were carrying cases and thinking, “I don’t have the skills to deal with all this variety and complexity”.

Those are just some of the things we are doing. The national professional development offer will come on board later in 2027—I cannot quite remember the date. We have just concluded the procurement, we will be making an announcement very shortly as to the lead supplier, and then the suppliers will start developing materials so that local authorities can get their staff on to those offers.

Q22 Baroness Redfern: I have a quick question: do you think a region’s workforce vacancy rate is heavily linked to the performance of its individual councils, where they require improvement or are judged inadequate by Ofsted?

Shazia Hussain: Yes, definitely, but it is incredibly complicated. We know that you are more likely to be inadequate, as judged by Ofsted, if you are in a more deprived area, as there is more strain both on local authority finances and on their families. There is a perfect storm: it is really hard to be good in those places, which is why particular parts of our country have more performance challenges, and then, if you are judged inadequate, you see an exit of the workforce. That is where we would see people—I was going to say “from Bradford” because that is where I am from, but I know others in the room are, too, so I will say A and B—leaving a place and going to a neighbouring authority because it has a better judgment. Some of the things we talked about earlier, about constraining that movement, are quite important. I do not believe people turn up to do a bad job—that is rare. There are contextual factors, and those might be external to the local authority or might be about broader corporate leadership. We have an intervention and improvement function in the department that is delivered through our regions group, who work with us on understanding which places the department needs to wrap around. To go back to workforce slightly, we are no longer a top-tier national departmental risk; we are monitoring every local authority, looking at what is happening to the numbers that I talked about. If, alongside our other set of numbers, we start to worry, we are supporting individual places alongside the national programmes that I mentioned, whatever the specific prescription might be in that area.
