Q163 Chair: Let us compare the Government’s approach to AI with its approach to, say, aviation, financial services, pharmaceuticals and food safety. Cat Little: The Committee will be aware that our approach to the regulation of general technology is to regulate within context. Q180 Martin Wrigley: What have you learned about your testing processes after the breach in July? Do you trust your environment?

Henry de Zoete: That is a very good and important question. The thing to be clear on about our incident, which was an incident, is that there was no security breach. It was not like some of the other incidents that we have heard about, where the models have worked out an exploit to get out of their sandbox and to go and do bad things.

When we set up our evaluation, we connected the models to the internet. We did that on purpose because we wanted to see what the models’ capabilities would be to go out there. Bad people are going to connect these things to the internet, so there is a very fine line between doing testing in a simulated environment and connecting it to the real world and seeing what is going on.

What the model then went and did was definitely bad behaviour. When we became aware of it, we shut it down within an hour, and published a full report on it within a week. I could talk through the things that we have changed. Essentially, we reviewed when we give internet access to the models to make sure that we know when we are doing it. We have made our sandboxes harder anyway, even though there was not a security breach. We have improved our monitorability, creating a synchronous monitor that looks at what the AI is doing.

Frankly, the speed and change of the capabilities caught us off guard at that point, because we did not think that the model would have the capabilities to go out and do the kinds of things that it went and did. On reflection, we should not have connected it to the internet, but these things are judgment calls. Previously, we had done that and things had not gone wrong, and we learned lots of important things. It is very important to test in real-life situations in order to see what is happening, but we have improved the simulated environments. The speed of improvement across these models is quite something to behold.

Q181 Martin Wrigley: Is there any other environment in which you would try something in a live environment when you did not know how it worked? The way that we are trying to evaluate these models is just so dangerous.

Henry de Zoete: This is a nascent science. Evaluation of these models is very new. It started only in the last two or three years, and we are learning all the time. The kinds of things that we are doing now include creating very detailed cyber-ranges, where we simulate a computer network for a model to attack. This time last year, the models were able to do very simple, narrow cyber tasks of a certain nature. We have now created cyber-ranges that involve 32 steps, covering everything from reconnaissance to exploit identification, lateral movement inside the system, and then data exfiltration. These take a human 20 hours to do. We found in April this year that the models were able to do these autonomously, and now they can all do it.

We are having to build more and more difficult cyber-ranges, and doing lots more difficult tasks to get them. We have one that is called the cooling tower. It is a harder task, but it involves the models making things happen in the real world physically. That is also a cyber-range that has now been completed by the models, so we are having to use all our resources and talent to keep building these simulated things in order to keep on top of the speed of the capabilities of the models.

Q182 Martin Wrigley: Are you faster than Anthropic, OpenAI and everybody else?
