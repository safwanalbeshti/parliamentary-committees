Witnesses: Sir Ian Cheshire and Dame Melanie Dawes.

Q1 Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Committee. Today, we are holding a session on the work of Ofcom. We are delighted to welcome, for the first time since his appointment as chair earlier this year, Sir Ian Cheshire. Alongside Sir Ian is Dame Melanie Dawes, who is familiar to us as the chief executive of Ofcom.

We are also joined by a star guest this morning, in the form of Dame Chi Onwurah, chair of the Science and Technology Committee. She is our star hostage for the day and will obviously be known to both of you through that role. Before we begin, I remind Members to declare any interests at the point they ask their questions.

I start with you, Sir Ian. This is the first time you have been in front of our Committee since your appointment as chair. What assessment have you made so far of the scale of the challenges facing Ofcom and how it is rising to meet them?

Sir Ian Cheshire: It is very nice to be here again, having been here before with a Channel 4 hat on. Thank you for giving us your time. I have probably been struck by three main things since coming in, some elements of which I suppose are fairly obvious from the outside, but a couple maybe are not.

First, Ofcom is a multiregulator. It has different remits in different areas and, obviously, needs to think about different staff in different areas. In the last three years, it has become totally transformed by the addition of the online safety challenge. With what is coming now with social media, this is a complete inflection point. The old Ofcom, created as somewhere to put competition in telecoms and broadcasting standards, has expanded into this very different animal. From the outside in, that looks like a very broad set of responsibilities. In the shape of online safety, it is genuinely evolutionary because no one has tried to do this before or regulate it in this way. Trying to get to how we make it work is a whole new set of challenges.

Having been at BT, where we were regulated by Ofcom, I saw a very clear, practised form of regulation that was well established, but also the framework opened up to give us fibre. It was all good regulation work there. At Channel 4, we had broadcast regulations which worked very well. Again, there was lots of practice.

Online safety changes the game. There are 450 people now in the organisation who were not there previously, with a year and a half of writing codes—I think Melanie has written something like nine major pieces of codes. That is not very exciting from the outside in because it sounds as though you are not doing anything, but until you have the code you cannot measure the response against it, and then you cannot get to enforcement.

I feel as though I have walked in at a point where Ofcom has gone through its transformation and is about to get into the next phase, which is much more about achieving impact and compliance. It is not just fines or keeping score, but genuinely changing behaviours. I think we have some good early examples of that with things like X, which has changed. Even Meta, despite suing us three times, is engaging with us. I think what needs to happen now is that we learn our way into how to have maximum impact over the next three years. The team are very up for it, and we will be discussing a review of how we are organised to make sure we have maximum effect.

Secondly, this is a highly respected regulator, both in the industry and overseas particularly. I find it interesting that the online safety players are sufficiently concerned about us that our lovely head of legal, Martin, is in court this week—one of two against 50 lawyers from Meta, TikTok and X. They are taking us seriously. This is emerging, but it has not yet got to the point where we are in a full cycle. We are in flight on this.

Finally, when I was sat here before Chi, I was asked, “What are your early perceptions from what you have heard pre-appointment?” I heard a lot about perceptions that Ofcom was slow or legalistic or did not move fast enough, maybe did not have the right people, or was too big. I genuinely do not think it is too big. You need to understand the whole digital economy to be effective in this. We are still much smaller than the FCA. I think we are about the right size but we will need more resources, which we are asking for, to do a better job on online safety, particularly with the social media ban. That will take more resources.

We need to recognise that if there is a perception that we have not moved fast enough or been risky enough—however it is expressed—we need to communicate that. We need to communicate, for example, that we have been very busy. We did a great job on porn but we have also been busy writing codes in other areas, so there are not flashy engagements or enforcements.

Also, we have to set a tone. We will not just give up if we find that we hit the limits of the Act, as we have done before. We have talked about the suicide forum and things like that. Instead, we are saying, “Right, if we can’t do that, what more can we do?” Potentially, that includes asking the Government for more powers to be more effective. I think, as you learn to use these things, you find that out.

The team has done a fantastic job of pushing and shoving, but needs to signal—particularly on online safety—from the early engagement all the way through to compliance and fines that this is a continuum of enforcement. The thing we are interested in is the outcomes for citizens. Ultimately, it is not a scorecard of how many days we have had in court; it is about whether more people have been better looked after.

We are just at the beginning of that and we are going to challenge ourselves very thoroughly. We have been doing that, but particularly from a board point of view I will challenge the team, saying, “What more can we do? What can we signal?” But we are not going to take stupid risk profiles on key legal cases or something like that, because a lot of what Ofcom does is highly legal, highly disputed and quite hard to figure out. What did Grok actually do with nudification? Until we got into the investigation, we did not know; it just appeared.

We are very motivated. It is brilliant for me, having been on the other side of the regulator, to now be inside it. I have been impressed by the quality of people. I feel that I can contribute to that. Perhaps there needs to be a reset in the perception, which is a lot to do with communication, but ultimately about our performance. I take that very seriously.

Q2 Chair: I sat in on Chi’s Science, Innovation and Tech Committee on your appointment hearing. One thing stressed there was the importance of the chair understanding that Ofcom’s primary duty is to the British public.

Sir Ian Cheshire: Correct.

Q3 Chair: What more do you think needs to be done to demonstrate that at this moment?

Sir Ian Cheshire: I have two thoughts. As I say, we are literally just finishing a review of the online safety structures, measures and resources. I probably need a first round with the Secretary of State to make sure that that vaguely lines up, but I would love to come back here and talk about that in a bit more micro detail.

The two observations are that, first, we have to lay out quite explicitly what we are doing to move the dial. There is great work on, for example, the small but very nasty sites, including the sharing of child sexual images and porn. Then, the big question for me is: how does the average young child consume social media, and how can you change that? Probably, being realistic, that is a change over time and includes cultural change with parents if this is to happen.

When you have more than 80% of 11 to 16-year-olds using social media, frankly, despite the 13-year-old age policy, we are not going to press a button and declare it shut, and suddenly it is. We will have to be set up to make a continuous programme of change. We have to demonstrate, “Here is the whole set of initiatives we are doing.” There will not be one thing. Effective age assurance is vital but it ain’t going to solve everything. We have to say, “There will be waves of change here. We are going to move the dial.”

Crucially, the second thing for me, as well as sharing a plan, is that we have to share some metrics. Using the brilliant research capability that Ofcom has, and dialling into that, let us see what is actually happening out there in the country. What is the reality? One challenge with this whole sector, as I have found, is that there is a tonne of anecdotal experience, including some really awful, genuinely atrocious experience that was really upsetting. You have to say, “Yes, we probably can’t get 100% perfection, but we have to move the dial.” Sharing the metrics is what I would like to come back on and perhaps have a debate with many of you about.

Q4 Dr Rupa Huq: What you have on your plate has become so vast. You alluded to this yourself, from originally postal services and broadcasting to online harms and all these things. Do you think is has got too big and unmanageable?

Sir Ian Cheshire: I won’t comment, but you should probably ask Melanie as she lived through the last seven years and saw that remit expand. We understand, as we build up our work plan, that it is remit by remit. Something like Royal Mail is a new addition. Deep-sea cables will be another. There is data centre security. The question for me, if you did not put it in that digital economy bucket, is where else you would put it. Would you create another one? I do not see resource in one level being bled off to deal with something else. We have added to do online safety, rather than taking it away. Shared comprehension is really important. If you do not understand what is going on, for example, in the digital platform world and how it is building data centres, you cannot really talk about the security side of it. We generally find the cross-cutting piece works. Also, we obviously get economies of scale on things like finance and HR.

Q5 Dr Rupa Huq: One thing we are contacted about all the time, and where people accuse you of taking your eye of the ball, is that in the original Communications Act 2003 you laid down the law on impartiality—quite literally. You have statutory duties with the broadcast code. However, a lot of people say that GB News seems such an anomaly, and to some extent TalkTV as well.

There is the whole controversy about politician presenters. There is a sense that they are all from one side. You have a party leader regularly doing a programme. Then you have X number of MPs from that same party, and the whole roster is very right wing. If you see someone from the other side, they are set up to be pilloried. I think a lot of people think that Ofcom is not doing its job; it just seems blatant. Originally, you were a sort of arm’s length regulator but now this argument of freedom of speech comes up. It has turned into a culture wars and warrior thing. What is going on?

Sir Ian Cheshire: I do not agree with that characterisation.

Dame Melanie Dawes: No, I do not either. It was not until very recently that we had many concerns about due impartiality. We had very few complaints. What we have seen over the past few years is new broadcasters coming on to the scene, innovating and satisfying quite a lot of public appetite for opinion-based current affairs programmes. It is a challenge. We have opened up 24 investigations into GB News over the last few years, and 19 into Talk. Most of those are more recent.

I think Talk has been pushing the boundaries a little bit more in the last few months. We looked at a number of those programmes. We are also clear that Ofcom is a post-broadcast regulator. We do not set detailed rules in advance about who should present which shows or how broadcasters should do it. We leave editors, producers and journalists lots of freedom to be able to do the job—to be honest, in the UK we have an extraordinarily strong news and current affairs ecosystem—and to bring that to the public in new and different ways.

The test, when we look at a programme after it has been broadcast, is: does it meet the due impartiality rules? There, I think some people would like us to move to something with absolutely equal amounts of time. The rules are that you do not have to do that. It is about a due and appropriate balance. With the exception of the rule that politicians cannot present the news, which is a very long-standing one, and our election rules as well, we do not set up-front rules.

Finally, at Ofcom we get asked this question of whether serving politicians should be allowed to present current affairs. We asked the public about it a few years ago. It was a relatively small survey, but people were not entirely clear about this. Honestly, I think it is for politicians to decide what politicians may or may not do. I know the Secretary of State raised this matter recently. Our role is not to look at programmes in advance. As I say, we have always been very sparing in the number of rules we set up front, because of freedom of expression—which is very important.

Sir Ian Cheshire: The question of whether we regulate a programme or channel has been tested in law now. The answer is that, under current legislation, we regulate a programme. On a question of multiple issues and people, providing they put in enough due impartiality—maybe it is two talking heads and one maybe gets a bit more time than the other—I think it currently works. That is unless Parliament feels it is time to change the rules, which obviously it can.

Q6 Dr Rupa Huq: It literally feels like that, though, to anyone watching it. If it was on YouTube I think it would be fine, but just as a public service broadcaster there is a massive lack of balance there. Other channels do it as well. At the weekend, Sky had a Labour Baroness presenting a show, but it would say it will then have a Conservative Baroness the week after. Within the channel, and LBC as well, which has politician presenters, they would claim balance, whereas there is no balance within those other channels. If there is a trade unionist, they are set up as a punchbag.

Dame Melanie Dawes: Just to say: GB News is not a public service broadcaster. It is a licensed broadcaster.

Q7 Dr Rupa Huq: Is it subject to your codes?

Dame Melanie Dawes: It is subject to our due impartiality rules but it is not a public service broadcaster with those additional obligations. Look, I think this is about how you interpret due impartiality.

You initially asked the question whether Ofcom is too big and I would like to say something about that. Ofcom regulates communications services and communications infrastructure. I think the synergies between those things have only grown in the six years I have been at Ofcom. If you think about Google and Amazon, they are cloud providers, infrastructure providers and provide services. They are right at the centre of the advertising value chain. You need to be able to see across that.

I need to have a strong relationship with the senior people at Google, for example, for Ofcom to be able to carry out its job. What you get by having all of that in one place is a lot of synergy. It is way more efficient than if you have a few, smaller regulators, each needing their own general counsel, head of economics and so on, not to mention their own chief executive. For me, it makes a huge amount of sense and we are finding that those synergies only grow every day.

To reassure you, and bring this back to broadcasting, there is no sense at Ofcom that we deprioritise that work just because our remit has grown for online safety. We have the same number of people doing it as we have always had. It is just a debate about whether the rules are fit for purpose for the modern world and the way broadcasting has changed.

Q8 Dr Rupa Huq: Are there different standards for, say, BBC presenters? Gary Lineker got pilloried for his tweets. We know that the directors general from the BBC that we have had in the chair are scrupulous and that people leave their politics at the door when they present. That does not seem to apply for GB News. Even the subject matter is often Reform Party talking points: things like rape gangs, gangs, small boats and Muslims. That is the subject matter as well. Is there a different standard for the BBC?

Dame Melanie Dawes: I think the BBC requires that its staff are extremely careful about how their personal opinions are expressed. That was the debate about Gary Lineker online. It was not an Ofcom regulatory issue and was very much the BBC board’s decision, principles and processes for what it requires of its staff. If the board were here, I think it would say that it is extremely careful about those things because of its role as the BBC, our national broadcaster, and the most trusted news brand globally, with a big reputation to defend.

As far as the broadcasting code is concerned, Ofcom’s job is to apply that across different formats and broadcasters. It is true that a very wide range of different programming is available but our job is to judge due impartiality and sometimes due accuracy on the basis of the programme we see. We allow for variety. As I said, it is not about equal balance; it is about sufficient balance in a programme, with different perspectives.

Q9 Dr Rupa Huq: GB News was fined for the Rishi Sunak election show that was seen as blatantly having no balance at all.

Dame Melanie Dawes: One show is still subject to a court process in fact. We are being challenged on that.

Q10 Dr Rupa Huq: I thought there was a £100,000 fine.

Sir Ian Cheshire: A fine of £100,000, yes.

Dame Melanie Dawes: Yes, we are being challenged on that.

Q11 Dr Rupa Huq: They are appealing it. There has also been Boris Johnson being interviewed by two MPs who are married to each other and from the Conservative Party. That all looked a bit cosy. In what world is that balanced?

Sir Ian Cheshire: If there is an appetite to change the current guide and codes, that would be something to discuss. We have asked the same thing. The board asked the same thing as well as asking whether we are too big, which I think is a reasonable strategic question to ask if we could be more effective. I genuinely think that this is something that we will keep asking ourselves, but I am satisfied, having been round this with the team, that it is applying the code in a consistent way.

I think the channels are very different. I wanted to understand it, so I took time out to meet the people at GB News, to understand their compliance effort. It is fair to say that they started very chaotically, with their technical transmissions all over the place. They have invested in compliance and are moving in the right direction. The sheer fact that we have had 23 investigations into them shows that we are not just sitting there letting them do anything they want.

Q12 Dr Rupa Huq: I think the public feel you are not applying the rules enough, going hard enough and so are letting them get away with it. For example, Andrew Neil left. He is not of the left; he said it was from the nutty end of politics. People like Stewart Purvis and Chris Banatvala—he set up Ofcom in the first place—have been highly critical. They are not of the left.

Sir Ian Cheshire: I would urge them to say that we also have a duty on free speech and freedom of expression, so there is a balancing act.

Q13 Dr Rupa Huq: This seems a new dimension from what was originally set up in the 2003 Act.

Sir Ian Cheshire: I encourage Stewart and the others to set up a left-wing version of GB News.

Q14 Dr Rupa Huq: I would disagree with that if that happened as well, but I will get a pile-on now for saying it. So bring it on. There will be a huge pile-on coming my way now.

Sir Ian Cheshire: I did not see that coming.

Chair: We will move on. Thank you, Rupa.

Q15 Liz Jarvis: It is now nearly a year since the GB News interview with President Trump that prompted multiple complaints about impartiality. Why did it take you so long to decide to investigate?

Dame Melanie Dawes: Sorry, are you talking about the “Panorama” programme?

Q16 Liz Jarvis: The Beverley Turner interview.

Dame Melanie Dawes: Yes, we actually looked into that straightaway. There were in fact two versions of that show. We investigated one and are now investigating the second. We were pretty quick on that, I think.

Q17 Liz Jarvis: As far as I am aware, it took six months for you to launch an investigation. Is that right?

Sir Ian Cheshire: No, it is not.

Dame Melanie Dawes: I do not think so. It may well have taken that long to run it through because these processes take time. You have to ask the broadcaster for information; you have to give them time to respond. There can be a bit of a to and fro. I do not have the timescales in front of me but can happily come back to you on that. I think our initial investigation was quite quick.

Q18 Liz Jarvis: When will a decision be made on that?

Dame Melanie Dawes: We have already decided that one programme was not in breach of the rules, and we are looking at the second. I believe that that is still outstanding.

Q19 Liz Jarvis: What lessons has Ofcom learned from the High Court judgment in the GB News judicial review, and how have those lessons influenced your approach to future broadcasting standards investigations?

Dame Melanie Dawes: There was a court judgment where, again, GB News appealed our decision. That appeal was upheld. That was interesting, because it underlined the point that freedom of expression is a very serious consideration before a court in these cases, and we should not go beyond what is necessary to secure the requirements of the broadcasting code. In that case, the question was whether a programme could count as both news and current affairs, if it had a news bulletin in it and it was a current affairs show, and therefore could we apply the rule about politicians presenting news in the way that we had done in that case?

The court said no. We consulted on changing the rules. In the end—we concluded this a little while ago—we decided to keep the rules as they are but strengthen our guidance in a number of ways. We broadened the definition of “politician” so that it can include a slightly larger group of people. In the end, we rest on that principle of due impartiality, so that if you have a politician presenting a current affairs show, you have to work that little bit harder to make sure the public get the range of views that they are entitled to expect under the code.

Chair: Thank you. Let us move on to Chi.

Q20 Dame Chi Onwurah: Thank you, Dame Caroline. It is a great pleasure to be here, and to see Sir Ian again and Dame Melanie.

Sir Ian Cheshire: Thank you for the process.

Dame Chi Onwurah: I want to follow on from Dame Caroline’s question. You stressed to us that you understood that Ofcom’s primary duty is to the British public, but you have another duty, which is to support growth in the economy. I know that for many of my constituents and other stakeholders there is a sense that the support for growth, for the investment by the big tech companies, is preventing you, or may be preventing you, from taking the swift action that so many people want to see against harms online. How do you balance those two duties? How can our constituents know that you are balancing those in their interests?

Sir Ian Cheshire: I will give a board view and then some more practical examples. First, I do not see those two in practice being traded off. We have two clear duties. The growth agenda particularly applies to things like digital broadband, notspots and investing in the future, particularly mobile quality, which will be the next big investment cycle that firms will have to do. There is also developing the encouragement of appropriately sustainable data centres and the others bits of physical infrastructure, including spectrum.

Q21 Dame Chi Onwurah: Sir Ian, just to clarify, are you saying to the Committee that you are not aware of any threats from or concerns by large tech investors with regard to the regulatory environment in the UK?

Sir Ian Cheshire: I would say that the experience of people who have never been regulated when they come here and find that they are is that they are not big fans of being regulated full stop. What they say and do, we cannot control. I will not be worried about that. It is quite clear that we have an economic duty, which the Government are very clear on. I think that with the investment in fibre that has gone on, and the upgrades coming in mobile, we are absolutely underpinning the growth of the digital economy in the UK. We are very clear on that.

However, the question of the overriding duty to our citizens is absolutely paramount. On the harms issue, there can never be a trade-off that says we will turn a blind eye to harms because we want more investment and growth. That is not going to happen.

Dame Melanie Dawes: Yes, and our primary duty is to further the interests of citizens and consumers. That is right there in the Communications Act 2003. Then we have further duties. One of those is on growth. There has actually been a carve-out from online safety to the growth duty. We asked for that a few years ago because we did not want to create another potential stick for the tech giants to beat us with in court by saying that maybe we had not taken into account growth.

Generally, I agree with Ian: in most of what Ofcom does, there is a real synergy between growth and supporting consumers. What do consumers need? They need really great communications infrastructure. That is about building fibre.

There was an article this morning in the papers about what the EU is trying to do to get satellite direct-to-device services going across the continent. We have already done that in the UK. Ofcom has already licensed all our mobile network operators so that they can partner with satellite companies to do direct-to-device satellite phone calls. That is a good example where it is great for the consumer and really powerful for growth. We do a lot of that across our remit.

Q22 Dame Chi Onwurah: Can I just clarify that? I have heard threats from tech companies saying that they will not invest in the UK if there is more regulation on artificial intelligence or online harms, for example. Have you not heard those threats?

Sir Ian Cheshire: They have been reported. Do I think people would play that card if they are trying to lobby? I am sure they would. Would we pay attention to them? No.

Q23 Dame Chi Onwurah: You do not pay attention to those threats.

Dame Melanie Dawes: We would not pay attention to them. I should add that we have not had any pressure from the Government on this at all—rather the reverse. They want us to crack on with online safety, definitely not to hold back for the reasons you describe.

Q24 Dame Chi Onwurah: Sir Ian, one commitment you gave to my Committee was to take on the tech bros, which we appreciated at the time—and still do. Meta recently wrote to me saying that, as to the changes and safeguards being implemented in the US in response to litigation there on the mental health of children specifically where they were obliged to change parts of their algorithm etc, they will not be implementing those in the UK. Can we rely on you to ensure that children in the UK have at least the same levels of safeguards as in the US?

Dame Melanie Dawes: We recently opened enforcement action against Meta because they have introduced a new service. Our question is whether they have done a risk assessment. Again, we are already in enforcement with them and many other large companies.

Q25 Dame Chi Onwurah: I asked specifically about the changes being made in the US. Will you ensure that they are brought here?

Dame Melanie Dawes: The changes in the US are voluntary commitments by Meta. They are broader than the Online Safety Act, in that they cover addiction. That is just not covered by the Act, although of course the social media ban for under-16s will be much more draconian for Meta than anything that will come out of the US settlement. It has committed to some other measures, for example on anti-grooming. We have not seen anything there that is not already in our codes. We are looking into it but we think they are already part of the UK system.

Q26 Dame Chi Onwurah: I think you are saying that they are already in the system, but if they are not, and are not covered by the Online Safety Act, you do not have the powers now. Will you ask for those powers?

Dame Melanie Dawes: The social media ban will remove under-16s from the social media services, so if you are concerned about addiction for teenagers, that will achieve a stronger effect. That is the Government’s intent.

Q27 Dame Chi Onwurah: The under-16s ban is for only under-16s, not all teenagers.

Dame Melanie Dawes: No. I think the Government are looking into what the remaining requirements should then be for 17 and 18-year-olds. You are right: addiction is not covered under the Online Safety Act. I think it is a policy question for the Government and Parliament as to whether we want to expand that in future years. The social media ban will go quite a long way, certainly for younger teens.

Sir Ian Cheshire: Rather than just mirror it across, because we have different existing and incoming legislation, as I said earlier, we want to keep looking at this and say, “Is it moving the right way?” If we see holes that we think do not match, in that they are not doing something here and we think it is material, we would be more than happy to talk to the Government again about where we have not got coverage in the legislative capability. Ex that, we are very clear we are trying to achieve at least that sort of outcome.

Dame Melanie Dawes: We have spoken to the Attorneys General in the US who have been pushing this litigation. I think it is very good that the US is doing what it often does best, which is to deal with these problems through class actions at state level in the courts rather than through a regulatory environment. It is just different.

One of the big breakthroughs was with big tobacco. This is all very helpful, but the UK regulatory system is more comprehensive; it has the law behind it, and I think that in the short term we need it to drive progress because, if you rely on voluntary commitments by companies like Meta, it will never go as far as you want.

Q28 Dame Chi Onwurah: My question is almost the opposite. I am saying that there are voluntary commitments in the US as a result of litigation. How will we ensure that UK children and teenagers benefit from those commitments in the UK? I am still not entirely clear that that is going to happen and that Ofcom has been proactive in ensuring it does.

Dame Melanie Dawes: Many of them are in our codes and they have committed to doing stuff in the US that we are already doing.

Sir Ian Cheshire: Yes, they are sort of catching up.

Q29 Dame Chi Onwurah: It is the ones that are not in our codes, like addiction specifically.

Dame Melanie Dawes: With the ones that are not in our codes, that is because they are just not covered by the Online Safety Act. The coverage of the Act is a question for the Government. The social media ban will go part way, and then I think they will be thinking of 16 and 17-year-olds as well. I think the cup is half empty on this for the UK, but it is a different way forward and it will be slightly different from what we are able to achieve. Did I say the cup was half empty? I meant the cup was half full.

Q30 Dame Chi Onwurah: I am not clear about how we get to that point quickly and practically. My next question continues on that theme: the former Secretary of State for Science, Innovation and Technology asked you to “conduct an urgent review of whether Ofcom has the right capabilities in place” to enforce the Online Safety Act. What progress have you made on that review, and will you send this Committee a copy?

Sir Ian Cheshire: It has just been finished, and yes.

Q31 Dame Chi Onwurah: Can you give us a—

Sir Ian Cheshire: As I think I alluded to earlier, I agreed, partly because of contact with the Secretary of State, that I would go back to the now responsible Secretary of State at DCMS to talk through the outcome of that review. It has been a comprehensive, blank-sheet-of-paper start. I think the team has taken the chance to reflect particularly on this inflection point about what we have been doing for the past 18 months and what we can do now. As soon as I have got past that conversation, I will certainly share it with you anyway, but if you want to have a further chat with me or Melanie we are very happy to do that.

Q32 Dame Chi Onwurah: Will it address the Molly Rose Foundation’s criticisms, which mirror other criticisms that we discussed when you came before my Committee, that Ofcom has a “weak and unambitious approach” and does not prevent harmful content being recommended to young people?

Sir Ian Cheshire: I understand Andy’s views on this, but I do not agree with them. I got quite personally involved in the process of the suicide forum and whether we could go to the final business disruption measure and further legal options. Again, that falls for me under a bit of learning, which says that I thought we had more capability of going to BDMs, say, if people did not pay their fines. We do not, legally. If it is geo-blocked, as that site has been, we have exhausted our remedies under the Act.

Now, I believe we should go further, if we can. On that agenda with the Government, the question is: what are the options we can have?
