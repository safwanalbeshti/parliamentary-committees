John Dunne: That is our bread and butter. We work with councils, and we do not start working with councils to replace their current internal service. We are enhancing their internal capacity. I will give you an example of how that has worked in a small rural council, Rushcliffe Borough Council, which we have been working with since 2021. It had a major fly-tipping issue because it was so rural that criminals were coming over the border from nearby councils, and they were dumping their waste around Rushcliffe Borough. Due to capacity issues, the council was just sending the cleansing team out and clearing the waste on the same day. It was solving the problem, but all it was doing was putting a plaster on it because these offenders would just keep offending within the borough.

Where working together worked really well is that we got in between the complaint and the cleansing. The complaints would come in to us and we would go out, investigate the waste that was being dumped and inform the council. The cleansing team would come out and still remove that waste but then we would have evidence. It would then lead us to a doorstep or a business, and then we would investigate and take enforcement action against that. In the first 12 months it reduced complaints by 60%. The downside to that—we have evidence to the fact—is that all we did was push them over the border to the next council. Once we started working with some neighbouring councils, we would come across exactly the same offenders doing exactly the same things.

Working together works extremely well in that type of scenario, and it is a genuine partnership. When you are working with a private company such as ours, it also needs to be transparent. The council needs to be able to see everything that is happening so that should we take any enforcement action, immediately it can see the body-worn camera footage of the officer, the penalty or the enforcement action that was taken, the witness statement, and the notebook entry of the officer. It allows the council to performance manage that private company the same way that it would performance manage its own company.

The barrier is on the data side of things. As a private company, we need access to these offenders and past offenders, waste contracts, the council tax register, the electoral roll—so that we can confirm the identity of offenders—and the same police support that the councils get. That true partnership can then come together from there because the problem is across council borders and not just within a particular council.

Alexander Jan: I agree with everything that has been said. Perhaps a bit more structurally, there are two or three things that we could do to help address the problem. If I may say so, the problem was elegantly articulated and it is one that many people will recognise, particularly in our major cities. I know everybody is talking about devolution. After 30 years of asking for it, our ship has come in and we will see what happens. But there is a genuine issue around the devolution agenda here.

If you look at the legislative powers of, say, New York City compared with any major local authority—Birmingham, Liverpool, London local authorities—they are orders of magnitude greater. You could argue they are too great. However, with the mayor and the five boroughs, its ability as an authority to legislate on issues we have been talking about is a real thing. It can decide to reduce the number of commercial waste providers in its city, to franchise it, to containerise waste, or to bear down on the roadworks problems.

Here that does not happen because those powers are held centrally. Of course, because different areas have different priorities, needs, stresses and strains, trying to get the attention of senior civil servants and Ministers to think about problems that might be quite chronic in one area, when they are not really affecting other parts of the country in great numbers, is a real challenge. If you take the pedicab example—I realise pedicabs are not street clutter as such but they are moving street clutter—it took 10 years for that legislation to get passed.

There is something we could do there, whatever form local government is going to end up with, with the reorganisation that is or is not happening. Strengthening powers for local authorities on those sorts of issues—within a framework, with safeguards—would be a very powerful thing.

I will quickly give you one example. We saw in the last 10 years the explosion in the provision of fibre networks. That meant the same streets were dug up four or five times by different fibre networks. By the way, a number of them have now gone bust and are now being bought out by the others. I am sure that Ofcom economists—good people—probably thought, “This is fine. This is the market. This is creative destruction”, whatever you want to call it. The impact of the externalities of that process on everybody who lives, works and visits those streets was very high. In a more rational world, such as in New Zealand or parts of the United States, believe it or not, they have a “dig once” policy that says, “All right, you all want to put your fibre networks in, so you’ll dig one trench, everybody piles in, and then it’s done”. That is a good example of how central government needs to respond more to the local needs of the people in the areas we are talking about.

The other thing is that we cannot get away from the issue of resourcing. I know every time people appear in front of committees they will say, “The Government need to give everybody more money to solve problems”, but it is a real thing here. I left my crib sheet behind, which probably means I will be more brief than I would otherwise have been. But if you look at Islington, it has a budget of £500 million a year.
