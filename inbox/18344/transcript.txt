Examination of witnesses

Katie O’Donovan, Tim Scott and Rebecca Stimson.

The Chair: Good afternoon and welcome to this meeting of the Communications and Digital Committee. Today we are taking further evidence on the Online Safety Act and I would like to welcome our witnesses, who represent some of the largest companies regulated under the Act. Thank you for joining us.

First, I will ask each of you to introduce yourself by telling us your name, your organisation and your role.

Katie O’Donovan: I represent Google. My job at Google is director of government affairs and public policy.

Tim Scott: I am senior director for public policy for Europe, Middle East and Africa at Roblox. We are a gaming platform.

Rebecca Stimson: I am the director of public policy for Meta.

The Chair: Before we start the questions, I want to speak about the context of this evidence session. Obviously, we are reviewing the Online Safety Act, its implementation and its enforcement. In recent months, Parliament and the Government have reached the point of bringing in a social media ban for under-16s, because we all recognise that children are not safe on the platforms they are using; that is your companies and other companies.

I feel that action was necessary but that, in the longer term, children should not be banned. I think that is the feeling a lot of us have. Instead, online spaces should be safe, inclusive and private for them to use. The committee, in all the sessions we have had so far, has heard a lot of evidence of continuing harm to children and young people. We want to end the market in online hate and harm, and we are going to need action from you to do that.

Technology companies, your companies, should be responsible for what you create and the impacts that it has. The Online Safety Act, as we heard recently from the MPs and Peers who saw the legislation through, had a duty of care at its heart. We will be bearing that in mind, and safety by design is something that is looming very large in our findings.

Ofcom recently found no significant overall change in children’s exposure to harmful content during the first nine months of the children’s safety duties. As I said, we have heard from many witnesses that that is the case. Does that show that companies such as yours are failing to make your platforms safer? I will start with Katie.

Katie O’Donovan: Thank you for the question. At Google—and it was good to hear you reflect on this comment—we believe strongly in safety by design, and that is something that we have been working on for many decades. We have not waited for regulation and legislation in the form of the Online Safety Act. We do think, though, that the Online Safety Act is a very serious piece of legislation. It is a very deep regulation, from our analysis one of the most complex pieces of safety regulation in the world, and with some of the most serious powers if you think of fining and other business measures.

The Online Safety Act is something that we really take seriously. We are fully regulated by it and it has changed the way we operate within Google, both in organisations of our teams and in meeting the requirements of it. There are measures that we have put in place across Google, across search and across our core ways of working that are very much in line with the Online Safety Act and will benefit our users here and more globally. For example, in the UK we are locking on SafeSearch for under-18s. That means that an under-18 cannot access content that an adult would be able to. Where we do not know the age of the user, we use age inference to assure that the user is over or under 18.

For over a decade, we have been enabling people to notify us where there is non-consensual, explicit or intimate imagery discoverable through Google Search. We have updated the way that users are able to do that, so now they can update with images and we can help them find additional images. We are also working with NGOs within the UK to take their hashes of those images.

Those are some of the examples of how we have responded to the Online Safety Act that go beyond just search and YouTube. We are thinking about how the recommendation engine that we use on YouTube elevates good-quality content, reduces low-quality content and reduces the frequency of content that, viewed in isolation, may be appropriate—for example, a fitness video or something around body image—but, viewed in repetitive form, can be harmful.

The Chair: In a single short sentence, on the question I asked, does the current situation where harm is increasing show specifically that companies such as yours are failing to make their platforms safer? I know you are talking about aims and about some features you have introduced, but they are not safer, are they?

Katie O’Donovan: I think our platforms are demonstrably being made safer by the actions that we are taking. The Online Safety Act regulates a huge number of companies, a huge number of services, and we are one of the most frequently used but not one of the highest harms. Through actions we are taking because of the Online Safety Act, and additionally because of our own safety ways of working, our users are increasingly able to use safer platforms.

Tim Scott: Thank you for the question. The Online Safety Act, and the Bill as it went through before that, has taken a long time to come to fruition and the implementation is ongoing. What we have seen from the outset at Roblox is that we take safety as core to our mission. We are a child-appropriate platform. We have users from effectively five years old and up, so we have always had these safeguards in place.

What we have introduced has, to some part at least, been informed by the Online Safety Act, similar to what Katie was saying. It has enabled us to rethink how we apply these mitigations and to continue to develop the safety mechanisms that we have, the innovations that we use, to reflect that growing threat across the internet and the user base that we have.

Over the last 18 months or so, we have introduced things such as facial age estimation and age assurance, which has allowed us to restrict certain features and functionalities for people on our platform. Earlier this year we introduced, for the first time, what we have called Roblox Kids and Roblox Select, so you effectively have two completely different experiences with different functionality and different content put in there. In addition, there is a whole raft of stuff that has been informed by how we have approached the implementation of the Online Safety Act.

Directly in answer to your question as to whether it is safer as a result, I think it is but safety is a continually moving target, shall we say, and something that we are constantly chasing and pursuing. That is why the Online Safety Act is a crucial function in how we respond to it, but also how we look at it globally. We are across 180 countries and have users from all around the world, and having something so detailed and so rich as the Online Safety Act helps us to appreciate how we might apply this in a global setting as well.

Rebecca Stimson: I agree with what the other panellists have said. It is quite early days for the Online Safety Act. The child safety duty has been in force for only nine months, and I know that Ofcom’s report was a survey across a very large number of platforms. I think it would be premature to conclude from that that it has not worked.

Certainly, from Meta’s point of view, we have had a long history of risk management and safety by design. One thing I would add to what the others have said is that one of the biggest impacts the Online Safety Act has had is probably one of the least visible to most people: the risk assessment process has codified into law a very robust and extensive process of risk management and risk assessments that we now have to do on every feature and product that we bring to the UK.

We obviously were doing risk management and safety by design before, but having that, and how extensive it is, ingested by the compliance teams that we have stood up, and then reflected by all the product teams in the company, has made a real step change. We have made a number of specific changes on the platform, which I am happy to go into, as a result of that.

The Chair: We will have further questions on that. It did take Ofcom to issue a public challenge to Meta, did it not, to get commitments from you to strengthen anti-grooming measures, including hiding teenagers’ connection lists and other changes? What we are about is trying to understand what you as companies are prepared to do.

Rebecca Stimson: That was a challenge to multiple companies. We have already had grooming measures in place since 2023, and everyone under 18 has been defaulted into a much more restricted private account experience where it is harder for people they do not know to connect with them. Ofcom felt that we needed to go further. We have agreed with that and we are currently in a conversation with it about what that looks like and how we can ensure we balance people’s ability to use the platform as a social network with not connecting them to people they should not be connected to and putting them in harm’s way.

We are going to say some more about that very soon, but we absolutely accepted that challenge to build on what we were already doing to prevent grooming on the platform.

Lord Storey: Good afternoon. Your companies, of course, are continually developing your products with new features, presumably to increase user engagement. Are you applying the same ambition and resources in innovation in online safety or simply trying to do the minimum to treat compliance correctly?

Rebecca Stimson: Over the last few years we have spent upwards of 30 billion on safety and security. We have 40,000 people working on this throughout Meta. Facebook is now over 20 years old, one of the oldest around, and we have had rules from the very beginning. We do not take the requirements in the Online Safety Act as a ceiling. They are a helpful floor, and we can talk about some ways that we have already gone further than what is required in the codes. For example, we already limit features for certain teens in the teen accounts; that is not required. You can also see that Ofcom is going further than what is in the code.

In practice, we certainly do not interpret it as a ceiling and it is not being acted on as a ceiling. Part of what makes people enjoy these platforms and services and use them in their billions is that it is a good experience. If we were interested only in maximising time spent, we would not have the same safety features. For example, parents can reduce the time online down to as little as 15 minutes a day on our platform.

It is key for us, and key for how we make money through advertising, that the experience on our platforms is as good as it can possibly be. It is key to how we want our services to work.

Tim Scott: Absolutely, I think the investment that we have always made and continue to make in safety functions, functionality and features is up there with everything else that we invest in. As Rebecca says, there is no incentive for a bad experience, particularly in the games industry, which is fiercely competitive. There is a lot of choice in the games industry for people to move around and play different games on different consoles, different platforms et cetera. We absolutely do that.

Some of the changes that we have introduced over the last 18 months, which I have briefly referred to before, actually do have the effect of slowing things down and preventing people having a completely open environment and experience. Perhaps that is not what they were originally looking for. One of the things that we have noticed about the Online Safety Act, and this will be a bit of a slow burn—Baroness Keeley briefly mentioned the social media ban in her opening remarks, for example—is that it is having the effect of informing everyone, from you guys here to parents and kids, that safety is a really important thing. So if I cannot just run around and do whatever I want in this online environment, maybe that is for the better. People are now beginning to appreciate that being looked after and being kept safe is a positive way to experience the internet and being online.

Katie O’Donovan: Yes, I think much of our safety work pre-dates the Online Safety Act and those requirements. One of the things that is really important to us is making sure that safety is by design. At the product iteration stage, not only do the engineers have access to our principles, whether they are AI or safety or child safety, but they also have access to experts who can help with the design phase. That then continues through every stage of the company through to a very significantly resourced trust and safety team, which makes sure that we are implementing our own policies and standards and working on safety. As you can see from action that we have been taking for decades on child sex abuse material, NCII or thinking about the guidelines of content that is and is not allowed on YouTube, that is a long-term commitment from us.

Lord Storey: It is the duty and responsibility of us all to make sure that children and young people are protected from harm. I have used the example before that when I was a head teacher you would not go into a school that was unsafe for children. As we have sat here and been taking evidence, I have been quite shocked by the number of children’s charities or organisations that deal with children and say, “All right, some things have changed but really it has not made a difference”. That is quite worrying. There is almost a feeling that you—not you personally, you understand—have been taken kicking and screaming into making these changes.

My question is: can you give me an example of a safety measure you have introduced that goes beyond the minimum standards required by Ofcom’s code of practice? Just one.

Rebecca Stimson: I go back to the one I alluded to. We have this thing called teen accounts. Everyone under 18 is defaulted into it. It is private by default and has a range of different features—no livestreaming, no location data, various things like that. One of the things that we have changed as a result of a conversation with Ofcom is that it also wanted what it called safer feeds: not just us addressing illegal harms, terrorism or child exploitation but going further into content that may not violate our policies but would be inappropriate for a younger person to see. Last year, I believe, we rolled out what we call a 13-plus rating. If you think about movie ratings, PG and so forth, it tries to mirror that. It may not be content that we would remove from the platform for you and me, but it is not appropriate for a younger person to see. We have now defaulted everyone into that experience. We have had independent research done that shows they are seeing 70% less of that inappropriate content. That is, as I say, separate from the proactive removal of illegal harms and other things.

Lord Storey: Has Ofcom praised you for this?

Rebecca Stimson: It has praised us for our work on violence against women and girls but it has not commented on that particular one.

Tim Scott: We introduced facial age estimation initially to restrict chat functionality but then to broaden that out to content and functionality, which goes beyond the measures that were aimed at. The Online Safety Act, initially at least, was principally around social media, and as a gaming platform it is a lot more complicated for us to get across. By doing that we are now able to restrict chat between people, but imagine a football game in real life where the teams cannot talk to each other, the referee cannot talk to the teams, and the manager and trainers et cetera cannot talk to anyone. There is no communication going on, and that will be a poorer experience.

What we are trying to do is create environments that are appropriate and safe, and by introducing things such as facial age estimation we can put people in appropriate cohorts and allow trusted friends from parental connections.

Lord Storey: I am glad you are not having the real environment. You might get a shock in terms of language.

Katie O’Donovan: I will share a bit of information about how we deal with non-consensual intimate images. Often they are hosted on third-party websites, so not on Google Search itself but Google can be used to direct to those images. We now have a very sophisticated system where users can upload their own images and we will take a hash of that image, a digital fingerprint, and block access to that content across the internet.

We also will extend that to wider information around you that is online so that you can have a regular update on that. We will automatically remove those images if need be, if that is what you request, and we will take the hashes from StopNCII.org within the UK, we will quality-control those and then remove those too. We are really taking an approach that is holistic in removing that content and keeping it down.

The Chair: I have a follow-up question to you, Tim, on the protections that you have described. Why did you not bring them in sooner? Along our line of questions, why not bring in sooner? I think Ofcom finds that in Roblox you reach 61% of UK eight to 14 year-olds in a month. That is a very large proportion of our young children who are online. Why is everything so late, if you like? We have had the Online Safety Act for three years.

Tim Scott: It is a great question and there are a number of elements to the answer, the principal one being the development and pace of change of the technology. A number of the innovations that we have put in on safety that I have described briefly would not have been possible 12 years ago or 18 months ago. By keeping up with that technology, innovating it ourselves et cetera, we are now able to do things such as deploying facial age estimation. We were the first games company to come out and do that voluntarily. The accuracy, which is always improving, for the under-18s is more accurate than for the over-18s. Bear in mind that not that many under-18s have any other form of ID. The level of accuracy now is about 1.4 years, which means a 10 year-old might come out at 11 and a half at best.

The principal reason is that technology is now allowing us to go a lot further. Safety is not an afterthought or something that we have just employed now; it has always been there. We are a child-open platform, as it were, five and up. Compared with some of the other companies that you will talk to, we have always had that safety as a foundational thing. We do not have things such as end-to-end encryption. We do not have video sharing, file sharing, image sharing or anything like that. We have intentionally kept out all those things, which we see as risky and which previously we were able to deal with.

Baroness Fleet: Good afternoon. I would like to ask a question of each of you in turn. Perhaps we could start with Rebecca and then move to Tim and Katie. I would just like to hear from you what specific evidence you can demonstrate that your current age-assurance systems are ensuring that children receive appropriate protection on your services. This is your starter for one; we are coming back to some of the detail. You will be aware, Rebecca, of the Ofcom report on the teen accounts, which did show that the accounts receive algorithmic recommendations of self-harm and potentially harmful body images. How exactly are you dealing with this? What have you recently added that makes your age assurance more effective?

Rebecca Stimson: It is a huge challenge accurately identifying the age of children. This is why we take a multilayered approach and we have done for some years. We work with Yoti and we will direct young people to use its facial estimation technology that is very accurate. We will have social vouching, by which we mean a parent or a guardian. We will have AI detect young people who we think are too young. Similarly to what Tim was just saying, recently the AI technology has advanced sufficiently that we are now able to use it for actual detection of people’s ages on the platform where they may have lied about it. We have a good track record of investing in age estimation.

It is difficult, and there is no escaping the fact that telling the difference between a 13 year-old and a 14 year-old when there is no formal identification is quite a challenge. You will have seen in our submission to the committee that that is why we think expanding this out to include age verification at device levels and operating systems is a key part to add—not to remove anything that we are already doing—and a key bit of the jigsaw that is currently missing.

Tim Scott: Very similar. We use Persona as an organisation to do the video selfie, effectively. I actually did it just before I came here to link a parental account and it literally takes seconds to do, is pretty non-invasive and allows you to just get on with the flow pretty much straightaway.

On top of that we look at the signals that are happening in play—what time people might be playing, for example, or what experiences and interactions they are doing. If we spot that there is something anomalous going on there, we can go back in and say, “Hey, do this again or upload an ID verification” or something different. We are using that multilayered approach to understand more about our users while respecting as much data minimisation as we can.

Baroness Fleet: It is interesting that you have to be pushed to do it.

Tim Scott: Well, as I said before, prior to the last 12 months or so the technology was not there to be relied on, and it is really difficult for an eight year-old to have any other form of ID. We know that parents are time poor, potentially, or just not as engaged as others might be, so we are working with the technology and making it as non-invasive as possible to keep people as protected as we can.

Baroness Fleet: Are you suggesting that it is the parents’ responsibility?

Tim Scott: Not remotely, no; it is a shared endeavour. This is my previous point about one of the unspoken values of the OSA: it is creating a far more informed audience, whether that is people in this House or the general public as well.

Baroness Fleet: Thank you very much. Katie, I would like to hear your view and to follow it up with a particular question.

Katie O’Donovan: Okay, no problem. We also take what we call a waterfall approach to age assurance and age verification. At account set-up we ask people for their age. Now, if the person puts in an age that is under 13, that becomes an account that can be created only with parental engagement, what we call a family link account. Then through a family link account a parent can determine the access to apps or websites or what time the child may spend on their device.

We then use age assurance to determine the age of an unknown user—and that can also be for someone who has declared that they are over 18 but we do not believe that the signals are demonstrating that. Across Google accounts you might have a search history or a YouTube history, which can give a really accurate indication of how old someone is. We are finding that highly effective, and it is fully scrutinised by Ofcom.

Then there are instances when we will ask for hard age verification. For example, on YouTube if somebody has created an account and is uploading content, and we believe that content actually is coming from somebody who is under the age that they should be—it might be animation or something like that—we will ask for hard age verification, which is government-supported ID. If they cannot provide that, we remove the access to that functionality and that content. We think that is the right way to do it.

Ofcom has also talked about the responsibilities of app stores in the age verification process. We run the Play Store, which enables people on Android devices to download specific apps. We will not allow access to over-18 apps if there is not proven age verification on that. We are also developing a signal that we can send developers, used on our age assurance, that gives them an indication of how old the user may be. We are clear that that is an important signal that we send and is useful for developers, but once somebody has an app, that app can contain a wide variety of things. It might be information about a community litter pick-up and it might be information about self-harm. Therefore, while an app store can send helpful signals, we also think it is very clearly the responsibility of that app developer to make sure that they understand their own user and are doing their own age verification too. That is important for apps that are downloaded from the app store and increasingly apps that are downloaded from web browsers.

Baroness Fleet: It is interesting that YouTube is continuing to recommend harmful material, including things such as thinspiration—encouraging anorexia, for example—which can often lead to suicide. How can you possibly want to have that material accessible at all? Do you not recognise the damage that you are doing to young people with making that material available?

Katie O’Donovan: We have really clear guidelines on YouTube where we will not allow content that encourages anorexia or thinspiration or other content like that. We also have worked with academics, parents and families, in the UK and globally, to institute guidelines so that content that is recommended will not be content that is in those categories that you cited. There can be content where one video is a fitness video talking about weight loss and that might be appropriate on its own, but watching 10 videos like that really will not be appropriate and could lead to some of the challenges that you described.

Our recommendation engines have now reduced the frequency of those videos being reviewed, but we would not allow content on the platform that encourages anorexia. We publish quarterly transparency reports that talk about the content that we take down. Much of that is found through automated reviews, but people can also flag that content to us.

Baroness Fleet: You could take down more content, could you not?

Katie O’Donovan: If you look at our quarterly transparency reports, we take down a huge volume of content. What is really interesting is that over the years, as our tools have developed, we are taking down more and more content that has fewer than 100 views. The granularity and the functionality of the tools that are able to do that will remove that content when very few people have seen it. That is increasingly effective, coupled with very clear guidelines of content that we will not allow.

Lord Knight of Weymouth: I have a bunch of questions so I will try to be pithy, and pithy answers would be appreciated. Just quickly, Katie, why is 13 the right age for family link? The Government want a social media ban at 16. Why 13?

Katie O’Donovan: Thirteen is the digital age of consent in the UK and that is our grounding age, but what we do with family link is that when someone turns 13, the parent decides what protections they want to continue and what protections they are happy to remove or reduce. For example, there are many apps that you just cannot download as an under-13, as you would expect. I think we all know 13 to 18 year-olds, or 13 to 16 year-olds, who are able not only to improve their education but to discover a world in a way that none of us was able to. They are able to find a community that they might relate to. Rather than thinking about the age, it is thinking about the product, the design and the feature, and it is very much insisting, as the OSA and the social media ban will, that those responsibility levels are met.

Lord Knight of Weymouth: I think there was a question about the role of parents and whether 13 is the right age. I was equally upset when the WhatsApp age was lowered to 13 from 16 for similar reasons.
