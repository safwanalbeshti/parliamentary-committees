Could each of you introduce yourself and talk briefly about what the data tells us about changes in cancer incidence among adults under 50?

Dr Rous: Hello. I am Dr Brian Rous. I am a consultant histopathologist by background and a clinical lead at the National Disease Registration Service. My involvement with the data is that I am not actively involved in research but I do a lot involved in the curation of the data in the cancer registry, and with quality assuring it.

The data in this area is very complicated. Whether early onset cancers have increased or not depends a lot on the periods of time you look at and how you break it down by age groups and by cancers. A lot of the published literature has looked at data from either 1995 or 2000 up to today. There are some issues with looking over that longer period because these are rare cancers, so small changes in data can give the appearance of a large percentage increase. We prefer to look for changes over a shorter period of time, from 2013 onwards. If you do that, you get a slightly different picture from looking back at the previous periods. There are good reasons for looking at more recent data rather than at the longer period. There have been a lot of changes in that period.

NDRS is one organisation that covers the whole of England collecting cancer data, electronically, from every NHS trust. We use datasets to collect it. In 1995, there were eight different cancer registries, which were partially computerised. They kept records in filing cabinets on bits of card, and so on. There is some concern that the data might not be entirely comparable to what we collect today. In addition, the classification system has changed, so some things that pathologists decided were cancers in 1995 would not be considered cancers today, and vice versa. If you go back that far, a lot of things considered to be benign are now considered malignant. Depending on which cancer sites you look at, that may have a big impact.

If we look at the 2013 data onwards, we can see definite increases in some cancer sites—bowel, endometrium, kidney, thyroid and pancreas. Some of those might still be subject to some sorts of artefacts in the data. Even within that shorter time period, there have been some changes in classification systems and in clinical practice. Particularly for bowel and endometrial cancers, it is difficult to identify anything that would cause an artefactual increase.

Chair: To clarify the language, when you talk about “cancer sites”, that is different types of cancer, or where they are; and when you talk about something being artefactual that is a change that is not statistically robust.

Dr Rous: Apologies for that. We tend to talk about cancer by site—bowel, endometrium, brain, breast and that sort of thing—but lots of different types of cancer grow within those sites, so that also creates a more complex picture. To use the pancreas as an example, we see an increase in the incidence of pancreatic cancer among young adults, but there has been a change in the classification for a type of cancer in the pancreas, called neuroendocrine tumours, which historically were considered to be benign but are now considered malignant. Some of that change might be related to the change in the classification. So, when I say artefactual, I mean not a true biological increase but an increase from the way we either detect or count them, because of the way classifications changed.

Chair: Okay, thank you very much. We can understand some of the complexities in the data.

Sarah Stevens: I am a public health consultant by background and lead the National Disease Registration Service. The relative increase we have seen is greater in the under-50s, but from the absolute numbers of cancers in that age group it is still predominantly a disease in those over 50.

Brian highlighted some changes in cancer types. We have seen some decreases. The most notable is cancer of the cervix, because of HPV vaccination. We have seen some decrease in cancers of the bladder and of the testes as well. Our data emphasises cancers of the bowel and of the uterus in that period.

For colorectal cancer, evidence from England, which has been covered already, as well as from the UK and internationally, points to a genuine increase in the incidence of colorectal cancer in young adults. There is a question how much of this increase is real. It is not mirrored in older patients, although we should remember that the natural course of illness in older patients is influenced by the fact that we have screening. Colorectal cancer screening removes cases from the population, so the increase in colorectal cancer incidence that we see in young patients might also have been seen in older patients had we not had colorectal cancer screening, which was introduced in England in 2006.

My answer is that, if we take as a premise that endometrial cancer and colorectal cancer are the ones that are perhaps rising most convincingly, our first explanation would have to be the change in the rate of overweight and obesity.
