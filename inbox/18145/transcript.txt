Q194 Chair: As the Minister will know very well, this Committee launched an inquiry in March this year to examine the significant changes to elections policy in the Representation of the People Bill as well as to point out areas where we think the Government might go further. Over the course of this inquiry, the Committee has considered a number of proposals for the Government: voter registration, voter ID and the implementation of votes at 16. We have also asked what more the Government can do to protect our democracy in an increasingly complex online information environment, so we will come to these issues today with the Minister. During this inquiry, the Minister will remember that Philip Rycroft published his significant review into foreign financial interference in our politics, making some important recommendations for Government, and I know that the Prime Minister is also very interested in this area. This is a topic that we will cover this morning.

Minister, thank you for being here. Before I turn to the Government proposals, on behalf of the Committee I want to refer everyone to the financial register of interests. All our declarations are publicly available, so I ask everyone in the room and online to check our register of interests.

I will begin by asking you this: how confident are you that the reforms to the political finance system will address the risk of interference in our democracy as identified by Philip Rycroft?

Florence Eshalomi: Thank you, Chair, and thank you for the invitation to address the Committee this morning. What we are seeing through the Representation of the People Bill is one of the most significant changes in looking at political finance for decades. The recommendation led by Sir Philip Rycroft looked at the forms of keeping foreign and illicit money out of our politics, making sure that any money coming into UK politics is transparent, traceable and from permissible donors. We are also working with the independent Electoral Commission on strengthening any additional regulation that we need on that, looking at any additional fines that the Electoral Commission can impose, strengthening the way in which political parties, individual donors and campaigners register their donations and a range of measures looking at how individual companies donate.

In response to your main question about foreign interference, we know that there are examples of where foreign actors are trying to influence UK politics. I think it is very important for us to be loud and clear that UK politics is not for sale, and it is so important to make sure that any money coming into UK politics is traceable. I think it is fair to say there is no appetite for public funding for political parties but the reality is that politics, elections and candidates all cost money. It is about how you make sure that any money coming into UK politics is through a transparent measure, that we know who is donating to political parties and candidates.

We are looking at making sure we are strengthening the know-your-donor framework, making sure all of us as candidates and political campaigners know there are additional risk assessments for donations over £11,180, which is the current threshold that is permissible from the Electoral Commission, making sure that any illicit ways to subvert campaigns and donations is traceable. That is why we are being very clear about the use of cryptocurrency, which is a new form of payment. This is not about saying that cryptocurrency is bad but it is about saying how do we trace where cryptocurrencies are originating. It is about looking at an annual cap placed on donations from overseas electors.

We feel that a number of these measures will help to trace and ensure that there is no illicit money coming into UK politics.

Q195 Chair: Thanks for that, Minister. It is good to hear the Government confirm they are supporting Rycroft and the Electoral Commission is working with the Government. You mentioned that the Government are looking to implement the know-your-donor framework, essentially requiring political parties to carry out a risk framework for funds. Will some sort of guidance be given to political parties? Each political party will measure what its risk is very differently. How would that be fairly measured and guidance given to political parties so that they know what they need to report?

Florence Eshalomi: It is very clear that during election campaigns you have paid party staff and many people who are volunteers with political parties. It is about ensuring that the rules are clear. It is working with all political parties and their co-operation is really important on this, making sure that all political parties are aware of those changes before any major elections happen, as we have done and successive Governments have done as well. You will be aware, Chair, that the Speaker’s Committee on the Electoral Commission also takes a keen interest in this, with representatives from all the major political parties in Parliament feeding into that. Ultimately, it is about how we clarify existing rules on donations and ensure that the parties are fully up to date with how any new changes are implemented for elections going forward.

Q196 Chair: That is very helpful. If political parties are required to make risk assessments, they need to be given some sort of framework so they know what they are required to do. The Government have said that they are accepting Rycroft’s recommendation—and you have just confirmed it now—to put in place a moratorium on crypto donations. My understanding is that the amendment added at Commons Report does not place the mechanism for lifting the moratorium on the face of this Bill. Why are the Government not making that explicit?

Florence Eshalomi: Just to reconfirm, the Government accepted all the recommendations from the Rycroft review, including the moratorium on crypto donations. Once we have a system in place where the Electoral Commission can work with Parliament to outline a proper mechanism for regulating and identifying cryptocurrency, that will help with protecting the integrity of a donation. The Government’s immediate priority is having that ban in place, and it is about continuing to engage with the EC and other organisations to look at a regulatory framework that will help identify cryptocurrencies in the future. It is also about being clear to say that not all cryptocurrency is bad. A number of people operate in cryptocurrency; it is a new form of payment. It is about getting a better understanding of where the funding is coming from.

As it is now in place, any requirement to lift that ban will require primary legislation, and I think it is right that the Government will come back to the House seeking views and comments, not just from this Committee but from Members right across the House, giving Parliament and wider stakeholders an opportunity to scrutinise that. Given the risk that the Government feel that cryptocurrency currently poses, they wish to keep the moratorium in place until we have a better understanding.

Q197 Mr Gagan Mohindra: Minister, welcome back. On company donations, the Government accepted Rycroft’s recommendation to apply a profit test rather than revenue. However, companies will still be able to donate their profit many times over. Why have the Government chosen not to apply the donation limit to a company’s total political donations in a year, as recommended by the Electoral Commission?

Florence Eshalomi: One of the key things, which I am sure many of us see in our role as constituency MPs, is that many small and medium-sized companies want to donate. That does not mean that they are part of a political party, but they support individual candidates, individual MPs and individual councillors. We also know that for many companies there are periods where they are not making a good profit; there will be peaks, repaying of loans, difficulties and challenges. We think striking the right balance in looking at the post-tax profit over a longer period will help in capturing the permissibility of a company’s finances. It is about closing a loophole for people who are setting up unincorporated companies, shell companies where they can in some cases—and there have been examples—funnel money into the political parties. It is about how we have a political regime and a political finance regime that is robust and able to respond to some of those challenges and make sure that any illicit or foreign money is not coming through.

We have listened to some of the stakeholders and taken on board the comments from the Rycroft review. We feel that by having a longer period looking at actual profits as opposed to a smaller period of two years, a five-year window would help in giving a company credibility and the opportunity to show that they are based in the UK, paying UK taxes, employing UK staff, and making sure that the viability of the company exists as well. I think that on the face of it we have struck the right balance to ensure that there is genuine economic activity in the UK to support genuine donations to candidates, political parties or campaigners.

Q198 Mr Gagan Mohindra: While I applaud the Government’s efforts to make sure we are not creating fake companies and creating turnover and donating off the back of it, the point I was trying to make was about using the same profit level multiple times. To me, that feels like a bit of a loophole around this because if you have a successful company, what is to stop that profit being distributed many times?

Stuart Ison: That is a very good point. That is one of the reasons why we are reflecting very carefully on this and we will be looking to bring forward proposals in the House of Lords to address the challenges that you have just disclosed.

Mr Gagan Mohindra: On the timeframes, in the previous answer you justified why it is five years rather than two. I am fine.

Q199 Andrew Cooper: It is good to see you again, Minister. My question is about the regulated period. For the benefit of the viewers at home, the regulated period happens in the 365 days before an election. As you know, Minister, there are spending limits for the short campaign and the long campaign but political activity happens all year round, whether that is campaigning or donations. Philip Rycroft acknowledged this in his review. The chief executive of the Electoral Commission has talked about the need to set up a regulatory regime that covers the period between regulated periods, not least because foreign interference does not worry too much about when the regulated period is. It happens all the time. Does the Minister consider the current distinction between regulated and unregulated periods remains appropriate in an era of continuous campaigning?

Florence Eshalomi: There are two parts in response to that, Mr Cooper. The regulated period is quite important because it helps with providing transparency around the campaigning period. Many of us in this room know when the short campaign kicks in, when spending limits kick in, but there are also periods where it may feel that a campaign is going on but essentially we have seen over the last few years that there is a lot of activity during the periods outside of a formal election cycle. It is important for us to come back to why some of the key measures in the Representation of the People Bill are outlined. It is about how we ensure that those trying to influence and unduly target UK politics do not have a loophole, do not have a period where they can do that. It is about looking at accountability during the long periods where people may feel that there is not any campaigning going on.

Equally, we must also be careful about not overburdening campaigners. I mentioned that a number of campaigners are volunteers. They are not paid staff. They are doing it because they support political parties or no parties or independent parties. They are doing it because they have an interest in politics. It is about how we make sure that the regulations don’t put off those genuine people and that anyone coming into politics, anyone participating is not hampered or put off by that.

Looking at the current framework set out, we believe that it is the right balance for ensuring that the rules remain fit for purpose. We are looking at how we deliver recommendation 4 in the Rycroft review about the transparency of candidates’ donations in the lead-up to an election outside of the regulated period. There is still a lot more work to be done in that area and that is one of the areas that I think we will definitely come back to in due course.

Q200 Andrew Cooper: Is there a risk that if we don’t get this right we will just end up displacing activity into an unregulated period, in particular from foreign influence?

Florence Eshalomi: On balance, that is one of the things we would want to test with the Electoral Commission. A number of different political parties or people who don’t even see themselves as political candidates are fundraising now. I know that if you go on some pages you will see a donate button. Would that count as election spend? When do you start valuing and quantifying that money? These are all areas that I think we need the Electoral Commission to be a lot clearer on as the independent body that will regulate the funding and donations received by individuals and political parties.

Q207 Ashley Dalton: Welcome, both of you. Minister, earlier you touched on the issue of overseas voters and donors. Recent cases have demonstrated significant legal complexities associated with trying to target donations caps specifically to overseas voters. Can you tell us how and if the new task and finish team will be considering universal donations caps as a potential solution?

Florence Eshalomi: Thank you, Ms Dalton. The Government are still in the process of writing the formal terms of reference and key areas that will be discussed in the task and finish team, but it will be looking at the issue of foreign donations and the loopholes there. I talked about the know-your-donor rules to look at that assessment and risk assessment. There are also the additional measures of the residency test for people coming back to the UK. We understand that many people don’t spend a large proportion of time in the UK. They are overseas, which is fine, but from the day they come back to the UK, are they then donating large amounts to relevant political parties or independent parties?

It is about how we make sure, following on from the Rycroft recommendations and in line with the £100,000 cap, which was the lowest threshold recommendation in the Rycroft review, it is designed to identify any immediate risks. It is making sure that when people come back to the UK they can demonstrate that they have spent the preceding 365 days in the UK before they can donate. Some of these additional measures will help with looking at the donations from people going through. A blanket cap on overseas donors would cause problems with tracing the source of the funding. It is about how we make sure we have measures in place to target some of the larger donations that we see coming into politics such as through cryptocurrencies.

The new task group, which will be chaired by the Secretary of State and the Lord Chancellor, will identify a number of these areas, test out possible scenarios and come back with recommendations to me as the Minister and ultimately back to Parliament.

Florence Eshalomi: There is a lot going on at the moment, Ms Dalton, but because this is a key priority for both me and the Prime Minister, we are pushing colleagues to give us some information on that. As soon as we have any additional information, we will be happy to share it with the Committee.

Q209 Ashley Dalton: Thank you. You talked about what the donations cap might look like and you mentioned the £100,000 lower recommended cap. Some of the witnesses at the Committee, who I am sure you will be aware of, have previously recommended donations caps as high as £1 million to ensure that additional state funding of political parties is not required. Do you have concerns with that approach or any thoughts on that?

Florence Eshalomi: I think it is how we get the right balance and the right funding coming into funding elections. Many of us in this room will hold different fundraisers, events to raise money for our individual campaigns. I think it is testing the level of appetite for public funding in political parties. That appetite is very low at the moment when we look at the situation that the Government and the country are dealing with in funding for key services, public services, key areas. The rationale for a concern about £1 million cap, which seems quite high—I feel that the current threshold is not just in line with the recommendation from Rycroft but it strikes the right balance.

Ultimately it is looking at some of the issues we have talked about with donations coming in during the unregulated periods. A lot more scrutiny of what money is coming in, of companies’ and individual donations and where the funding is coming from will help, but the debate is around where should we put this figure, how much is enough, when you make comparisons to other countries where there are continuous political adverts and no caps or spending limits. The last time the spending limit was increased was under the former Conservative Administration, when the cap was increased to about £1 million I think. We have a healthy balance in the UK, but the questions around the limits will continue to be debated.

Q210 Ashley Dalton: Obviously that limit does continue to be debated. The Secretary of State has asked the Electoral Commission to recommend a lower spending limit. The Committee on Standards in Public Life made a recommendation in 2021. What is the rationale for another review to make a decision?

Florence Eshalomi: The Committee on Standards in Public Life reported in 2021, and a lot has changed in those five years. We are seeing new forms of funding coming into political parties. We are seeing new forms of campaigning, online campaigning taking off. We are seeing new parties form. It is not the traditional two-party politics. We are seeing an appetite for how politics are delivered. It is not a process that we take lightly. I think it is right that any major changes are led by proper consultation with all views being fed in. It is right that the independent Electoral Commission and other expert stakeholders come to a conclusion and an outline that we, as the Government, can then take forward.

Ashley Dalton: Thank you.

Q211 Lewis Cocking: Welcome, Minister, and congratulations on your appointment. I want to talk about automatic voting registration. Will this be done across the whole country for everyone who is eligible to be automatically registered to vote before the next general election?

Florence Eshalomi: Thank you for that, Mr Cocking. We have been very clear that across the country there are about 7 million or 8 million people who are either incorrectly registered or not registered to vote at all. Over the next two years we are hoping to work on creating a new automated voter registration system to ensure that more people can participate in our democracy. To help support that, we are hoping to have different methods tested in different parts of the country to look at a form of an automated system, looking at how we work on existing technology, new technology, to help improve that, and how any permanent changes will be supported by robust evidence. The Bill as it stands now is hoping to help roll that out across the country. I think the Government have been very clear that we are hopeful and trying to move towards an automatic voting system before the next general election. As we all know in this room, Mr Cocking, we do not know when the next general election is. If we are working on the cycle of the current Parliament, the next general election will have to be held before August 2029. We are working with local authorities and the Electoral Commission to have a system in place, hopefully before that date.

Q212 Lewis Cocking: If this Parliament goes the full term until 2029, can you guarantee then that the whole country will be automatically registered or not?

Florence Eshalomi: We are working towards the ambition as set out in the last Labour manifesto of having a system of automated voter registration across the country before the next general election.

Q213 Lewis Cocking: You do not envision having a general election where some people have been automatically registered to vote and other people have not. If that does become the situation, can you not see how the public could perceive that as being unfair?

Stuart Ison: I completely understand your question and your concern. For a bit of context, taking a step back on this a little bit, first, arrangements for automated voter registration are incredibly complex. If you look back at the Australian example, in the context of a compulsory voting system it took them over a decade to introduce an automated voter registration system.

What we are trying to do is to build towards that and take some important steps to improve voter registrations for the reasons the Minister has set out, the very high levels of either incorrect or no registration at all. Your concern is very much one about doing it in certain areas and not others, which I understand. That is why we want to test automated registration in a range of different areas, different demographics, different geographies, different locations, different types, testing rural and urban, and so on. We will try to ensure that we protect these arrangements, and to inform outreach with local authorities who may want to pilot, is to develop a set of criteria with the Electoral Commission to ensure that when we do this, it is done in a fair and balanced way.

We also want to talk to political parties about this because we understand the concerns that you have raised, Mr Cocking. They are entirely understood. We will be working with the Political Parties Panel, which the Electoral Commission chairs, to make sure that we are listening to the political parties as this is rolled out. There is a lot of work to do. There is some uncertainty about it in the sense that we are trying to use data in very innovative ways, pooling data from across Government to make this happen. There is a lot of work still to do.

To finish, there is also a lot that can be done within local authorities already. We are conscious that people will move frequently within local authorities, sometimes in the same authority, and there is data that is held there where people have already registered at one address and then have moved, and therefore their registration is not valid any more. We think there is more that can be done within local authorities to address that as well. Forgive me, a lot there, but there is a huge amount of work going on.

Q214 Lewis Cocking: You are not answering my question, and you do not give me any comfort that we could not have a general election where some people are automatically registered and some people are not. The point I am trying to make is that it will take a while to get this running. We need to make sure it works. We are protecting the electoral register here. It is important we get this right.

Q215 Lewis Cocking: For complete clarity then, if we have the next general election in 2029 and half the people, so half of that 8 million, let us say 4 million, have been automatically registered and 4 million have not, would you perceive the result of that general election as fair or unfair?

Stuart Ison: As I said, what we are trying to do is to make sure that, over time, we are building and improving the way in which voters are registered. I simply cannot comment specifically on the question you have asked, but the process will be there, and we will be engaging with you and with colleagues, and indeed with this Committee, to make sure that you understand and are fed into the way in which those pilots are taking place.

Florence Eshalomi: I think one of the other issues is that some of us will speak to people on polling day who say, “Yes, I do want to vote”, but then they will find out that they are not on the electoral register. That is the main aim. It is not a case of the aim being to look at areas to have more people on the electoral register; it is how we get a fair balance across the country.

As an example, we have a new initiative starting this month where, for anyone renewing their driving licence, as you are filling out the DVLA form, there is a box that will say, “Do you want to be added on to the electoral register?”

How do we make it easier for people to be automatically registered to vote? In answer to your specific question, what we are doing at the moment is inviting local authority areas to be part of the pilots and to come forward. We will be working with the Electoral Commission, making sure that there is a fair spread, a geographical spread across the country—coastal areas, rural areas, urban areas—so that we can get a better understanding of how we can make sure that this automatic voter registration works in practice before we then roll it out across the country.
