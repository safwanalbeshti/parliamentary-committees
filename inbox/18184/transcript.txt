The Chair: Thank you very much for those introductions. We will move straight on into questioning with Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson.

Q27 Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson: Thank you so much for being here with us this morning. I want to start with a big picture question. Our committee has already heard a lot about the extensive issues in the children’s social care system and the challenges faced by children in care. We have also heard about the very poor outcomes regarding educational attainment, mental health difficulties and employment for care-experienced children.

Could you tell us what you think the hallmarks of a good social care system for children in care would look like? What should the system be targeting? What are the benchmarks? What does good look like for this cohort of children?

Nicola Smith: The simple way of viewing this is that these are children of the state: the state becomes their parent and, as their parent, we should want the very best for them. We should have the same aspirations and ideas for these children as parents up and down the country have for their own children.

In terms of how that looks, we really need to address the record numbers of children in the care system. While some children will always need to enter children’s social care and that will always be important, we also need to think that perhaps some children are entering the system because we are not doing enough to support their families earlier on. Can we do more to make sure that all children who can safely stay with their birth families do stay with them? A good social care system would definitely do that.

As I said, when it comes to the children who enter the care system, we are trying to do the same for them as any parent would do for their own children. In that sense, we need to make sure that they are in the right homes, with the right love, with the right care, for the right amount of time and with the right amount of support and opportunities that we would expect. This ensures they are able to participate and achieve in education at similar rates to children who are not in the care system.

When those children become young people and move into adulthood, we must support them at that stage, just as many parents do. When their children get to 18 and they go off to university, their parents are on the end of the phone to support them; we need to do the same for children in the care system. We need to give them that support and what they need to move into adulthood, so that they do not face higher rates of homelessness, higher rates of entry into the criminal justice system or higher rates of suicide and things like that. That is the overall vision as I see it.

Jessica Ford: I really agree with what Nicola said. Broadly speaking, we are on the right track with the reform programme. The Families First model spans the entire spectrum of the system. Obviously, we are looking at providing better support for children to remain safely within their families and whole family support.

From there we move into the kinship space for children who are unable to remain with their families, where there is much better support for kinship arrangements and crucially that they are sustainable over time. We must ensure kinship carers have what they need, so they are not doing this in the face of total adversity because they are committed to caring for the child, but that it actually works for the whole family in that situation.

For children who need to go into care, the Families First model prioritises fostering for the majority of children. However, crucially—this is a really key message that I want to land today—there must be recognition within that that fostering cannot work for all children. It works for the majority but not all. For those children who need to go into residential care, we need to do a lot more thinking about what that care looks like. These are largely children with high and complex needs who need a lot of therapeutic input. For me, that part of the system needs to be much more squarely focused on therapeutic care and outcomes.

Equally, that Families First model needs to go beyond that, so we are thinking about how children can return home. We need a lot more post-removal support for families. Where children go into care and the prospect is that they will remain there for some time, we cannot say, “Okay, job done; they’ve gone into care. We know they’re safe and we know they’re getting the support they need”. There needs to be continuity of work with the family. This would ensure that, for cases where reunification is appropriate, it becomes possible because the work has been done with the family. Eavan and I work on reunification policy a lot together and I am sure she will say more about that this morning.

As I said, we are on the right track, broadly speaking. It is just that we are at the very start of this process and we are essentially unpicking large-scale system dysfunction. The system has been designed for younger children and for harms within the home. However, I am sure the committee has heard from the DfE that we now have an ageing cohort within care. We are looking at adolescence a lot now, and in that space we are talking about extra-familial harms—harms outside the home. How do we get good outcomes for those children in a system that was not really designed for them? We need far more specialist expertise in adolescence, alongside professionals who are on the front foot of emerging teenage trends.

Another crucial thing for me—this comes back to what Nicola was saying—is that we need to look much more holistically at outcomes, particularly for children in care. We need to look not just at their experience of care, which is largely where Ofsted’s focus is, but at what their life looks like outside of that. What does it look like at school or in relationships with their existing community? We then build up a picture that allows us to look at the child’s journey over time, rather than just looking at a snapshot in time, which is what the current system and data in it largely do. We need to look at the trajectory: where have they moved on to from a starting point? We are quite good at doing this in the education space but are not there yet with children’s social care. Moving away from that snapshot in time is a key point.

An ideal system, achieving better outcomes, would be much better at capturing subjective experiences, and this is really hard to do well. I know that Ofsted and the DfE are trying, but in a lot of cases it is a bit of an add-on. It is a bit of, “Oh, let’s go and talk to some children and some parents and carers”, and then you get into a whole issue around inclusivity, representativeness, and the accessibility of those opportunities to engage with those children and families and carers, particularly where there are complex needs or there are communication needs; or you may have a child in an out-of-area placement where the local authority is being inspected and actually that child is now out of that local authority area. It needs to be a very focused effort where what we refer to in the sector as participation is recognised a specialist skill and is not just an add-on. It is not just about having a board of children and young people who meet quarterly. We need to be a bit more upfront about how tricky it can be to obtain that qualitative, child-centred data. In a system at the start of immense reform, that data can quite easily get left behind because it is very hard to do well, and in all honesty we struggle with it in the sector as well.

Eavan Mckay: I agree with everything that has been said so far. A system that works well and is delivering good outcomes for children is going to be one that is focused on prevention at every stage of the child’s journey. Jess set some of that out just now. At the moment, we are in the process of delivering wide-scale children’s social care reform, and there is a lot of hope and optimism in terms of the ideas behind that reform of building a more preventative system.

Earlier this year our charities, together with the Children’s Society and the NCB, jointly released the Children’s Services Funding Alliance report, which we have done for the past—I cannot even remember how many years now.

Jessica Ford: Yes, I think it has been five years.

Eavan Mckay: That report showcased what we have seen over previous years: currently 80% of children’s social care funding is being spent on late-stage intervention and crisis support. There has been around a 40% reduction in early support funding for those early preventative services over a 10-year period. That is really concerning.

We have these reforms that are really focused on this Families First model that is investing in preventative earlier support. However, it is going to take time for those to filter through the system for us to actually see the impact of those reforms and for that to impact the number of children who are reaching crisis point. We need to be realistic about how long that will take and talk about the level of investment required so we can really see those changes come through into the system.

I would also say that neglect is a really illustrative example of where the system does not work in a preventative way and is not targeting harm early enough. In 2024, we produced research where we spoke to local authorities about neglect and multi-agency professionals, including social workers, told us that often they feel very confident in spotting the signs of neglect but, because of the way the system is structured, often those incidents do not reach that threshold of intervention. Essentially, they are told that they need to wait for that harm to escalate before support can be put in place. That is really wrong for children and families because we are allowing them to stay in harmful situations for a protracted period of time. It is also really awful for the system, because we are allowing families to get to a crisis point that requires later-stage intervention, skewing the funding and creating awful situations for those children and families. That is a really good example of where there should be more focus: the current reform programme does not have a specific focus on particular harms, such as neglect, or how it is going to tackle that through greater investment and greater workforce upskilling.

One of the things we have talked about is looking at the definition of neglect. At the moment, the definition stipulates that neglect needs to be assessed as persistent. That is really difficult for a practitioner to observe, because it is often a cumulative harm; there is a kind of drip-drip effect in terms of the harm that is building for that child. The definition is wrong to say that support cannot be given until it reaches that point of persistence because we are losing opportunities for early support to be in place.

Jess also mentioned other areas that I would absolutely echo. The preventative model and this idea of working with families in a holistic way is really honing the belief that I think we all have that, wherever possible, children should stay with their families if the support can be in place to allow that to happen safely. Those principles really need to be applied at every single point in the system. There is a lot of focus on how we can prevent children entering care, and that is absolutely right but, as Jess mentioned, what can we do to say when a child could safely return home? How can we make sure that is possible?

At the moment, when children return home—this quite often happens in an unplanned way, or even if they are supported to return home in a planned way—local authorities are telling us that they struggle to provide and sustain long term support. We produced joint research with Action for Children in 2024 showing that local authorities want to provide more support to families before and after a child returns home, but they simply do not have the resources to do so.

This is a real problem because essentially what is happening is that children and families reach this cliff edge where they return home, the support is then cut off and of course the problems that resulted in them going into care in the first place could well resurface. That return home then breaks down and they return to care. We know that that is happening from the data we have: return-to-care rates are really high and that is worrying.

There is also a really good economic case for the Government to pay more attention to this. We commissioned Professor Lisa Holmes to do some research on this issue. We know that the Government are spending around £300 million per year—I will make sure I get the exact figure—on return homes that break down. We also know that, if they were to invest in longer-term support for reunification, that would cost about £67 million. The difference is really stark. That preventative approach needs to be looked at throughout the whole system.

Baroness Shawcross-Wolfson: Thank you so much. Those were incredibly rich answers and I know my colleagues are very keen we start digging in. We have some great material here about what good looks like, but I want to come back to you on one point, Jessica: it sounded like as we do not necessarily have a very clear idea of what good looks like for children with very complex needs. Is that fair?

Jessica Ford: Yes, that is fair. Part of the issue is that we do not always really know what we mean when we talk about complex needs. For me, there are three categories. First, complex needs that are inherent in the child are things to do with health and learning needs. Secondly, complex risks are about harms within the child’s direct environment, whether that is a harm to them or by them. Thirdly, children in complex situations is about their environment and structural issues, such as poor-quality housing, pressures that the family are facing, poverty and all those kinds of things. Those definitions need unpicking a little, but key to that, once you have understood those definitions, is to apply them to the cohort of children who we have in care.

We talk about how care entries have stabilised but, when you look below that data, we know that they have dropped significantly for under-nines and particularly under-fives, whereas the adolescent cohort has increased, particularly at 16-plus. You have to be careful with the data because there are significant kinds of influencing factors—for example, unaccompanied asylum-seeking children are included within the care cohort. However, we know that a lot of this adolescent rise is about extra-familial harms. Over time, we have become much better at identifying those harms, but the care system’s service response to them is not good enough yet.

There is a really critical point to make here about the current drive towards fostering. We wholly support it; it is absolutely the right thing to do and, as a provider of fostering places, we will be part of that attempt to grow places and diversify the foster carer market. However, we have to recognise that, for a lot of that cohort, the foster sector is probably not going to able to meet that sort of need. This is why I am keen for the committee to take away a key point around the importance of looking at children’s homes and not minimising the role that they play within that fostering debate, particularly where we are talking about complex needs.

Whether we are talking about complex needs or complex risks, where there are high needs—they are not adolescents and it is due to neurodiversity, autism, health needs, all those sorts of things that we are all very familiar with—we must recognise that the children’s home sector has a key role to play. It will always have a key role to play, but there is a lot of discussion around having a therapeutic sector. My sense is that there are a lot of providers that are not actually delivering genuinely therapeutic care and that is really critical in this complex needs space. What needs to go before that is really understanding the cohort and understanding what we are all talking about when we say complex needs.

Q28 Lord Johnson of Marylebone: I want to come back to the general description Jessica Ford made of the system, when you said “large-scale system dysfunction”. Could you help us understand whether there was there a particular policy change that contributed to this concerning state of affairs arising or whether it was just a general drift over time? Have there been any one or two specific policy changes that have inadvertently worsened the state of the system?

Jessica Ford: I have not thought about that before; that is a really interesting question. I believe that there has been drift over time. We also have to be a bit careful about what we mean by dysfunction as well. I know there is a lot of media interest and public discourse around deprivation of liberty orders, the rise in the use of unregistered homes, high costs and all of that. Sometimes that type of conversation masks the reality of what is going on in the system and particularly on this high costs point. As I was previously implying, the care requirements for a lot of the children who are in the cohort now will have high costs. That is what the requirement is and we need to make the distinction between what is a justified high cost and what is not. In order to do that, we need better data and I know that the DfE is working on that. It is wanting to develop a dashboard where you are able to do like-for-like comparisons across different placement types. The dysfunction has certainly emerged over time.

In the care system, the market has been left to do what it wants. I do not think there is enough national oversight of what the market looks like, such as the inherent risks in it. The ability to shape the market as you would want for the current cohort of children just has not existed and we are now doing that very late. The market reforms that have been proposed are very comprehensive and they will get us to a better place, but obviously that does not do anything for the children currently in the system.

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: In your remarks, your description of the dynamics within the age groups of the children in care was interesting. I have not seen that in the briefing material that we have had so far—that the younger group populations are stable or falling, and adolescent children are increasing as a proportion of the overall mix. You also said that the percentage of unaccompanied asylum-seeking children was a growing and important part of that story. Could you help us understand the dynamics of what is going on in the older population and the extent to which unaccompanied asylum-seeking children are a part of it?

Jessica Ford: Obviously you get fluctuations over time of unaccompanied asylum-seeking children entering the care cohort, and that tends largely to affect the adolescent cohort. We have perhaps in the past made some assumptions about the extent to which that is driving those increases in the older cohort and given less focus to the non-UASC cohort and the extra-familial harms that are having am impact there. Certainly when we do our Children’s Services Funding Alliance report, there will always be a line in there as a caveat that UASC are included within the data. It can tend to affect the proportion of what type of care is being used so, over time, how unaccompanied asylum-seeking children are coming into the system will then affect the split across the different care types.

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: Could you just give us a sense of the proportions? I am not sure I have a grip on that.

Nicola Smith: Some 9% of children in the care system are currently unaccompanied asylum-seeking children.

Lord Johnson of Marylebone: How has that changed over time?

Nicola Smith: In terms of the percentage, I am not completely sure. I can say that, if you look at the increase of children in care over the last 10 years, the number who are unaccompanied asylum seekers is comparatively small. There are definitely bigger issues at play. If you look only over the last one or two years, a lot of the increase in children in care came from the unaccompanied asylum-seeking children.

Jessica Ford: We can follow up with the committee on the data, if that is helpful.

The Chair: I was going to ask you to do that, please.

Q29 Baroness Redfern: I have a question on social worker instability. I see that 24% of young people experience three or more different social workers in one year. Do we have difficulties in recruitment in certain areas? It just seems quite a lot for a child to have three social workers in a year.

Eavan Mckay: There are definitely difficulties in recruitment and retention; that is absolutely agreed. I would separate it into a couple of different issues within the context of the current reform programme at the moment. For example, we are really supportive of the new family help model that is trying to address the fact that we need fewer assessments and fewer handovers of care. There needs to be less unnecessary thresholds that determine who is working with a family and what type of support they receive. We strongly support the idea of having a more streamlined system, one of the effects of which could be having fewer social workers or fewer workers who work with a family, so that they have more consistency. That is really helpful.

I will let others talk about retention issues, but one thing that we have thought about in relation to that change is that we know that we need prevention across the whole system, as I talked about at the beginning. Actually in some local areas we have spoken to there are real issues around skills and expertise. For example, previously, early targeted help and early help workers might have been delivered through non-social workers. That new model now—the Government have just consulted on this—will mean that it will all be delivered through Section 17; this should mean that there is social work oversight but essentially there could be non-social workers holding those cases.

We have heard from local authorities that that could place additional pressures on those workers due to a lack of confidence or a lack of skills and they are worried that that could potentially hamper retention as well. That definitely should not be happening; that is not the ideal system for a child or for a family. We want consistency of care. We want it to be relationship-based and trauma-informed. We know from the evidence that, actually, it is far more effective when they have consistency of workers—consistency of relationships—who can really get to know the child’s lived experience and all the pressures on a family. That new model is not what we want.

Baroness Redfern: No, because a child would feel more detached would they not? You will not be able to build relationships and build their confidence if this is what happens in certain areas.

Jessica Ford: I am not sure if the DfE mentioned to you that there is an enduring relationships strategy now, which is all premised on this idea that we need to get better at recognising the importance of continuous trusted relationships. That strategy is 0more focused on children’s relationships with friends, family and members of their existing community. The principle obviously applies across the system; however, the ability to deliver that is very difficult in the face of workforce issues not only because it is the workforce that facilitate that process of finding those relationships—maintaining them and nurturing them—but because those relationships are also directly with the workforce. The issues with social workers around recruitment and retention are replicated across the system.

In local authorities, alongside the social work teams, you also have leaving care teams. There are figures within those teams called personal advisers who work with care leavers in that transition out of care. That role is very variable across different areas, and the case load is very variable. Some of the capabilities around really understanding all the different challenges and risks around that transition to independence and leaving care are not always fully understood. I am not sure that the expertise is necessarily always there across all those life domains. That is how they are referred to. You are looking at health and well-being, relationships, social security, finances, housing and all of that. We need to have a system where those teams and personal advisers really know what those challenges are and what support is available locally, regionally and nationally for those young people to tap into. As with social work, what meaningful work can you really do when you have a high case load?

I think we are going to get on to a conversation about residential care, but I would say within this context that the residential care workforce also needs a lot of attention. The DfE has recently commissioned a residential workforce review that is looking across training, CPD, qualifications et cetera, and I am quite hopeful that is going to support this. Hopefully we can get more into that in the later questions.

Q30 Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Indeed, and that is going to be the area I am going to ask you about. Thank you very much for coming to see us this morning. Two of you have experience working for organisations that run residential care homes. Can you tell us a little your experience? How extensive is your coverage? How much more would you like to do? Why is it so much more expensive to put children into residential care than to have them in foster homes, and numbers are going in the other direction? Jessica, you said that you strongly favoured fostering but the numbers are coming down. I would like to get a sense of why you think that is.

Jessica Ford: In a market where you do not have enough foster placements, a lot of those placements ultimately end up being inappropriate to the child matched to them. You hear a lot about instability in placement, placement moves and out-of-area moves. When you see children eventually going into children’s homes, they have bounced through several foster placements. To be honest, a lot of the time, those placements were probably never likely to work because they were not adequately matched, whether that is due to the carer’s skills and ability to meet the needs, or whether actually maybe it was not the child’s preference to go into a foster placement in the first place. We hear about that. When you add that together with rising complex needs, you start to see many more children going into children’s homes.

Our view as a voluntary sector provider is that we definitely need a much more mixed market in the residential space, if we are going to adequately meet needs and if we are going to do very careful matching of the child to the home not only in what the home can offer but in matching to the children who are already in the home and making sure that all those peer groups are going to work. The voluntary sector—I am sure Nicola will tell you about this as well—is doing a lot of this specialist provision and we need to properly recognise the role that the voluntary sector is playing in that space.

All the work that the Government are doing around the market needs to really recognise that when we talk about a diversified market, a mixed market and reducing private sector dominance. We need to really think about what we need to do to unblock voluntary sector growth so that we can play a bigger role in this. I have mentioned previously that, in our view at Action for Children, we need to really understand what we mean by therapeutic care. We need to make sure that residential homes are genuinely offering that, ensuring that there is an evidence-based psychologically informed practice model understood by the workforce in practical terms. Everyone who is in the home needs to know what they are driving at.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: How many homes do you have?

Jessica Ford: We have a residential growth programme at the moment, so we are in the process of planning and registration. I think currently it is around 15, but I can confirm that. We are across the UK as well.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: It would be helpful to have a similar figure from Barnardo’s too, please.

Nicola Smith: I believe it is about 10 across the market, but I can also check that figure. There is definitely a lot that Jessica said that I would agree with. Going back to Lord Johnson’s question about how we ended up here, and this sense of market drift that has happened, it feels very much like the residential care market was ignored for a certain amount of time and that there was not a lot of focus on it.

We are seeing more and more older children entering care, more and more children with complex needs entering care, and at the same time we are seeing a reduction in foster carers. We have lost just over 7,000 foster carers from the foster care market in the last four years. This is resulting in much greater pressure on residential care homes to be able to take children often, as Jessica said, because foster placements have broken down.

We also know from research Ofsted did a couple of years ago, where it looked at children’s care plans, that only 50% of children in residential care had residential care on their care plan. One-third of them had fostering on their care plan. Clearly the residential care market is taking some children. Part of your question was why there is growth in residential care; it is because we have not had those foster carers coming through the system. We have had a reduction in foster carers and an increase in children entering care, and that has put pressure on residential care.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: What do you think the role of the regional care co-operatives is going to be?

Nicola Smith: The role of the regional care co-operatives has the potential as a system to help improve some local authority practice in commissioning services. At the moment, every local authority does it on their own. When it comes down to things that you need to do, such as sufficiency planning, working out how many foster carers you need and what types of foster carers, you are doing it on an individual basis. If you put everybody together, you are going to be able to grow those skills and understanding. You are also going to be able to commission at a much bigger level. This should hopefully mean that, if you are a local authority that needs only one or two residential placements, by placing with other local authorities you can block commission rather than spot purchasing, and spot purchasing at a relatively last minute. It has potential.

However, I have some nervousness around regional care co-operatives. One of the things that might happen is, once you start commissioning at a regional level, you are going to encourage people who can provide at a regional level and those are the bigger providers. We know that there are some very small providers out there currently that might operate in only one town or one city. They do a very good job but they are very small and they could end up pushed out in a regional commissioning market, which could result in fewer providers rather than more. We are a bit concerned about that point.

The other point I am concerned about takes very much from our experience of when they did a regionalisation programme in adoption. This is the idea that we could split the care system. I know we have talked a lot on this panel this morning about the importance of prevention and things such as reunification. However, under regional care co-operatives, those co-operatives are going to take the care bit, the fostering bit and the residential care bit, but family help, family support, advocacy and independent visitors are all going to stay with the local authority. In that situation, are we going to end up splitting the care system a little more and preventing a system that could look like a continual journey for a child who, for instance, enters care, but where reunification with some family support is on the agenda? Could we end up splitting all that because different people are doing different things in different places and they are not talking to each other?

It is possible that we could get round those issues with the regional care co-operative programme, but it will need a careful rollout. We need to be learning from the pilots and really investigating what is going on there. It cannot be seen as a panacea, and there are some concerns around it.

Eavan Mckay: I encourage the committee not to look at residential care in isolation. When we look at this, we need to appreciate that the pressures on the residential care market are influenced by these wider pressures on children’s social care in general. Yes, we need to look at market reform, and this is all really important—Nicola and Jess are speaking to that—but we need to continually assess that support up stream. We need to ask whether families are receiving that support before needs escalate, because the placement pressures are not just a market issue but a reflection of the wider pressures on the system.

They need to know that they will have the same level of assessment and there will be the same expectations when they are next inspected.

Related to that point about needing buy-in, we need to get to a place where Ofsted has a relationship of both oversight and support so providers and local authorities are supported to go on this process of continuous learning and improvement. Some of this is about the accessibility of the inspectors. In an ideal world you would have the same inspector showing up over a period of time. They would be available between inspections to have meaningful discussions about quite basic things such as what the distinctions are between the different grades, what they mean in practical terms, what they can do better next time to get to that grade and remove some of that slightly opaque process.

Equally there need to be feedback mechanisms. When I speak to the operational directors who oversee all our provision at Action for Children they tell me that the feedback mechanism must sit outside the formal complaints process. There is no confidence that using the formal complaints process is not going to affect you in your relationship with the inspector and potentially the outcomes there.

I have talked about this point around assessing outcomes relative to starting points, looking at the journey and trajectory and looking holistically at the child’s life beyond the setting and specific care that they are receiving in the local authority. Within that there is something about not making comparisons between what are essentially non-equivalent cohorts by Ofsted.

Another key point that we raised in our response to the Ofsted consultation is around the voice of children, parents and carers. I mentioned this earlier in the session. There needs to be a lot of proactivity on Ofsted’s part about how this is done, offering different modes of engagement at different points inside and outside the inspection processes. Ofsted needs to think about all those different groups it needs to reach and not just go for those that are most available for the discussions.

My final point is quite specific. There is a new evaluation area on enduring relationships. This goes back to what I was saying earlier around DfE really wanting the whole sector to focus on building and maintaining continuous trusted relationships for children. That is really welcome; the new evaluation area is really overdue. At the moment I would say it is framed quite squarely on what the local authority or provider is doing to support the child to build and maintain relationships with family, friends and their community.

What we as a provider find—particularly in the children’s home space—is that there is a lack of clarity around how those relationships can continue with the workforce within the placement. When a child leaves a placement—whether that is due to breakdown or because they move out of the system—what does good look like in that situation? How can we navigate all the issues around consent and risk for some relationships to continue with the workforce?

There is something that you hear quite commonly in the sector around children who have left a children’s home being invited back for Sunday lunch every week. In practical terms that is quite hard to do in some settings, particularly where the relationship sits with a particular member of the workforce in the home. Some of the solution is about seeing it as an organisational responsibility. There is a gap where there is guidance needed to support that process so that settings feel really confident to do it, obviously where the child has the preference to continue those relationships. That new enduring relationships evaluation area is really positive but that is a really crucial missing part of it.
