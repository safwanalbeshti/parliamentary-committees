Mabli Jones: Yes, of course. Thanks for inviting us today. The general picture is probably one that people are familiar with. We know that one in three children in Wales live in poverty, and that is a figure that has not really changed over the last two decades. It has basically hovered around the one in three level. However, despite the fact that the headline income poverty rate has not changed, what has been happening under the surface is that poverty has got deeper, particularly over recent years. We know now that almost half—47%—of people in Wales who live in poverty live in what is classed as very deep poverty. That is an income that is at or below 40% of the median. We know that families who are experiencing that kind of poverty are likely to be going without many of the basics. Those are the headlines at the moment. Q2 David Chadwick: This is a question for Tom. I am sure everybody in this room is well aware that we have suffered job losses and lack of investment in Wales for decades now. How do that economic weakness and those economic failures contribute to the very high levels of child poverty that have been mentioned already?

Tom Wernham: It is definitely a key part of the underlying context. Obviously the labour market and the earnings are a key driver of household incomes. I think what stands out when looking at Wales and comparing it with the rest of the UK is that a gap seems to have opened up between Wales and the rest of the UK in employment rates, particularly since 2019. The data has got very uncertain since then, so it is hard to be sure of exact magnitudes. It looks like Wales had been catching up with the rest of the UK in employment rates up until then, and the gap has widened again.

That makes the job of bringing poverty rates down a bit harder. It also makes it more expensive to bring poverty rates down because you have to either pay more in benefits to those families who are getting less money from work, or you have to take real efforts to restore that level.

Then we also see lower earnings among those who are in work in Wales, and that has quite few contributors. There are fewer very high productivity industries like the financial sector and the tech sector, but there also seems to be lower productivity when comparing industries like for like with the rest of the UK. There are a few different challenges there.

Q3 David Chadwick: Do you agree that developing—growing—the private sector in Wales is the best long-term tactic to solving child poverty? Do you see any policies from either the Welsh Government or the UK Government that give you particular hope at the moment that this will be closed?

Wales also stands out: part of the story of the lower earnings is that there is a bigger gap between the private and public sectors in Wales than in other regions of the UK. That said, if you want to achieve quick reductions in child poverty, the benefit system provides the levers to do that quickly and deliver change now, but it is also the costlier option.

Clearly, if you can boost household incomes by achieving increased employment or earnings, that saves money for the Exchequer and boosts families’ incomes, but it is a much harder job to do. It will depend on policies at both the UK and the Welsh level, so it is a bit of a trade-off there.

Sarah Crawley: Would you mind me coming in on this one? I think it is important because we have to have good work. We have to have well-paid work that is accessible and which people can get to. We are seeing children in families that are in one room, and that is particularly prevalent when we have families with no recourse to public funds. We are seeing greater levels of poverty, and families are really struggling to make ends meet.

I am very concerned, and Barnardo’s is very concerned, about the rising cost of living. The cost of living has gone up in excess of 30% in the last five years, but families’ incomes from employment have not gone up by 30%, so they are really struggling in deep poverty. That is affecting children’s health, their life chances, their mental health and their education. Tom Wernham: Partly it is that people mean different things when they are talking about poverty. We have a relative sense of poverty and an absolute sense of poverty. Sometimes people think that you need to be concerned about relative poverty, particularly over time, because you are concerned about inequality and the poorest families falling behind the standards set by society at large. That is what relative poverty statistics are getting at; the poverty line grows as median income grows. That is a real distributional concern.

As for whether, in absolutes, there are people falling through the gaps of the safety net, if you think about cases like no recourse to public funds and that sort of thing, making those comparisons over time is probably just too difficult because of the low sample size, and we do not have comparable data over time. Trying to quantify whether there is, in an absolute sense, a certain group for whom poverty is persisting, and the scale of that over time, is a bit difficult to do just from the data.

Laurie Ryall: From my perspective, I appreciate that people might look back at poverty and how things have changed, but from what we are seeing, poverty is very real for the children and families that we are working with.
