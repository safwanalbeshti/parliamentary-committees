Professor Wolff: Professor Kim Wolff from King’s College. I am expert in drink and drug driving, and I was previously chair of the expert panel that established section 5A.

Chair: Currently, legal blood alcohol limits are set at higher levels for drivers in England and Wales compared with Scotland. Do you think the Government should consider reducing the limit in England as well?

Dr Dolphin: Thank you for the opportunity to answer these questions. The limit in Scotland is significantly lower than it is in England. When it was first lowered, there was not an immediate change in the rate of accidents. However, we have seen in more recent evidence a much more consistent drop in the level of accidents involving alcohol and, therefore, associated deaths. We know that in other countries across Europe the limit is 0.05, as it is in Scotland. England and Wales are out of synch with the rest of Europe and Scotland. It is reasonable to assume that if we dropped the limit, that would lead to a significant drop in deaths and injuries associated with drink driving, and we recommend that.

We also think there should be a different limit, a lower limit of 0.02, for new drivers because of the cognitive load required to be a new driver. You have less automatic behaviour in your driving, so it takes more mental effort to drive safely. Even a small amount of alcohol could impair you enough for that to be more of a problem, so new drivers should have a lower limit of 0.02, which is effectively zero.

Chair: Is that your main rationale for the distinction between novice and experienced drivers?

Dr Dolphin: Yes, because of the extra cognitive load required to drive when you are a new driver. There is less automaticity about your driving, less that you are used to doing automatically. That is also for commercial drivers, not because of the automaticity but because of the kinds of vehicles they are driving, either because of passenger numbers or because they are driving large commercial vehicles with a heavy mass behind them that is significantly harder to control if they have an accident.

Ean Lewin: My experience here is in the workplace for the last 30 years. One of the biggest issues we have is our clients not really understanding what the levels are. By that I mean how many drinks they can have. Quite often we will supply a breathalyser, they take it home and they say it does not work, “I’ve had two beers, I’ve had three glasses of wine and I’ve had a shot of brandy and I’m still below the limit. Your device is not working.” We say, yes, it is working, it is calibrated, it is perfectly fine. The other thing is rather than the blood alcohol, industry tends to look at breath alcohol, so this is the 35 versus 80 and the 22 versus 50. Getting those messages across to people so that they actually understand is extremely difficult. With that, we always recommend zero—no alcohol. I agree with the submissions of Dr Dolphin and Dr Piper that we should move to a lower level.

Dr Piper: Thank you very much for the opportunity to answer this question. Our view is that it is now nearly 60 years since this country last looked at this law, and there was a view that we should move to 0.05 and then consider 0.02 as soon as possible. There is a line and position that is shared by groups like the BMA, AA, RAC, many of the Royal Colleges and most professionals in this area that 0.02 is the desirable endpoint. Given the speed of change on drink driving limits in the UK, our view is that now is the time to grasp 0.02 for everybody.

There are three clear reasons why. One is that we don’t really want to be paying for our police to be clearing up messes. We all want prevention, and prevention and deterrence are key. Clear communication is essential to prevention and deterrence. The Government currently spend significant funds on a don’t drink and drive campaign, yet they have a limit that says, “But you can drink a certain amount. We’re not sure how much”. Moving from 0.08 to 0.05 does not solve that problem; in fact it continues that problem because 0.05 is still a certain amount. How much? We don’t know but it is different from don’t drink and drive. That mixed messaging, as any communications expert will tell you, is poor messaging.

We have already heard about European alignments. You may be aware that many European countries are at 0.02; Spain is moving to 0.02; the Netherlands is talking about moving to 0.02. There is a Europe-wide movement towards moving to 0.02 as a maximum for all drivers for all EU countries, so a number of EU countries have put their own moves towards 0.02 on hold because there is European movement coming in. If we were to move to 0.05, about 20 years after most countries moved to 0.05, we would end up being way behind again.

The third and most important reason why moving from 0.08 to 0.05 to 0.02 is the right solution is that you are three times more likely to kill someone at 0.05 than 0.02. You are four times more likely to be involved in an accident at 0.05 compared with 0.02. This Government state that they want to save lives, state in their strategy that they have clear ambitions to reduce road deaths and also the injuries and the bereavements. Every road death affects many people. Entire families, including their economic wellbeing, are affected by this. If the Government want to achieve those targets, my strong view is that they will not achieve them with the 0.05 reduction. We need to move to 0.02 for everybody.

Chair: Jo, do you have anything different?

Chief Constable Shiner: I particularly support the points that Dr Piper has made. I think it is important for us to bring it back to victims, families and those who die or are seriously injured at the hands of those who have got behind the wheel, whether through drink or drugs. We definitely support a reduction, because we see the impact of it every day. We don’t think it is for policing to say what the level should be. The evidence, as we have already heard, is better sat elsewhere.

Professor Wolff: The risky group in the UK is young people. It is the 17 to 24-year-olds who are most at risk, and I agree that there should be a very low level for that particular group. There should also be some thought about new and novice drivers and how that can be taken into consideration. We are one of the safest countries in Europe with road safety, so that needs to be taken into consideration as well. Definitely there is an overdue move for the alcohol limit to be reduced. I think we need to gather further evidence on whether that should be to 0.05 and a lower limit of 0.02 for our youngest, most high-risk population.

Dr Dolphin: Most of the public don’t have any way of measuring what their blood alcohol concentration is. Lowering the limit, trying to promulgate what the limit is, is fairly pointless because most people don’t know what their blood alcohol concentration is at any time and don’t know what the concentrations represent. Dropping it from 80 to 50 sends a clear message that it is becoming more restricted. Dropping it to 20 sends a very clear message that there is no safe level of drink driving. The messaging would need to be qualitative rather than quantitative when you are promulgating this to the public. It needs to be very clear that there is no safe level of drink driving, and dropping it to 20 would support that.

Chair: Several of you have recommended different levels for different drivers—novice or young drivers, or whatever. Do you think there is a risk that setting different levels for different drivers could cause confusion?

Dr Piper: There is that risk. In particular, even in the way we are wording the question, we are talking about young drivers or novice drivers. Many people pass their driving test at the age of 40. Are we talking novice or are we talking young? We also know that many of the alcohol harm deaths are caused by persistent drivers who have been driving for 30 or 40 years. I would say that the logic works against that. It is worth mentioning that pretty much every European country we have referred to that has 0.05, already has a 0.02 for novice drivers and commercial drivers. Those countries are looking to move to 0.02 for everybody.

One evaluation has indicated that there is confusion, as your question indicates. There is also huge public support: 83% of people in the UK believe the limit should be zero. Technically, a zero limit is very challenging. This will sound weird, but things like ripe bananas and certain types of blueberries have very high levels of alcohol.

Chair: Cough medicine?

Dr Piper: Cough medicine and so on. A zero limit is unhelpful; a 0.02 limit is tantamount to a trace amount and is supported by members of the public.

Ean Lewin: Police equipment is able to be set and calibrated at those levels, so it is achievable.

Chair: Thank you. We have started off on alcohol and blood alcohol limits, but now we are going to look at drug driving.

Rebecca Smith: Professor Wolff, do the Government have a sufficiently strong understanding of the scale of drug driving and its different forms? What has changed since the review you undertook for the Department for Transport in 2013?

Professor Wolff: There are several questions there. There is an awful lot of data out there to enable the Government to understand the trends and the facts and figures around drug driving. Of course, things have changed since 2012-13, when the panel originally sat, and I am sure people know that drug driving offences have recently surpassed drink driving offences and are up since 2022. There has been an increase in our ability to detect and, therefore, see drug driving offences more effectively. The most commonly used drugs, cannabis and cocaine, are both still No. 1 and No. 2. In legislation, identifying those drugs as a high-risk driver safety issue remains the same. Can you remind me of your other question?

Rebecca Smith: I think you have answered it. It was whether the Government have a strong enough understanding of the scale of the issue and the different forms that it takes.

Professor Wolff: The different forms are important. The data is sometimes produced by different organisations, so it would be useful if they could be pulled together and looked at as a whole. That would be very useful. Different forms is very interesting. We have seen over the last 10 years or so that alcohol drinkers and drivers who use drugs can be one and the same. There are a lot of drug users who use alcohol, and vice versa, which probably was not the case 10 or 15 years ago. I think that is a very important concept, because drink driving and drug driving together are hugely risky. It is important to look at that in more detail than we already do.

Rebecca Smith: I will ask you about the prevalence of nitrous oxide, which is a big issue, certainly in my constituency, and it is usually linked to antisocial behaviour, gathering as groups and all that goes with it. Do you think the Government have an adequate grasp of that sort of more modern issue of which young people, in particular, seem to want to partake? Should the Government be exploring that more?

Professor Wolff: I think a review of the legislation would be very helpful to take into consideration newer substances like nitrous oxide. A lot of work has been done looking at that particular compound and whether we can detect it and how we might collect evidence to establish a drug driving offence. I agree that there needs to be some consideration of newer substances and a review of the legislation as it stands.

Chief Constable Shiner: I completely agree with the previous point about a review of legislation to understand some of the synthetic drugs that are now being produced, including those that are non-alcoholic drink alternatives but in small print say “don’t drive afterwards”. I think it is important that we understand some of those. I wanted to come in on the point of the extent of this challenge, particularly with drugs. I would describe it as an epidemic. It is very easy to go out on patrol, which I still do, and very quickly arrest somebody who is under the influence of drugs, and I don’t think that we should be afraid to say that.

At the National Roads Policing Coordination Centre, for which we are very grateful for the funding and being able to take that forward in December, we are trying to develop more evidence to understand not just the extent of drug driving but the double offending of drink and drugs. We are grateful to the police and crime commissioners who are supporting that work, again because of the funding. We know that some of those who are testing positive for alcohol would test positive for drugs as well if we did double testing.

Rebecca Smith: More broadly, what do conviction statistics tell us about how effective enforcement of drug driving is at present?

Chief Constable Shiner: I could give you some of those statistics. However, that is scratching the surface, so I don’t think they are worth that much. Of course, they show an increase in drug driving but, as I say, it is so prevalent now that if we had more officers, more resourcing, better testing, arguably more fluid laws, the figures would be entirely different.

Professor Wolff: There are a few things to point out. The highest group is young people, 17 to 24-year-olds, who commit 80% or so of those offences. Something needs to be done to look at that particular population. There is also the risk of repeat offenders. There is a population within our drug drivers who ignore legislation, persistently offend and commit other crimes. That is a high risk that we need to look at, and we possibly need to consider a high-risk offender scheme for drug driving as we already have for drink drivers. I think that would help to remove those very dangerous drivers from our roads.

Rebecca Smith: On the drug driving and the identification of drugs, the BMA said in its evidence that the road safety strategy does not adequately distinguish between illicit drugs, prescribed medications and other over-the-counter medicines. What changes to the law, guidance or public information are required in respect of this?

Dr Dolphin: There are a couple of things to say about that. To distinguish illegal drugs—drugs of abuse—from prescription medications, some of which are used by people who are prescribed them and some by family members who take them from cupboards and so on, the law is fairly clear on illegal drugs. It is more a matter of detection and enforcement. We know that drug testing at the roadside is quite difficult, and I am sure we will hear more from Ean later about the types of kit you can use, how easily you can detect, how reliable are false positives and false negatives. Professor Wolff is the expert on that, and I don’t want to step on her toes.

There is also the element of people not believing that they will be caught. Policing of the roads dropped quite significantly under the previous Government, and there were significant drops in the numbers of police officers out on the roads doing stopping and testing. People now don’t believe they will be caught if they drink drive or drug drive. Part of the intervention will have to be convincing people that enforcement will happen and there will be consequences if they are caught, and also getting people to understand the consequences.

With prescription medications, quite often people don’t take in the fact or understand that they will be impaired with these medications. With some of them it is fairly obvious and the doctor prescribing them will tell the patient, “This drug will cause impairment”. With benzodiazepines, sedatives and so on, it is fairly obvious and we will tell them. It is less obvious with some of the other drugs. Some over-the-counter drugs even, like antihistamines and some antidepressants, can cause impairment of driving, and people just don’t realise that.

Chair: Although it says so on the packet.

Dr Dolphin: It does say on the packet, but you have seen the patient information leaflets. They are not visually appealing and most people don’t read them because they think they know what they have. You need a specific warning when you are given the medication, handed to you, “By the way, one of the significant side effects is that this causes driving impairment. You shouldn’t drive if you find that it is causing that”. That is difficult to do, and people don’t take in all the information at the time they are being prescribed because lots of information is coming in that consultation about your diagnosis and what it means. The side effects of the drugs are a secondary thing for the patient, so it is quite difficult for them to take that in.

Chair: Would it be helpful if pharmacists made that point when they are selling them? If you pick up too many packets of paracetamol, the pharmacist asks you what it is for and makes sure you are not buying too many. Is that a possibility?

Dr Dolphin: Yes. Some of the medications you can just swipe on the automated till without interacting with a human, but I agree that if there is a pharmacist interaction they can, and already do, warn patients.

Chair: You can’t swipe more than two packets of paracetamol.

Dr Dolphin: I meant antihistamines but, yes, you are right.

Ean Lewin: There is the workload that is now put on pharmacists, and then you have the different levels of counter staff. You have the complication of how many medications a person is on at a particular time. It is quite tricky but the patient information will just depress them, so nobody properly reads that.

Dr Dolphin: It is possible, of course, to follow up. I think it would be difficult technically to do it now, but it is possible to conceive that in a few years’ time when somebody is prescribed a new drug that could cause that, you could reinforce the message with a semi-automated text message the next day saying, “The medication you were prescribed causes driving impairment. Please be aware”; the consequences are linked to it, more information.

Ean Lewin: On the scale and types of drug driving, when we are teaching in the workplace, we use drugs as the term for illegal. We don’t confuse drugs as medicines, but there is a grey area of people using them outside of the prescription. The biggest problem we have is illegal drugs. To put it into perspective, in Op Limit 2024, public information from Merseyside Police shows they made 469 drug driving arrests at the roadside versus 191 drink driving arrests at the roadside. When we met the Minister at the time, I asked how many of the 469 were prosecuted—because the figure that was referred to in the press was about 30,000 being more than 29,000—and only 156 were prosecuted. These are CPS figures.

If we look at this as a process, I believe police at our roadsides are doing a fantastic job in all the circumstances they have, but the process behind that is not sufficient. We are losing at least half of our prosecutions. Not only is it an insignificant deterrent, we are actually teaching people that they can get away with it. We have to stop that, and I will explain later how I think we could do that on a cost-neutral basis.

Rebecca Smith: This is a bit of a leap from where we were before, apologies. There are widespread reports of delays in processing roadside drug testing. What are the broader impacts of these delays?

Ean Lewin: The process can be that the officer has seen a reason to stop, has stopped, has probably breathalysed and then maybe decided to do a drug screen. There is a failure with the drug screen. They decide to arrest and then wait for transport to take them to a police station, wait for a healthcare professional, and we are talking about several hours at least before samples are taken.

Blood might be the gold standard, but they are taking it far too late. It might be the case that they have had so much cocaine that they can’t extract the blood. They may not give consent. I am sorry, but I am much firmer than that. In France they can forcibly take a blood sample, but then you have to get it off to the lab. The labs are then, for whatever reason, taking far too long, and in all this time the person is allowed to continue driving. This is just shocking.

I will talk later about how I think there is a cost-neutral solution, but the process goes from getting the blood to the lab, back to the station to charge, to CPS and then to find a court slot, and eventually they are banned. Just as an example, a couple of weeks ago I was in Blackburn and it took two and three-quarter years between the offence and the magistrates banning this kid, and I think that is shocking.

Chair: Meanwhile they had been driving. They are out breaking the law.

Ean Lewin: Yes.

Rebecca Smith: Sorry, I don’t want to monopolise you, Ean, but I will put the question now rather than later. In your written evidence, you recommend permitting immediate saliva samples to be taken by police officers to speed things up. Do you think that should be more widely used by police officers?

Ean Lewin: I think we should learn from Australia, which does nearly 10 times as much drug testing as we do, with a third of the population, but they use random testing. That has been saliva for many years. In 2016, the French—I mentioned that they were forcibly taking blood—realised that having to take a doctor out with them to do these stops was almost not feasible, but they changed to saliva because they realised it was an adequate sample, especially for illegal drugs above the level in our legislation. They now have success rates in the high 90s, with those failing a roadside screen also failing the confirmation. They have a laboratory that processes the saliva sample within a matter of days. It costs the force €20 and the result is there, they are in court and they are banned within, let’s say, a week.

In that situation, their ability also to suspend the driving licence is credible but I don’t think society would be so accepting if you were to try to suspend somebody’s licence for six months. However, there are steps through which you can increase the ability to do that. New York state police did a study in the last two or three years that was published about a month ago. They had almost but not quite perfect correlation between blood and saliva on the main drugs. With that correlation, the error was that saliva saw more drugs than blood, certain drugs, so it is feasible.

Chair: It is really interesting, and if we don’t already have those international comparisons, please send them in. Jo, what is your response on roadside testing and saliva.

Chief Constable Shiner: I largely support that. From a policing perspective, we need a tighter proof test that is effective by the roadside. We have been putting forward proposals for some years that we should have the ability to take a licence there and then—whether it is temporary or permanent, depending on whether there are aggravating factors or if the driver admits it straightaway and it is the first time they have been tested and caught—to take the risk off the roads. That is what we need to do because that prevents the KSIs and the victims and families suffering, and then there should be an escalation process—a tighter proof test by the roadside, the ability to take the licence there and then.

We need procedural justice. It is really important where there are aggravating factors or where we need a follow-up test for them to go to custody, and then to court within 48 hours to contest it so we have that procedural justice. We must not overcomplicate this. The simpler the test, the simpler the legislation and the simpler the ability for us, as soon as we possibly can, to remove the privilege of a driving licence from the driver. That is fundamentally important.

Chair: And remove the danger to other road users.

Chief Constable Shiner: Exactly that.

Professor Wolff: It is important to note that we must understand that countries like Australia have a very different judicial system from ours. They have random testing, they have a bus and they only test for drugs. Their legislation is separate and different from ours, and they run a system that would not currently be possible in this country. I completely agree that there should be something we can do at the roadside to speed up the process, but we also need to understand that if we are looking at trying to identify either a section 4 or a concentration above the thresholds in section 5A, we cannot do that with saliva.

The scientific evidence is not there. There is no robust relationship between oral fluid levels and drug concentration in blood, so for the prosecution or the defence, it would be very difficult to interpret oral fluid unless it was a zero-tolerance approach. So, in much the same way as we have been discussing with alcohol, a zero-tolerance approach could be managed through oral saliva testing, but that would not be the case if we had to measure a threshold or impairment.

Chair: Thank you, that is partly why we do these inquiries.

Ean Lewin: I would just like to disagree. Are we not in an environment where we are meant to be looking at the legislation that is required? If that means somebody has to stand up and say a certain level for a certain drug is as per the type approval, then that is what we should do. We should be looking at the other common drugs like ketamine. We have asked the Home Office time and again whether we can have a level for testing ketamine at the roadside, which would solve a big issue for the police.

Chair: The road safety strategy is obviously being led by the Department for Transport, but at least three other Government Departments have responsibilities in this area. One thing about being a Select Committee is that we can recommend legislative change as and when.

Dr Dolphin: This is a slight side point, but we would be very keen for the collection of samples to stay with the police and police surgeons, police-employed doctors, rather than starting to involve other healthcare workers, partly because we have to maintain the therapeutic relationship between patients and doctors and nurses. If they start to think that we are working with the police in order to catch them out, take samples and so on, that will impair people’s ability to access healthcare. We are very happy for them to do that, but it should stay with the police rather than becoming an NHS function or drawn into that direction.

Chair: That is very helpful. I just want to go back a few steps to Jo. You mentioned that it would be helpful if the law was more fluid. It would be useful to know what you meant by that.

Chief Constable Shiner: For us to be able to escalate proportionately either the procedures or the outcomes, the deterrents. If you have a person who has been caught—I will not say for the first time, because they may not be—but if they have been caught for the first time and there are no aggravating factors, there should be a pretty unbureaucratic but procedurally just way for the police to deal with that and take the risk off the roads, arguably without ever needing to go through court or anything like that.

As you escalate through that legislation, where you have maybe repeat offenders or where they have been the causal factor in a collision in which someone has been injured, both the penalties and the legal system they need to go through should increase proportionately and procedurally. That is what I meant by having fluidity in the scale of how they could be dealt with, from very simply by the roadside right up to causing death while under the influence.

May I take this opportunity to make another point? I think it is right that we are talking about whether it is saliva or blood, and I leave that to the experts here who have spoken very well around that. I still go out on the frontline and still stop vehicles, and on a road stop I was at in last Christmas’s campaign, we ran out of officers within about 10 minutes because we had arrested the first six drivers for being positive for drugs, most of them young males. They all admitted it. There was no lack of admittance on their part, they were all, “Yes, I took cannabis last night”; one of the drivers said, “Yes, I have had some cocaine”. So, there should also be a route—as long as there is, and I do stress this, procedural justice, in the same way that we do for other crimes—so that we do not waste time and money going through all these other routes if the person is saying they are guilty of that offence, “I am driving; I have taken cannabis and cocaine”. I make that point because I think sometimes it gets missed in the confusion of what system we are going to use.

Chair: That cultural shift 30, 40, 50 years ago on alcohol, we are not going to stop everybody but a lot more people might think twice in the same way that they do now before driving after drinking.

Professor Wolff: I agree with Ean and other members of the panel that there could be more substances detected at the roadside. We only currently have two, and there is the capability to test for more. I think that would be a good idea.

Alex Mayer: The road safety strategy sets targets to reduce deaths and serious injuries by 65% and child deaths and serious injuries by 70%. Do you think the measures in the strategy go far enough to achieve this?

Ean Lewin: I think it is great that you have that ambition, but the hard part is yours. You are going to have to stand up and make some serious decisions, and some of that involves the Home Office, the Treasury—you are going to need to equip Jo and everybody else—the Ministry of Justice and the Crown Prosecution Service. It is achievable with that money and those decisions, and if you act quickly. If we spend another five years trying to put legislation in place, you have no hope of achieving those figures, whereas everybody in the room wants you to. We are all here to help.

Dr Piper: First, I think it is important to be clear that other countries have moved towards a Vision Zero approach. A 65% reduction is welcome, but the targets could be more ambitious. There should be no deaths or injuries on the road, and having that vision has driven significant changes to road safety in other countries. Given that, and taking those ambitions as set, alcohol is going to play a significant part, but it is important that it is one among a range of measures. In relation to that in particular, we are not just talking about the limit but the communication around the limit. That is super important.

In fact, probably more of the benefit will come from the communications than from reducing the limit, because it changes our attitude and behaviours, and you will prevent so many. I think it is important that does not get lost. We could obsess about the number of the limit and forget the communication.

Thirdly, enforcement. I think enforcement is so important. We know that mandatory education is currently lacking. What that means is if you are convicted of an offence, you can choose to attend a course. The effect and impact of those courses is high; they have a high reduction in repeat offences, but they are currently optional. In most other countries, they are mandatory.

Chair: They cost money.

Dr Piper: They cost money, but the offender usually covers the cost, with some exceptions for lower-paid people. They are essentially cost-neutral for the state, and they significantly reduce reoffending. I would say you are looking at a large target. What is needed is going to be a range of different measures. This is why there needs to be a strategy. It cannot be a few silver bullets. It needs to be a proper strategy: this will reduce by 3% to 4%; this will reduce by another 6% to 7%. I think it needs that comprehensive approach.

The Government are moving in the right direction, but they will face resistance, much of it false resistance—the rural pubs argument is a classic red herring—but the Government need to be able to stand firm and say, “No. We want to deliver this strategy, and we will be bold enough to deliver on this.”

Chief Constable Shiner: I wholeheartedly support everything Dr Piper has said, so I will not repeat it. I also think there are additional measures that feed into that last point. For example, if you introduced random drug testing of people taking their driving test, it would have a huge deterrent effect, particularly for young people, as the people who most want their licence. Recently, some driving instructors have tested positive while undertaking lessons, so it would also send a wider message.

Also, improving some of that mandatory knowledge around not just drink driving but the “fatal five” more widely, introducing it into the theory test, making sure it is a much greater part of the theory that people need to know and understand. If you were to introduce either random or mandatory testing of those who are taking their driving test, they would understand the whole legislation a lot more. I also think it would significantly reduce the queues for driving tests.

Rebecca Smith: Jo, we have already heard about France this morning. You have to have an alcohol kit in your car along with a high-vis jacket. Do you think our Government should be looking at putting more of the responsibility on drivers, to deal with some of the cost? I am not advocating this, just putting it on the record, but I drove for the first time in France this year and had not realised that that was even a thing until I looked into what I needed.
