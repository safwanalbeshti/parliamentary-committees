Examination of Witnesses

Witnesses: Tom Guest and Andrew Thomas.

Chair: Today we are holding a further oral evidence session on the draft Conversion Practices Bill. This Joint Committee has been appointed to consider and report on the Government’s draft Conversion Practices Bill, which sets out provisions to prevent and criminalise abusive and harmful conversion practices. We are very grateful to both our witnesses for attending in person to answer our questions.

As I am sure you both know, the Committee is dealing with highly sensitive matters, and we appreciate that this can be quite a challenging area, but we hope that our questions do not put you into any difficulty. To begin with, can I ask you both to introduce yourselves?

Andrew Thomas: My name is Andrew Thomas. I am a practising barrister based in Manchester. I am the chair of the Criminal Bar Association of England and Wales.

Tom Guest: Good afternoon. My name is Tom Guest. I am a deputy director with the Crown Prosecution Service.

Chair: My first question is for you, Mr Guest. Do you or other law enforcement colleagues have data on the number of offences connected to conversion practices and the number of successful prosecutions? I should mention that we have been told that the Government estimate that between 75,000 and 93,000 people per year may experience conversion practices in England and Wales. Does that fit with your information, or do you have some different views to express?

Tom Guest: The CPS and, as far as I am aware, other law enforcement agencies do not collect that data. We do not collect data on the offence, as is currently being looked at, so we would need to look at whether any existing criminal offences have an allegation of conversion practices wrapped into them as part of the facts. I am afraid that we just do not collect data on that basis. In preparation for this Committee, we have made every reasonable inquiry to find out whether there are any recent or current cases, and we have not identified any. I have seen the polling that the Government have published, but we do not have any recent or current cases that have been seen by us and gone through the courts.

Chair: Maybe you cannot answer my next question, then. Why has there not been more law enforcement to deal with this particular problem? Is it because they are being prosecuted under different heads and dealt with under different offences?

Tom Guest: Overall, it is possible that they were dealt with by different offences, more generally and over time, but as I say, recently or currently, we do not have any visibility on that. We do not think they are being currently dealt with within the CPS.

Chair: If I wanted to know, what sort of analysis would I have to conduct?

Tom Guest: It would be quite a span. We would have to first define every offence that we think could capture a conversion practice, of which there are already quite a lot on the statute book. At the moment, we would then effectively have to do some kind of manual analysis of that. Maybe, in time, artificial intelligence will unlock that, but at the moment it is the same as if you asked, “How many offences of assault involve a crowbar?” We would be able to define offences of assault tolerably well—it would still take some time—but we would then have to go into each case and work out if a crowbar was involved. It would be the same thing: within the facts, does it contain an allegation of a conversion practice?

Regarding law enforcement, I cannot speak for the police but I imagine that one significant factor is the number of victims who come forward. I think that if a great number of victims came forward, policing would recognise that they are at least being asked to consider starting an investigation. I do not have the sense that there are a number of live investigations in England and Wales.

Baroness Brown of Silvertown: There have been private prosecutions, have there not, but not through the CPS?

Tom Guest: By definition, they will not have been taken forward by the CPS if they are private prosecutions, unless we took it over, so I am not aware of any—recent or current.

Baroness Brown of Silvertown: Mr Thomas, are you aware?

Andrew Thomas: I have practised as a criminal lawyer for about 35 years, mainly in the Crown court, and I do not recall seeing or hearing of any case that would fall within scope of the draft Bill.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Thank you for coming. I want to talk about gaps in existing legislation. Perhaps you would both give me your assessment of which gaps in legislation the draft Bill is designed to fill. Have you seen cases—from what you have just said, Mr Thomas, probably not—where you would have liked this piece of legislation in order to be able to prosecute?

Tom Guest: We have identified some cases where we would say there is a gap in the law. We do not want to overstate the extent of it, but we would recognise a gap in the law. Paragraph 22 of the explanatory notes to the draft Bill gives some examples of those gaps. Our own analysis is that that is a sound analysis of some gaps in the law. There are probably three areas that the CPS has identified. I need to make very clear that each case turns on its own facts. I am not saying this applies in every case as a matter of law or fact, but I would give three examples.

First, an allegation of child cruelty that involves a victim who is 16 or over is not currently covered by child cruelty offences. Secondly, some cases of battery—which is time-barred after six months—could be prosecuted under the Bill as drafted if the second limb of harm was established. The third example is psychological or psychiatric harm falling short of the standard for grievous bodily harm, where there was no assault but there was none the less psychological or psychiatric harm. We do not want to overstate that, but that is our analysis of the gaps in the law.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Thank you, Mr Guest; that is very helpful. Have you had experience of such cases in which you have not been able to prosecute?

Tom Guest: Again, that was precisely the question that we put when we wanted to make all reasonable inquiries in preparation for the Committee. We have not identified those cases; that is an analysis of the existing parameters of the criminal law.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Mr Thomas, do you want to come in?

Andrew Thomas: I agree with what Mr Guest said. I would just emphasise that the slightly lower threshold of “serious alarm or distress to the individual” perhaps would not be captured by the long list of other offences that otherwise would fall within scope.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Do you think it would be possible to achieve the desired policy objective, or fill those gaps, by amending existing laws—for example by adding a new aggravating factor to offences that we already have or by updating the Children and Young Persons Act?

Andrew Thomas: Certainly, the offence of harassment would capture a significant number of lower-level offences. Treating discrimination on grounds of sexual orientation or gender for purposes relating to conversion practices could add to the penalty. So, there is some scope there, but harassment would not capture the full range of offences that this draft Bill would.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Do you agree, Mr Guest?

Tom Guest: I agree. May I say three things briefly? First, it would not directly address that gap in the law. It is for the Committee to decide how large or small it considers that, but it would not address that. The second point I would make is about plain labelling. In our view, plain labelling is not a good reason for every new criminal offence simply to reflect a behaviour, but when it is one of the arguments in favour of an offence, it is sometimes helpful for it to be plainly labelled in the form of an offence.

The third point I would cover is that sometimes it is helpful to have a further offence to charge, in addition to existing offences, because you may want to reflect the behaviour. You may want to say, “There was an assault, but also, members of the jury, we want you to consider this as an allegation of abusive conversion practice.” Even if it is covered by existing offences—we might come to charge selection later—it is useful to have a further offence in the arsenal.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Can I just stop you there? Would it be helpful to have it as an aggravating factor in certain offences?

Tom Guest: That is often suggested as an alternative to new offences. Again, I just do not think it would address the gap in the law; it would address the question at sentencing. Again, if there is evidence of a shortfall or shortcoming in sentencing, I do not dissuade the Committee from looking at that at all, but the issue is the route to liability. If you are at sentence, you have already convicted someone of an offence.

Chair: If I may interrupt you, Baroness Prentis, both Baroness Brown and Lord Isaac would like to ask a question.

Baroness Brown of Silvertown: Is it possible to catch people who perpetrate a fraud in this regard? For instance, should a counsellor, a religious person, decide that they wish to “pray away the gay”, that is clearly not possible, but they might extract a goodly sum of money for that. Could that be caught under laws of fraud?

Andrew Thomas: Potentially, yes. Dishonesty would be a key element there. Normally, fraud would involve false representation, which I am sure would be easy to establish.

Lord Isaac: Mr Guest, on the question of aggravated existing offences, as I understand the Northern Ireland position—forgive me if you do not know this—could you not have an additional offence as well as the possibility of an aggravated offence, so that you had both?

Tom Guest: Yes. That is why I do not dissuade the Committee from looking at it in addition. I just think it is important to focus on whether the offence and criminal liability are covered.

Chair: Back to you, Baroness Prentis.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Could I ask you both for your views on the available penalties of up to five years in prison and unlimited fines? Do you think that is about right?

Andrew Thomas: In principle, Parliament sets the tone for sentencing, but this is an offence that contemplates serious harm. The maximum sentence of five years’ imprisonment would be in line with those for similar offences under the Offences against the Person Act or with controlling and coercive behaviour.

Tom Guest: I agree. I was thinking particularly of assault occasioning actual bodily harm and coercive or controlling behaviour in terms of having some thresholds to demonstrate seriousness, but other, more serious offences are available if there was sexual offending or really, really serious harm caused.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: What factors would determine whether you would use this legislation or other offences that might be available to you to prosecute? For example, if a higher penalty were available if you were to charge another offence, might you be tempted to go for that, or would you do both? How do you see this working?

Tom Guest: Section 6 of the code for Crown prosecutors addresses this, and we have just finished a public consultation looking at that. The key principles are as follows: first, to reflect the seriousness and extent of the offending; secondly, to ensure that the court has adequate powers of sentence; and thirdly, to present the case in a clear and simple way to the court and the jury.

In terms of seriousness and extent, if you were thinking of something that had taken place over a long period of time and where there were several instances, we may well look at charging this offence as well as others, just to make sure that we captured the span of behaviour. By contrast, if it is a very single-focus incident, it is probably not going to be helpful to charge lots of offences.

On your point about maximum penalty, it is exactly that: with adequate powers of sentence, if you have a very serious sexual offence or offences against the person, which carry more than five years, I would tend in favour of charging that offence. Equally, as I say, if you fortunately have relatively minor injuries, the powers with this offence may be sufficient.

In terms of presenting the case in a clear and simple way, that is to ensure that we do not just charge every offence that we can, so the case before the jury is clear and explicable. But again, it might make sense to present a picture and a story to the jury. You may be saying, “This was an abuse of conversion practice, and it actually culminated in this further separate, serious offence, so we want to present that entire picture to you,” and that is a clear and simple way of presenting it. I am afraid that it is not one size fits all, but those are the consistent principles, and I think they will continue to be in the revised version of the code.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Well, starting is not one consistent principle—I think we all understand that. Is there anything else you want to say about what public interest factors you would take into account when choosing to prosecute for this offence rather than something else?

Tom Guest: I think the selection of charges addresses the situation when you have a choice between charges. The public interest considerations in the code for Crown prosecutors are clear. There is an expectation that we generally prosecute cases that have been legislated for by Parliament, but it has never been a rule that we will automatically prosecute. We will obviously look at the facts of each individual case. Thinking about this case in terms of victims and the harm done to victims, which is quite a powerful public interest factor, because of the threshold within this, there is quite a clear further expectation that we would prosecute in the public interest.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Can we come to the detail of the offence that is created here? Obviously, as you will appreciate, there are three elements to it. The first is that a conversion practice has taken place. The second is that it is an abusive conversion practice. The third is that it causes serious harm or serious alarm or distress, as you have referred to. Can I take the first two of those in turn? Others will come on to the third. In relation to a conversion practice, it seems to me that the wording of the draft Bill is not limited to changing someone’s status; it is also potentially about trying to divert someone away from a particular status or indeed affirming an existing status. Would you agree with that?

Andrew Thomas: I would, yes.

Tom Guest: I certainly would in terms of it being to have or not to have—so dissuading someone from something. I think if you had someone who knew that the other person had a heterosexual identity, and they were trying to further imbue them with that, I am struggling with how that is conduct towards them with the intention of causing them to have or not to have. I certainly agree that it does not address changing at all directly; I just think, factually, it would be difficult to think of a scenario that would fall within that.

Sir Jeremy Wright: It is perhaps difficult to think of a case study in which someone would complain in those circumstances. I am just trying to understand how broad you think the wording currently is, and how much at variance it is from the way in which—if I can put it this way—the draft Bill has been advertised. The word “change” has been used a great deal in relation to the draft Bill, but I think you are telling me that it goes a little beyond that in the actual wording.

Andrew Thomas: I agree with that. There has to be some element of uncertainty in the mind of the subject, but certainly affirming an existing tendency would be covered in my view.

Sir Jeremy Wright: The second requirement is that the practice be abusive. Perhaps we can get into what “abusive” means in the terms of the Bill. I invite you to give any comments you have about the requirement of abusiveness and the way it is defined here, but can I ask for two specifics?

I would welcome your thoughts on the fact that this is a non-exhaustive list. If you have the Bill in front of you, we are looking at clause 1(6), which says: “In assessing the nature of the conduct, consideration is to be given (among other things) to the question whether it involves” certain things. Do you have any concerns about that being a non-exhaustive as opposed to an exhaustive list?

On the wording of those things that are listed, two further questions arise. First, words “of a sexual nature” would be one factor that the court would be entitled to take account of in determining whether something is abusive or not. Do you think that is appropriate? Secondly, there is the use of the word “pressure” twice, once in relation to “economic pressure” and once in relation to the “use of psychological or emotional pressure”. Sorry to throw a lot at you at once, but comments on any of that would be welcome.

Andrew Thomas: I do have concerns about the way in which that element of “abusiveness” is structured in the draft Bill. It seems to me that it is essentially leaving the decision to be a subjective decision by the tribunal of fact, whether it is a jury or magistrates. The essence of it is that subsection (5) would leave it to the tribunal of fact to decide as a fact whether they think that something is abusive. In theory, they could look at the non-exhaustive list, look at “violent or threatening words”, and decide that that is not abusive. I am a little bit troubled that it is so subjective and would leave it to the opinion of individual jurors, who may disagree in the jury room, or magistrates, who may form a different view from one case to the next. That is a concern that I have. It is not the structure that we see in other Acts of Parliament. For example, in relation to the offence of stalking, we have a list of what does amount to abusive conduct, so I am concerned about that subjective element.

My own view is that the first item in the list, “words or behaviour of a sexual nature”, really makes that second element almost pointless, because when we think of the first element and what amounts to a conversion practice causing someone to believe that they have or do not have a sexual orientation, it is difficult to contemplate when that may have taken place without words of a sexual nature. That is such a broad term. One can reasonably conclude that that is not abusive, so I suggest that that be deleted, as it is really covered in the first element.

I do not have a difficulty with “economic pressure” or “emotional pressure”. Pressure is something that juries are asked to take into account, again in relation to stalking and other similar offences.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Mr Guest, have you anything to add to that?

Tom Guest: I think I take a somewhat different perspective on that. First, the Public Order Act refers to “threatening, abusive or insulting words or behaviour”. We are told by the courts, “Don’t overcomplicate this. These are ordinary words. Unless Parliament is telling you that there’s a different meaning that attaches to them, there’s an ordinary, everyday approach to be taken.” I would say that with the Public Order Act, that equally applies, for instance with protests. A jury or a court will be asked—“You’re entitled to be offensive at this protest. You’re entitled to be shocking or provocative, but what you must not do is be abusive or threatening.” Before we get into the Bill, I would say on behalf of prosecutors that we think we can apply an ordinary and common-sense approach.

In general, non-exhaustive lists are not unhelpful in focusing the mind on the fact that the question of abusiveness needs to be considered. It is not a checklist, but it ensures that the question is given the attention that it needs, because it is an important threshold that is being consciously inserted into the Bill.

Baroness Browning: Mr Thomas, you have just had an exchange with Sir Jeremy on the list and words in clause 1(6). Would you change anything you have said to us if you were focusing on the fact that the person is between the ages of 10 and 18?

Andrew Thomas: No, I do not think I would on those grounds—including my view on subsection (6)(a), because I think that that is covered in the first element of the offence. Taking up Mr Guest’s point, I can see scope for having a further point on the list, about other pressure of similar seriousness, if there are things that might be missed, but, no, on those grounds, I do not think I would change my answer.

Baroness Browning: Mr Guest, do you want to comment on that?

Tom Guest: It is important that these are prompts about the nature of the conduct, so they are subject in subsection (5) to the whole of the circumstances. Again, it is absolutely important to look at location, age of suspect and age of complainant as part of those broader circumstances. These are just prompts to ensure that the nature of the conduct is properly thought about.

May I briefly address “words or behaviour of a sexual nature”? What we thought that probably covered would be if you had a complainant who was gay and was being subjected to overtly sexual words, either to encourage them to be heterosexual or to discourage them from being homosexual. I do take the point that a number of the conversations that would happen about sexual orientation anyway would be covered, but conversations are not per se abusive, so we would be talking about an abusive use of words of a sexual nature to shock or to try to impress upon a person a particular orientation.

Sir Jeremy Wright: The danger here, presumably, is that this becomes a circular discussion, because this is an attempt to define what “abusive” means.

Tom Guest: Which is why I think “pressure” is used rather than “abuse,” because in other legislation it is used. And that certainly is circular, because—

Chair: Some other legislation does not have the word “abuse” at all. Is there room for the view that the practice, as defined in subsection (2) with the intention that is there, is an abuse in itself? Then, all you need is to establish the harm element to constitute the offence.

Andrew Thomas: I know that the impact assessment has talked about having those high thresholds as a way of ensuring that the legislation is not too broad in its impact. Taking away that second element of abusive conduct would perhaps change, in quite a fundamental way, the nature of the Bill.

Sir Jeremy Wright: This may be a provocative question, and you should therefore feel free to disregard it, but do you think that there is a difference between saying that a threshold is high and actually making it so? Is this really a high threshold or are people just telling us that it is?

Andrew Thomas: I have expressed my concern about the subjective element that is there. If we look at matters such as violent or threatening words or behaviour, or controlling or coercive behaviour, that is a relatively high threshold. It did strike me to ask whether the Bill is correctly titled, because the offence is essentially one of abusive conversion practices rather than just conversion practices. It is designed to deal with abuse.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Yes. May I ask about intent? As the Bill is drafted, intent applies only to the conversion practice; it does not apply to the other two elements of the offence. Does that cause you any concern?

Tom Guest: It does not necessarily cause us concern per se. To touch on your previous question, we do think that they are genuine thresholds. For instance, prayer might be a conversion practice but, as it is commonly understood, it is very difficult to say how it could be abusive. There may well be facts and circumstances that manage to transform it into that, but that is an absolute requirement for the Bill. Simply prayer would satisfy the first element that you identified, but not the second.

Because these are genuine thresholds, what has been identified by other witnesses about a lack of mens rea causes us less concern, but we are certainly open to discussion about whether what is used in other legislation—a “knew or ought to have known” requirement, for instance—should be inserted, because it is about checks and balances. You have heard witnesses talk about balancing the risk of not delivering the policy intent with that of setting the net too widely. Those are areas that we could look at but, on the face of it, it does not cause us concerns.

Andrew Thomas: I was surprised that there was no further mental element beyond that first requirement. It is in contrast with the stalking offence, which uses that “knew or ought to have known” test, which is tested according to the standard of the reasonable person. That is what one would normally see where a criminal offence is being created.

Sir Jeremy Wright: My final question is about suppression. You may know that in other jurisdictions in which such an offence has been introduced into the criminal law, it is not just about changing or seeking to change someone’s identity; it is also about attempting to persuade or pressure them into suppressing that identity. That is not present here. Do you think it should be?

Andrew Thomas: It is a harm that is equivalent, so I think that would be a logical extension.

Tom Guest: Because we are looking at the case in its entirety, in all its facts and circumstances, we would not be prepared to say that every case of suppression is not caught by this Bill, because there will come a point at which the suppression is effectively causing the individual not to have a certain sexual orientation. Just to be clear, I do not think that the police will not investigate, or that the CPS will not prosecute, allegations of suppression when all the facts and circumstances meet every element of the offence, but, to support Mr Thomas’s point, it is better that it is put beyond doubt if the Bill is intended to cover that. It should address that, perhaps with an interpretative clause around sexual orientation. We would rather that it were unambiguously stated, if that is Parliament’s intention.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Understood. To be fair to the Government, I think their explicit intention is not to cover suppression, but the question we have to ask ourselves is whether we should recommend otherwise. That is very helpful; thank you very much.

Danny Beales: How likely is it that a defence will be successfully mounted on the basis of action by form of words, actions or pressure, which all quite clearly seek to lessen or hide someone’s sexual identity or make them less visibly LGBT? How likely is it that a successful defence can be mounted on those grounds if quite clearly the purpose of the course of action and behaviour—never specifically stated—is to say, “Do not be this. Change your status”?

Tom Guest: In terms of the conversion practice itself, we would look at all the evidence. A suspect may well deny it and may give a very clear account. We would obviously also look at what the complainant said and all the other evidence. There may well be sufficient inference to be drawn, notwithstanding the fact that the suspect denies in interview that the intentional conduct in clause 1(2) is made out. Again, we would be looking at whether it was abusive in all the circumstances. There are so many factors and sources of evidence, and we would look at that in the round. I cannot assess the likelihood; all I would say is that we would look very clearly at any such account and at whether the evidence pointed in favour of it or away from it.

Danny Beales: Would the muddying of the water with that argument make you less likely to bring a prosecution?

Tom Guest: No. Our duty is to bring a prosecution where there is sufficient evidence and where it is in the public interest to do so. All I mean is that every case comes with the challenges, first, of working with the police to follow all reasonable lines of inquiry to ensure that we have all the evidence to hand, and then of asking ourselves whether a court is more likely than not to be sure of the suspect’s guilt.

Naz Shah: You have talked about the issue of pressure; you said that juries are used to thinking about pressure, both economic and emotional. Can you confirm how those terms would be understood in cases of parents dealing with children aged under 16?

Andrew Thomas: I am not sure that I can give specific examples of that. I am aware of the debate around a parent’s reaction to a child’s discussion of their sexuality, for example, and how the parent’s ordinary parenting might then be interpreted in the light of that and whether that gives rise to any sort of threat of prosecution. But I think that that would fall to be judged within the context of whether or not something did, in all the circumstances, amount to abuse as opposed to ordinary parenting. That would be the discussion around that.

Naz Shah: That worries me. Am I right in saying that we cannot leave it for the judges and the courts to decide what abuse is, if it is not defined in the Bill as it is drafted?

Andrew Thomas: It is always going to be a question of degree. You are going to be leaving it to people—for example, a jury—to use their common sense and knowledge of the world to know whether the way in which a parent acted is consistent with ordinary parenting or is something that is specifically targeted at a conversion practice.

Naz Shah: I just want to pick that back up. The reason I want to home in on this point is that you talked about juries having the experience of dealing with coercive control, abuse and so on, but now we are talking about under-16-year-olds and leaving it to a jury. Coming back to the Bill as drafted: for an under-16-year-old, is that clear enough?

Andrew Thomas: I think I would turn that question around and say, “Could you put into legislation all the range of things that may take place in a child’s upbringing, list them and say what is included and what is not included?” I think it does fall into the scope of areas where we have to use our knowledge of the world and our experience. For example, juries or magistrates have to judge cases of child cruelty and assess the threshold that applies there.

Naz Shah: But—as one of the witnesses said, although not verbatim—if somebody took my phone from me, as an adult, that would be coercion, whereas if I, as a parent, took the phone off my child, that would be good parenting. Is the Bill clear enough to answer the questions or the exchange that you and I have just been having?

Andrew Thomas: You could put in thresholds or specifically acknowledge in the Bill that one matter to be taken into account is whether the relationship of parent and child, or of guardianship, applies. But all those elements fall to be judged in all the circumstances of the case.

Chair: There is not a defence of reasonableness, is there?

Andrew Thomas: No, which is, again, a concern about the Bill: whether that is an alternative way of addressing those sorts of issues.

Chair: Reasonable conduct, yes.

Naz Shah: To move on, unfortunately we do not have police witnesses here today, but could you help us to understand this? My understanding is that any conduct that meets the definition of a conversion practice in clause 1 will need to be investigated to see if it meets the threshold to be an offence in clause 2. Is that correct? If so, what resource impact will that have on the police and on the CPS in particular?

Tom Guest: The police retain their discretion on whether, and how, to investigate. They are not beholden to take forward every complaint; they must consider it, but they decide whether to investigate. To return to your previous line of questioning, before we would get to a jury, the police would first have to conclude that they had finished an investigation and that there was a realistic prospect of conviction. They would then have to ask the CPS for a charging decision, and the CPS would have to decide to prosecute. That is before it gets to a jury.

On your example of a parent talking a phone from a child, I talked about it being the ordinary use of the word—I hope that any ordinary use of the word “abusive” would not capture that situation. But if that was the concern, hypothetically, remember that before it would get to a jury, first, the police would have to investigate and decide that it goes further, and secondly, the CPS would be the gatekeeper for whether someone is prosecuted. We will come on to the consent question. I would put that in the overall context.

Chair: Before I move on to Lord Isaac’s question, Baroness Brown wants to put a point to you.

Baroness Brown of Silvertown: Mr Guest, you mentioned the word “prayer”. I presume you can envisage a situation in which prayer can be an abusive conversion therapy.

Tom Guest: Yes. That is exactly why, in our view, what is “abusive” should be a non-exhaustive list. It should be the ordinary use of that word.

Lord Isaac: I was going to ask about thresholds, but Sir Jeremy covered that. Turning to the Bill as drafted, I want to briefly examine the scope that you think is covered by “serious alarm or distress”. We have heard various examples of parents’ conduct in various day-to-day habits, such as withholding pocket money. What are your views on what might or might not count as “serious alarm or distress” in that context?

Tom Guest: There are two separate parts that are interlinked: the “serious alarm or distress”, but also the “substantial adverse effect” that it has. It is not mere “alarm or distress”, but “serious alarm or distress”; it would also have to have that causal link.

On what I said about different sources of evidence, the same applies here: what does the evidence show about the impact on the usual day-to-day activities of the complainant? I do not make light of the need to investigate and understand that. Although it is one limb of harm, it should be borne in mind that it effectively has at least three important elements: “serious”, then “alarm or distress”, and then the causation of disruption to day-to-day activities.

Lord Isaac: Absolutely, but could I push you on that? It is hard to generalise, of course, but might there be circumstances where the thresholds for each of those limbs are met and where day-to-day parental activities are covered?

Andrew Thomas: Again, this is an element that we are familiar with addressing in relation to controlling or coercive behaviour. I can remember directing a jury where that was very much an issue in a case; it was around what had been the real impact of, in that case, a daughter’s behaviour towards her mother. It is a real-world situation that we do deal with.
