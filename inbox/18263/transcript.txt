Examination of witnesses

Damian Collins, Baroness Kidron, Professor Lorna Woods and Sir Jeremy Wright.

The Chair: Good afternoon and welcome to this meeting of the Communications and Digital Select Committee. Today we will continue our inquiry into the Online Safety Act and examine how far the implementation of the Act to date matches up with the intentions of those involved in developing and scrutinising the legislation. All of our witnesses today played an important role in that process, and I thank you for joining us today. Before we move on to questions, I shall ask you each to introduce yourselves. You do not need introduction to many of us, but please do so for the broadcast.

Sir Jeremy Wright: I was the Secretary of State at the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport between 2018 and 2019, which is when we produced the online harms White Paper. Then, having left government, I chaired the All-Party Parliamentary Group on Digital Regulation and Responsibility, which acted, I hope, as a forum for several in both Houses, some of whom are around this table, to discuss the progress of the Bill and amendments to it.

Professor Lorna Woods: I am professor emerita at the University of Essex. Until recently I was professor of internet law there and a member of the Human Rights Centre, and I am adviser to the Online Safety Network. Thank you.

Damian Collins: When I was in the House of Commons, I chaired the Lords and Commons Joint Committee on the Draft Online Safety Bill, which was the pre-legislative scrutiny committee. Before that, I chaired the DCMS Select Committee and was briefly Minister for the Bill at the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport as well.

Baroness Kidron: I am a Cross-Bench Peer in the House of Lords. I was also on the pre-leg committee under the chairmanship of Damian, and I played quite a big part in the passage of the Bill.

The Chair: I am sure you have all seen that Ofcom has recently published a report showing that there has been no significant change in children’s exposure to harmful content, nine months after the children’s safety duties under the Online Safety Act came into force. Given that, in your view, does the Act still have the potential to achieve its original aims, or does the framework that it established now need fundamental reform?

Sir Jeremy Wright: The answer to that is yes, it still has that potential. I suppose I should declare, as the committee knows, a sense of parental responsibility for this Act, so I am perhaps not its most impartial observer, but I think the structure of the Act will survive the test of time. We had a choice back in the day when we were trying to decide the way in which we should approach this challenge: either we could have a rules-based regulation approach, where essentially we had a list of dos and don’ts for online platforms, or we could take a different, much more principles-based approach to regulation based on a duty-of-care model. Lorna and Will Perrin, who is probably the other intellectual godfather of this particular approach, persuaded me at the time that the duty-of-care approach was right, and I am still persuaded of that. If you pursue a rules-based approach, with a list of dos and don’ts, then what you find in this space above all others is that your list becomes out of date almost as soon as you have finished writing it. So I think the approach broadly set out in the Bill is the right one.

What we have to recognise, and this is probably reflected in the Ofcom report, is that the harms multiply all the time. There are many more and many different kinds of online harms that particularly affect children, so the fact that we appear to be standing still does not necessarily mean we are failing; it may mean that the harms are multiplying and we are trying to keep pace with them. However—and you may find that this is a theme of the evidence we all give to you today—it is important that the Act is properly implemented. I think we are now in a place where the Act allows for more action than the regulator is prepared to take. A large part of the problem is that we have handed over a piece of legislation, imperfect though I accept that it is, that could be used more effectively by the regulator than it is currently.

Professor Lorna Woods: I agree with what Jeremy has said. Maybe there is a certain amount of bias, given my work on why the duty of care would be a good thing. Focusing on the choice architecture of the services reflects the fact that they are not neutral as to content and behaviours. It is also something that the companies control, so to me it seems legitimate and appropriate to link that responsibility with some responsibility for doing something about it.

I had assumptions, which perhaps proved unfounded, as to how far the regulator was prepared to go without an explicit steer in the legislation. You can see in the Ofcom codes that there is a focus on aspects that were specifically mentioned in the legislation. For example, there is a lot in the codes on the requirement to have an effective takedown system. Illegal content codes for user-to-user services and highly effective age verifications are two specific things that the Act required, and it is noticeable that they are the two specific things that Ofcom has focused on, so maybe the regulator was at sea.

To pick up on a couple of points that Jeremy made, the world has changed. Having a principles-based approach and an approach that focuses on requiring services to look at what they are developing helps with future-proofing. For that reason, I do not think that moving away from the approach we have at the moment would be right. The Online Safety Act could never have been a silver bullet; it looks at a particular segment of the market and its particular role in an environment. You are going to have to look at people posting content and the rules relating to that as well, and how those fit in.

To be the voice of pragmatism: if you try to bring in something entirely new, that disincentivises services from complying with what we have now, and then there is the time lag. It is difficult to assess how well the regime is functioning at the moment, partly because there was the time lag in the codes coming in. Then we need to wait for enforcement, and we are only really at the beginning of seeing that enforcement.

Damian Collins: Chairman, the premise of your question is that the Act has not delivered the results that were hoped for and therefore the question has to be why that is the case. I do not disagree with what Lorna or Jeremy have said, and I am sure during the course of the afternoon we will not disagree that much, but I will say that at the heart of this debate is a problem in the way that Ofcom is interpreting the powers that it has been given. Clause 10 of the Act deals with the safety duties relating to illegal harms, and you have to look at Ofcom’s written evidence to this committee and use your judgment as to whether it has interpreted the Act in the way that it is written and the way that Parliament intended at the time, specifically regarding the extent to which platforms have proactive obligations to scan for and mitigate the existence of illegal content. In the written evidence to the committee, Ofcom says that those do not exist, but the Act clearly says that there is a duty to prevent access.

Canada has recently introduced its own version of the Online Safety Act, the Safe Social Media Act, which expressly says there is no obligation to proactively scan; it is written in the Bill. It was always intended that the illegal safety duties in the Online Safety Act would give guidance to the companies. The duty of care rested on a series of priority legal offences. We expected the companies to have systems in place to address those needs. It did not require perfection, but in our view it required the companies to be aware of those obligations, and they would proactively seek to identify illegal content, act against it in order to make it hard for users to find and remove it, rather than waiting for someone to complain and then retrospectively deal with it.

In fact, during the passage of the legislation, one of the lobbies from the tech companies said that, as we were no longer in the European Union, the e-commerce directive no longer applied. In that commerce directive, the companies are protected from an obligation to monitor generally what goes on on these platforms. We did not want to introduce that in the Online Safety Act because we knew it would be used as a get-out-of-jail-free card by the companies; every time they were found to have failed in their duties, they could say, “Ah yes, but there’s no obligation for us to monitor everything”.

The way that the provisions in Clause 10 have been interpreted by the regulator is wrong. It is certainly not what we intended, and I think it is at the heart of why enforcement has been weak.

Baroness Kidron: I broadly agree with what everyone has said. The report from Ofcom that you mentioned at the outset, Chair, is a travesty. It took 10 years to get here but then, nine months after implementation, nothing has changed. I just want to put into this room the voices of desperate parents and children at the other end of that “nothing has changed” so that we do not forget about their experience as we talk about the technical aspects of the Bill.

I largely agree with my colleagues: yes, it does still work, but there are four things that have to be thought about. The first is whether the powers that have been given to Ofcom have been adequately interpreted. As you have heard, the general view on this side of the table is that they have not.

Secondly, what has changed, what has moved, what could we not have anticipated at the time and what did the Government of the day not allow us to put in? Where are the gaps, and by what mechanism will they be filled?

The third is a culture question about Ofcom: is it too cumbersome? It has a big budget and hundreds of people, but it is very hard to get a sense of urgency. Even in the recommendations there are a number of things that it could decide to do right now, but it is waiting for future action from the Government. This “slightly more in sorrow than in anger” sort of communication from Ofcom is something that the committee might look at and say, “Are they supervisory or are they enforcers?” If they are enforcers then it is on them that it is not working, or their recommendations would have been, “We need this or that power right now in order to do our job”.

The fourth issue is enforcement, where I have some sympathy with Ofcom. I do not think the enforcement powers are all there. Unless there is proper enforcement capability, the Act will not work.

One of the critical mistakes by the Government, the regulator and, to a degree, even Parliament itself was that we were all used to, and had in our imagination, regulation of sectors that are used to being regulated and willing to be so, but I cannot emphasise enough how, both ideologically and practically, the tech sector does not see itself as being willing to be regulated. When you come up with your final recommendations, it is important to understand that and see how enforcement needs to change, which we may get to later. That is critical to the way forward, and it is something that sits with all of us, not just the regulator.

The Chair: You have been very comprehensive. I am going to try and stick to the time that we have available, which means that you do not all need to speak on every question.

The Lord Bishop of Winchester: In a sense I want to pick up on your last point about a tech sector that does not want to be regulated, but I shall specifically talk about the Act’s safe harbour provision. There is some feeling that this seems to have created a ceiling rather than a floor for platforms to mitigate risk. Do you think that is right? Should the provision be revised and what, if anything, should replace it?

Baroness Kidron: I absolutely think it should be revised. There is one view of safe harbour that could mean that the regulator could see that a tech company had done all that it could have but still some harm happened; however, because it did all that it could, it was offered safe harbour. How it has been rather bureaucratically interpreted—and I would go to Lorna on whether it needed to be that way—is that they have said, “Okay, if you do everything in our codes then you are fine”, even though we have found many risks outside of the codes, and that creates an automatic gap from day one.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Can I slightly disagree with that? I think there is merit in safe harbour provisions. Because we are in a very flexible and fast-moving landscape, it is important that we are fair to those that we are regulating. If we are simply going to say to them, “We want you to keep your users safe”, but not explain precisely or at least roughly how that might be done, then that would be unfair to them. However, the safe harbour provisions need to work on the basis that Ofcom, as the regulator, has higher, not lower, expectations. I fear that what has gone wrong here is that Ofcom’s expectations are too low.

What is in the codes of practice—offering safe harbour if you do what they say—is, frankly, not ambitious enough. That is strange to me, because these are some of the most inventive companies on the planet. We can expect them to innovate all over the place in order to advance their own cause or offer us a product we are more willing to buy, so I do not see why we should not expect them to be equally imaginative and innovative when they are presenting safer options or worrying about the safety of their users. I want to see the interpretation of safe harbour being, “We offer you safe harbour if you’re innovative and ambitious and if you do as much as you proportionately can”. We might come back to the definition of proportionate because that is important, but it is important in this context to expect an ambitious response, and that is what is missing.

Damian Collins: Jeremy makes an important point: there is nothing in the Online Safety Act requiring companies to do something that they do not already have the technology to do. They do it to target advertising content at users but they do not use it to keep people safe.

One of the big structural differences between the Online Safety Act and the DSA in the EU is the fact that the safety duties concern defined harms based on laws that Parliament has already passed rather than fundamental principles of human rights. The reason for that was to help with enforceability. It was not to create a safe harbour where a minimum safety standard could be achieved and nothing more beyond it would be expected; the expectation was that it would be a floor, not a ceiling. Ofcom is already facing judicial reviews from the tech companies but we thought that, down the line, the less specific and clear the duties and what they were based on, the more open that would be to legal challenge later on. So the intention was to create certainty for the platform, in terms of both what it needed to do and what enforcement would come if it failed.

Professor Lorna Woods: I agree with what has been said. The real problem is the subject matter in the codes and the perception, seemingly by Ofcom, that it has to give specific steps rather than programmatic obligations. That is the heart of the problem. I think the regime was that companies should try their best to minimise, not that they should come up with perfection. But what they have is a rather low-level tick-box list, which has been constrained by availability of evidence and Ofcom’s concern that everything should be very precisely measurable—seemingly.

One other point on the DSA: it also envisages codes but does not have a safe harbour provision. As they are not binding, they are of a different nature to the codes in the Online Safety Act, but the DSA sees complying with codes as evidence of attempting to satisfy duties rather than an absolute guarantee.

Lord Holmes of Richmond: What implications would reform of safe harbour have for legal certainty and Ofcom’s ability to enforce the Act? How could any difficulties be addressed? Can Beeban answer first, please.

Baroness Kidron: Sorry, I did not quite understand, Chris. Were you asking about removing safe harbour?

Lord Holmes of Richmond: If you were looking to change safe harbour, what implications would that have on legal certainty and Ofcom’s abilities?

Baroness Kidron: I have always been interested in how the risk register, where Ofcom sets out the totality of risk, is much richer, much better and more straightforward than the codes themselves. It is interesting what colleagues have said, because there seems to me to be little difference between having a safe harbour that guarantees you if you have tried everything and have been ambitious versus having no safe harbour, as in not having the low bar of a code.

In spring 2025, when Ofcom was doing these codes, somebody came to me from within the companies and said, “This is ridiculous. We are going to fix feasibility at what we say we can do in 2025, and it will be there for all time”. It is a floor and it is pushing us downwards. The safe harbour is part of what makes it unambitious.

I agree with others that there must be some flexibility of interpretation like there is in the DSA. But when I went to Ofcom and said, “Could your codes be guidance and the risk register be what you are going for?”, it said, “But how do we measure that?” I said, “I do not understand how you measure against the code”. At some point, you always have to make a judgment about whether you have reached the bar. I think that the bar should be the overarching risk register. That is better.

Damian Collins: One of the benefits of the structure that we have created—and I have heard tech companies talk about why they much prefer working within the OSA than the DSA—is that you can go to a regulator and say, “If we address this problem in this way, is that going to be good enough?” That is fair enough if that leads to better regulation. What was never intended was that, if the system put in place clearly had failed and is not delivering the results expected, it can carry on failing without any intervention, when the regulator, because it consented to that approach being taken, is powerless to do anything about it. It was clearly meant as a system where risks were assessed and companies put policies in place to address those risks, but clearly that is not enough. The responsibility on the company to meet that risk should still exist. Again, the codes of practice have been interpreted in such a way that the obligations of the companies do not go beyond what is expressly called for in the code, regardless of the risks or the safety needs of the users. That is wrong; it is certainly not what we intended when the legislation was passed.

Professor Lorna Woods: I sometimes wonder why the concern about uncertainty arises here when it does not arise in relation to product safety or in relation to the Act about building standards brought in following the Grenfell Tower tragedy—I cannot remember its name. It is a similar sort of theory of putting the responsibility on the person responsible for creating or constructing, but here and here alone we suddenly see concerns about uncertainty. I do not know why this sector claims to be so different.

Obviously, when you have a new regime, there will always be questions and some bedding in, so you end up with a better understanding of rules of thumb once you have some process and experience to guide you. However, I am not sure why there should be a problem here, comparatively, which we do not see elsewhere.

Sir Jeremy Wright: I agree with all of that. When Lorna and I were discussing the principles of this way back in the mists of time, we envisaged an overarching duty of care that would be the keystone to this entire legislative edifice. That has gone, and the duty of care has been broken down into its component parts. Had we retained it, there would have been the opportunity to say, “Whatever else we say to you about detail, you’ve got this residual overarching duty of care to keep your users as safe as you reasonably, proportionately, can”.

The other point is that, as I said earlier, the regulated are entitled to an expectation—to have some idea of what this duty of care means in practical terms. If you are not expecting a good deal of people, they will default to the minimum you are asking of them. One of the problems I have with the way in which Ofcom has approached the way in which it decides what to put into the codes of practice is that it has decided that it can only recommend in a code of practice something that would be proportionate to expect a platform to do. So far, so reasonable but, when you define “proportionate” as meaning “We know it works and have evidence to show that it works”, you are actually fixing the benchmark at current good practice, because you have evidence that it works only if people have been doing it and probably doing it for long enough to create an evidence base.

My worry comes back to this point about lack of ambition. If Ofcom is saying that it will only recommend something in a code of practice, something which then gives protection from regulatory intervention—because that is what safe harbour means—and if it sets the benchmark at things it can prove work because people are already doing them, this is problematic. You can fix things at the status quo but, in a fast-moving landscape, the whole point is that we want these platforms to scan the horizon and be responsive to problems that they see emerging.

Worse than that, an innovator—a platform out there trying to do its best and move the needle on safety such that it is doing something exciting and newly effective in keeping its users safe—is then being told that it is better off retrenching back to the standard current practice, because that gives it regulatory protection. That is the exact opposite of what we are trying to achieve, which is to encourage platforms to be ambitious and to be scanning the horizon themselves, and have the weight of obligation on them through the duty of care model, so that regulators and legislators, like all of us, do not have to run to catch up all the time.

Lord Holmes of Richmond: I would like to explore, particularly with Lorna and Jeremy, your points around an overarching duty of care. There was an interesting exchange in the Joint Committee sessions in 2021 between a certain Lord Knight and the then Minister Dorries, when this point about an overarching duty of care came up. Her comments at that time were along the lines that it could not get past parliamentary counsel. She discussed with you, Jeremy, that it was not a runner. Is that your recollection? Do you believe that it would not be possible to have such a model of an overarching duty of care?

Sir Jeremy Wright: It is rare for me to disagree with Nadine, but on this occasion I do. I do think it was viable. Again, if you go back—Lorna will correct me if I have misremembered this—the original thought was that we can do something in the online space similar to the operation of the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974. The Act does not require you to do something specific with the buckets on the floor or the shelving, specifically. What it says is that you have to create a safe environment. The objective here was to do the same online, and I am not persuaded that that would not have worked. As I say, it would have addressed some of the problems which we have identified already in this session. It is not a panacea. I accept that, and it is not easy to do, but it is feasible. What it would have done is set the tone for the obligation being on the platforms to do what they sensibly can to keep their users safe. I am perfectly prepared to accept, and always was, that if platforms do all that they reasonably can to keep their users safe and something bad happens to a vulnerable user none the less, I do not expect them to be in the stocks for that, but we need to set our expectations high because, again, these are companies capable of meeting high expectations. We are not asking companies to do something that they cannot do, and we should not ask them to do something they cannot do. This is asking them only to do what they can do. That should be our expectation.

Lord Holmes of Richmond: So it is not your understanding that an overarching duty of care will be problematic from the parliamentary counsel perspective?

Sir Jeremy Wright: I am going to defer to a better lawyer than I am. Lorna will tell you what she thinks but my view is that we should at least have tried. That is the kind of Bill that I would have wanted to introduce, because it is an important element that ended up being missing.

Lord Holmes of Richmond: I appreciate that.

Professor Lorna Woods: Unless someone comes up with a specific problem, I am struggling to think what the problem is because we have other sectors where a similarly broad approach has been taken. There may be problems but they are just not leaping to my mind at the moment.

Damian Collins: If you go back to the moment of that exchange, the Bill was framed and structured differently. The idea of having priority legal offences in a schedule of existing Acts of Parliament written on the face of the Bill that the safety duties will be built on was not in the Bill at the time. Actually, one of the challenges at the time was: what does the duty of care rest on? Does it rest on the Human Rights Act? Does it rest on, as it does in the EU, the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union? What does it rest on? There was some concern at the time about whether we were then delegating to a regulator lots of interpretations of rules around speech, in particular, which would be where the regulator would be giving rulings on harm based on behaviours that were not necessarily unlawful.

The question at the time was very much: what does that duty of care rest on? We felt the duty of care rested on the priority legal offences, and the proper enforcement of the platform’s own terms of service. It is interesting that Ofcom is saying in its evidence, certainly with regards to younger children accessing their services, it is not entirely certain what powers it has. Again, it is another example that the way in which the powers that were given to the regulator have been used or interpreted is weaker than we thought it would be at the time.

Baroness Kidron: I am in Lorna’s school. I heard all those debates and no one gave a good reason. Maybe the useful thing to say about parliamentary counsel at the time is this: we also had an excruciating exchange about whether there was anything else online apart from content—as in parliamentary counsel could not understand that the online world was made up of data, behaviours and activities way beyond the content itself. I am sure we are going to come to it in a minute regarding some of the other things that the Act therefore failed to get at. The compromise, if you look at the Bill, was that it says that content means content and activity but it has not been interpreted as such. It has only been interpreted as content.

My reason for saying that is that it is worth the committee understanding two things. One, which is fair enough, is that it felt like novel legislation. People were anxious. You live and you learn. But something much more important is that there was an absolute failure of understanding within the department about what the technology could and could not do, and where the dangers lay. I am afraid that the voices in the horseshoe were not taken quite seriously enough at that time.

Lord Tarassenko: I am interested, possibly because of my engineering background, in design and design choices. In fact, there is an interesting blog by the president of the Royal Academy of Engineering about making the right designs for chatbots and so on, which you may have read. I think everybody in the horseshoe and in this room will probably agree that the Act has not succeeded in making online services safe by design. I have read from cover to cover the document produced by the coalition involving the 5Rights Foundation, the Molly Rose Foundation, the Online Safety Network, the NSPCC and yourself, Lorna. It has some interesting ideas but I wanted to explore a little as to whether that is one way to go. One of the things that you advocate together with that document is that the Government should introduce a statutory definition of safety by design and then require Ofcom to develop a dedicated code in the same way in which you have put a template together.

I have one question, but it is slightly different for the members of the panel today. For Jeremy and Damian, it is a general question. What would the type of proposals in this model of a safety by design code of practice require platforms to do differently from what they are doing now? Does Ofcom have the powers to introduce these different measures? For Lorna, which parts of the code could Ofcom adopt tomorrow under Section 41 and which would need legislation? Are the technical amendments that you mentioned in your document amendments to the Act? Finally, Beeban, I will give you the choice to take the general question or the specific question.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Safety by design was always the intention. That was always the guiding principle for those of us who designed this Bill from the outset. We have been disappointed to see that the focus has moved away from that towards an unnecessary focus on content itself—specific pieces of content. Of course, those are important but the design features of the websites and platforms we are talking about are a fundamental aspect of the harm that we could all see being caused and that we wish to address. It picks up exactly on the point Beeban was just making about the way in which things happen here. It is not just about whether there is bad stuff out there online. It is the fact that the bad stuff is being served up to you, particularly if you are a child, in a way that drags you towards it. It is not you going looking for it specifically. It is the algorithm and the recommender system being designed in such a way as to promote that material to you. That is a design feature. It has to be addressed through the legislation and the work of the regulator, building on that legislation. Now, I take the view—this is a constant theme, I am afraid—that there is plenty in the Act already that allows Ofcom to do that if it wishes.

Many of us worked hard to get words like “functionality” into the Act more often than at the start. That is because “functionality” covers exactly what I am describing. If you look at the way in which this Act is designed, it starts with asking the platforms to do a risk assessment, do it properly and, having identified risks, go on to address and mitigate those risks. Setting out how you go about risk assessment and what you need to look for is exactly what this Act does. Included in the list of things you have to do if you are risk assessing, particularly for material that might be harmful to children, is a list of things, including the functionality of the platform and, for example, anything about the design of the platform that keeps children on it for longer. That is rather forgotten about but it is an important part of the harm that is being done. So quite a lot of this is in the Act already.

I return to the same point. We have to be enforcing the legislation we already have. I am not against further legislation. I never thought that the Online Safety Act would be the last word on the subject. We must do the best we can with what we have first before we can make a credible argument for more legislation.

Damian Collins: There has always been a tension in the debate here. The Online Safety Act does two novel things: it establishes broad areas of intermediary liability for platforms that do not exist in America, which is why the companies have always struggled with it, and it regulates feeds.

This debate about safety by design is not an unhelpful debate to have for people who are thinking about how you regulate AI. If you are saying that you have designed a system to work in a particular way, draw data in a particular way and produce certain kinds of outcomes, is that safe or not? Content feeds, for many users, work in a safe and benign way: they could direct people towards music or films that they might like. For some people, though, there are devastating, catastrophic failures.

I think the Act should be clear that there is not a safe way to recommend self-harm and suicide content to a teenage girl. The design of the platform should prevent that happening; otherwise, it has clearly failed in its safety duties. That is how I see it. What the Act does not really address are things like excessive use—the extent to which addiction is harmful. It relies on categories of harm to help the regulator to make that decision.

In some ways, I go back to what I said at the beginning. The regulator’s interpretation of Section 10 of the Act is partly responsible here. That section says that the safety duty is to prevent people from encountering illegal content, which is about the design of the system or platform, not individual pieces of content. It is the ability to use categories of content as an exemplar to proactively scan and identify similar content, to remove that content or mitigate it in some other way so that the user does not experience it, and to train those systems so that they do not recommend content that is illegal.

We know from other areas of platform moderation that they experiment all the time with the way the feeds work. They can upgrade and downgrade certain categories of content and where it is recommended from on the platform to change the outcome of the user experience. They do this all the time; it is well documented. I think the Act should require companies to manage the design of their platforms in such a way as to meet their safety duties.

Professor Lorna Woods: I want to say first that I agree with Jeremy. When I was thinking about this, I assumed that a duty of care would be aimed at ensuring that services were safer and that the key thing would be the way that they are designed, developed and managed. That seems to have got lost somewhere, maybe during the long legislative process, so it is important to re-establish that.

The House of Lords did manage to get in the reference to “safe by design” in Section 1, but it would be helpful to elaborate on that, not least because different sectors perhaps look at the topic slightly differently. You have “by design” discussions in data protection and cyber security, then in engineering as well. So part of the reason that we suggested that there should be a definition was to try to reconcile the different viewpoints.

When I was reading the literature, I tried to synthesise that into three.
