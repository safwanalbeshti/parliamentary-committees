Chair: Welcome to the Science and Technology Committee’s second evidence session in our inquiry into the exciting area of low-energy computing. This inquiry grew out of our “Under the Microscope” initiative, through which researchers drew our attention to the potential of neuromorphic computing and silicon photonics. In our first session, we explored the science behind these technologies and their potential to make computing more energy efficient, and the implications of that.

Today, we are going to turn to their practical application and the industry perspective. We want to understand where these technologies could deliver meaningful benefits, how soon they might be adopted more widely and what barriers stand in their way. To help us understand that, I am really pleased to welcome our first panel. I am going to ask each of these industry experts to introduce themselves and their organisations as they explain the neuromorphic technology that they use. Dr Mangoma, can you introduce yourself and explain the technology you are using and what is so special about it?

Dr Mangoma: Thank you for having us. My name is Tanya Mangoma. I am the chief product officer at Analogue Insight. We are a designing IP house, which basically means that, for companies, we take the ideas from a PowerPoint presentation all the way to silicon. We work with all the big foundries to be able to deliver that, and design for them. We focus on a specific skillset called analogue and mixed-signal design, and that is key component for neuromorphic computing, especially when you are talking about analogue neuromorphic computing.

I have a PhD in neuromorphic computing and, since graduating, I have been trying to commercialise the technology within the UK, facing many barriers, but overcoming some. Hopefully today I will be able to share more about what is happening in the ecosystem and how we could help as Analogue, but also as personalities.

Chair: Thank you. When you talk about analogue and neuromorphic computing, can you say a little about how that works? What do you mean by it?

Dr Mangoma: In computing, there are two types: digital, which is basically on and off, and analogue, which is basically signal processing, which is the analogue part. For signal processing, this was done years ago and SpiNNaker was actually one of the examples—I do not know if you are aware of Steve Furber and what he did in Manchester with SpiNNaker. He made digital compute look like analogue signal because he wanted to replicate spiking neural networks in compute. For my PhD I used technologies, chips and materials that inherently work as spiking neural networks so you do not have to emulate them. You actually emulate the brain; you do not have to simulate what they do in software.

Chair: They work as spiking neural networks.

Dr Mangoma: Yes.

Chair: What is the “spiking”?

Dr Mangoma: There are neural networks as we know them at the moment, then there are spiking neural networks, which bring in a time component so that, all of a sudden, you have an event-based kind of compute that you can harness. No longer do you train something and keep it trained. At the moment, you train an LLM and train ChatGPT, and then you have to wait for OpenAI to train another model before it is released. A spiking neural network trains as you do work in real time. That is where you get the spiking plasticity, or spiking neural network, coming in.

Professor Kenyon: That is partly true. Yes, there are elements that we would like to emulate but there are also lessons that we want to take from the brain that we can apply in different ways. If we interface that with photonics, where we have much higher data rates, we can then start to work in different applications at very high speeds and take inspiration. Chair: Excellent. What are the biggest material benefits that neuromorphic computing can offer the UK—which would you identify?

Dr Mangoma: To start off with there is the low-energy compute aspect of it.

Chair: May I ask you to speak up a bit, or come forward towards the microphone?

Dr Mangoma: Okay, I’ll speak up a bit. In neuromorphic computing the big area that is noticeable is the energy aspect. As soon as you have neuromorphic, or brain-like, computing, you automatically have lower energy consumption when you are doing AI influence and training, in general. That is well established and well acknowledged. That is the first way we would benefit from having neuromorphic computing.

The second part is in an area called “sense, detect, act”, or edge computing, where—in areas such as autonomous vehicles, UAVs, drone capacity and cyber-security—you want to be able to sense something extremely quickly and act extremely quickly. That is where neuromorphic computing shines a lot more than conventional computing. Those are the two areas that I see the most benefit in: the energy aspect, and what it can do for edge computing—for an area now called physical AI in general.

Professor Kenyon: I can add something to that. On where there are other opportunities, absolutely, there are opportunities in energy and security, but there is a third one. We talk a lot about data centres and the fact that we have to build ones that are bigger and bigger and faster and faster to deal with the deluge of data that we are experiencing.

Neuromorphic computing offers a different way of doing things: maybe we do not need to generate the data in the first place. Here, I make a distinction between a signal, which is something in the environment that we are detecting; data, which is what we turn the signal into, conventionally; and information, which is the important thing. When we look at the way the brain operates, it does not take the whole environment and commit that to memory; it pays attention to the things that are important.

A concrete example is a technology known as an event camera, in which there are a series of pixels that produce a signal or data only when something changes in the environment. That means that if something changes in a small part of the environment, that is the data that is produced, rather than the whole image being digitised. If we can take that example and apply it in other domains as well as in vision, you can see the potentially huge advantages in us not having to produce the data in the first place.

Chair: That is great; we will come back to examine it in more detail. I should declare an interest: I am an electrical engineer and a fellow of the Institution of Engineering and Technology, which is one of the reasons I find this discussion so interesting, especially your points about how it takes much less signal and data to share the changes rather than to share or generate everything.

Finally, on the application that jumps out, as Dr Mangoma mentioned, around reducing the power requirement, I have in my notes that a human brain uses 20 W of power to process data that would take around 1 MW of power to process in a data centre: that is to say, the human brain uses 2% of the power that existing compute does. Surely, that would revolutionise and have a real impact on the value of existing data centre infrastructure investments, which is in the trillions of dollars or pounds. Is that true?

Dr Mangoma: Absolutely, that is true. To clarify why that is, current data centres are built on digital compute. That means that the compute aspect is on one end, the memory is on another, and the data is moving between them. The memory is always on, so it is always consuming power, and the data is always moving. If it is moving from one server to another, from one data facility to another or from one compute edge to another, that all consumes energy. That is where all the energy is going. It is not going into compute; it is going into just moving the data and the storage of the memory, which is volatile.

The brain does not separate memory and compute—they are one thing and exist in one place—so all of a sudden, you are not moving data, and when you are not moving data, all of a sudden, that energy cost disappears, in all honesty. Even applying the simplest terms of neuromorphic computing—the co-location of memory and compute—you get the added advantages of not moving the data in the first place.

The second aspect comes with things such as spiking neural networks. The timing dependency of that spike goes to what you were talking about on signal processing: which signal is important? What information should we move? What data should we generate? That is where that comes in, to cut even further what data is moved between the two boundaries.

Freddie van Mierlo: It was Sam Altman.

Chair: What did you call the RAM used?

Dr Mangoma: ReRAM—resistive RAM. It is one of the many types.

Professor Kenyon: That is the type of memory that my spin-out is trying to commercialise.

Professor Kenyon: There are a couple of things to unpick there. I will start by saying that things are better now than they were, say, seven or eight years ago. When we were spinning out our company, we could not get investment, because the perception was that the UK does not do semiconductors. It is a combination of both public and private money. There needs to be an ecosystem that creates support for early-stage companies to get through this valley of death, which is the bit between what you can do in a university and what you need to then go to a foundry and unlock the capabilities of a TSMC or UMC. If you are developing a new technology in this space, a rule of thumb is that it will take you $100 million to get from having the technology in the laboratory to having something commercial. That cannot all come from public money; it needs to come from investment, and it needs to be patient capital.

What is holding us back in the UK is, first of all, the availability of sufficient capital, but the second thing is the risk appetite. It is very true that if you go to the west coast, to California, and present the same technology, you are likely to get an order of magnitude more funding and a much greater risk appetite.

The way I characterise it is that, if you set up a company in the UK and attract some investment, and then the company fails, that is shame on you and your family for generations. If you do the same thing in California, it is, “Okay, you’ve failed at that and you’ve learned something. What can we give you now? We believe in the team. We do not necessarily believe in the technology, but we think the team can develop another technology.” That risk appetite is very different.

Then, around that, you need a broad ecosystem. Tanya spoke about prototyping and pilot lines; you absolutely need that. You need test facilities, access to design tools and so on and so forth. One area where the Government could do something very material to help with this would be procurement. Where a new technology is developed and is promising—for example for defence, healthcare, robotics or whichever sector—the Government could step in and say, “We will be the first customer for this new technology.”

There is a lot of work that needs to be done there.
