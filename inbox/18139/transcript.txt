Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Rt Hon Miatta Fahnbulleh MP and Jonathan Brearley.

Q749 Chair: Welcome to this afternoon’s session of the Energy Security and Net Zero Select Committee, where we welcome the new Secretary of State and the new Permanent Secretary to their first session with us. Congratulations, both of you, on your appointments. We are looking forward to taking evidence today. My colleagues are eagerly anticipating asking you their questions. I will start, as usual.

Secretary of State, how well are the Government doing on their ambition to hit the Clean Power 2030 target?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: That is a very good question. If you cast your mind back two years ago when we set the target, there was a fair bit of scepticism about whether it was a target that could be reached. If I am candid with you, what it has done is build a huge amount of momentum and confidence in the system. I think about what we have done over the course of the last two years. We have unlocked £100 billion of investment in clean power; we have consented enough clean power to power over 20 million homes. Last year was a significant year because renewables powered the power system two-thirds of the time in the year. We are making huge amounts of progress. There is more to do, but the key for me is that we keep momentum, we have a clear framework that industry can work with in partnership and we maintain focus on getting to that goal.

Q750 Chair: Thank you very much for your first answer. We, as the Committee, hear from developers a lot about their frustrations with the system. They would like to do things faster. I will ask you about solar first, for example. Solar, along with other technologies, saw the grid queue reordered, but they still face frustrations. One of the frustrations with solar is that those developers who are now proposing storage along with solar generation cannot see them both in the same gate. Their argument is that they then miss out—and the country misses out—on effectively two contributors to power generation. It is one example of a number that we hear. Are these blockages an indication that perhaps there needs to be a system overhaul, that Ofgem, NESO, grid companies and other parts of the system are not aligned to Government policy? If you agree with that, what is your intention in addressing some of these challenges?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Let me have a go, and I will also bring Jonathan in. I don’t think that different parts of the system are not aligned in our ambition. I think the truth is that we inherited a system where we had not put enough investment into network build. If you think about the trajectory over the last two decades, essentially the transmission operators were maintaining the system, and we are now asking them to invest and build out the system in order to ensure that the system is viable for the future. That is a big ask, so there is a mismatch and there are bottlenecks. The same frustrations you are getting from developers we are hearing as well and we are incredibly alive to it.

I think there are three things that need to be done. We have obviously done the process of reordering and, in essence, trying to get to connections that are genuine and that are important in terms of priority. I think everyone welcomes that, but there is more to do.

The second is that we do need to build out at pace. The conversation that we are having with the networks at the moment is how we do that and how we support and facilitate it. Part of the rationale for why we have launched Great British Grid is to accelerate the buildout of grid. There is a problem that we are aware of; there is a live conversation where we have everyone around the table trying to solve that problem. It is one thing unlocking the generation, but if the generation can’t connect, we have a collective problem. I think everyone recognises that.

We are trying to take the steps to address that, but what I would say—and I don’t think this is about bashing the networks—it is a hard thing that they are being asked to do against the backdrop of underinvestment, which I would put at the door of the last Government. We are asking them to ramp up at quite a pace, so there are bottlenecks in the system.

Jonathan Brearley: As you know, I am probably well placed to comment on both Ofgem and DESNZ. I don’t think the ambition is different. If you look at Ofgem’s programme around ASTI, a massive amount of investment was approved at pace. It is definitely pushing in the same direction as the Government, and the same with NESO, which issued the 2030 plan. I think the main issue is the scale of infrastructure that we are collectively building. There is a job for DESNZ there, so we have to make sure we do what we can to resource the planning system, which is one of the things that we need to get through to make sure we deliver this.

In addition, we need to make sure that things such as the grid connection queues are properly ordered. Now, having worked through that process, the scale and complexity of that task is big, but we are getting to a place where we have more stable connections as a result.

Q751 Chair: Yes. To come back to the point about battery storage and solar generation, developers are telling us they will get their development consent order for both, but they can’t develop both because they are in different stages in the gate system. That is holding back the development of clean power at the kind of speed that you want to see and that is needed to achieve your ambitions. That is not the only example. The Committee had examples of where businesses offer alternative technologies that are proven, but because their up-front costs are higher, they are rejected by the cap and floor mechanism, for example, with long-duration storage—we have a number of those examples—where over the lifetime they are significantly cheaper. This seems to be at odds with what you said, Secretary of State, a few minutes ago: that there is alignment.

I say again, these are the frustrations we are getting. Do you think that something needs to change?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Absolutely we think that something needs to change. We are not resiling from that. The same frustrations you are hearing, we are hearing as well. We are doing a huge amount of work across the networks to think about how we speed this up, because it is a bottleneck to us getting to the end destination that we want. We are having a genuine conversation around the trajectory of buildout that we expect, the ability of our transmission operators to deliver that and what you need to do to enable them. That is either dealing with practical barriers like workforce, but it is also thinking about where the Public Sector Challenges can step in to help us accelerate the buildout. We are trying to look at it in the round.

On the specifics of long-duration storage that you mentioned, I would say the wider principle is that we absolutely are trying to support and incentivise technologies that can get us through this transition. I can quote lots of different schemes and projects where the Department is backing innovation. That particular scheme was one in which we had record numbers of companies coming forward. It was very successful in that regard. Obviously Ofgem has just consulted on it and I think companies that didn’t quite like the original outcome have had the opportunity to make their case and then Ofgem will come forward. The wider point is that we are at quite an inflection point where we do need technologies and we need technologies that will deliver for the transition in a cost-effective way. As a Department, we are very much trying to create the space in which we nurture that and enable that innovation.

Q752 Chair: Jonathan, you might be able to comment on this. I wonder if the up-front cost issue is a problem here because that seems to be what is driving Ofgem decisions rather than the lifetime value. That is the allegation being put.

Jonathan Brearley: I know that, and I have to say that that is now an independent matter for Ofgem, but on the projects that have been approved, for example—

Q753 Chair: Isn’t that exactly the problem though, that if it is an independent matter for Ofgem, then Government-stated policy is more difficult to deliver? There is this dynamic, there is tension between its independence and Government policy, but isn’t that a big part of the problem?

Jonathan Brearley: Stepping back from that, I want to come on to the long-duration storage. In the Ofgem review we already have, the reason why we want to firm up the strategy and policy statement is because the Government sets policy and the regulator implements it. On the specifics of the capital versus the lifetime costs, I do think Ofgem will have to come back to you on the specific project. To give you an example, Coire Glas is a very high CAPEX project. Its benefits are very long term and that is one of the ones that got through the cap and floor minded-to decision. Ofgem is still consulting. It has closed its consultation and it is coming back to its decision, so it is still considering this. If you think about the policy goal, which was to get enough long-duration storage to create a sustainable system, we do think that cap and floor and the process so far will do that.

Q754 Chair: One last question from me before I pass on to the other members of the Committee: are you committed to the CfD mechanism for solar?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I think CfDs have been a very effective tool. I quoted the numbers in terms of investment that we have locked in for clean power, and that has been because of the CfD. Countries across the world are looking at us and thinking about how we have used that as a tool. We are constantly reviewing what technologies we use it for, how we use it and how we get that balance between incentivising the clean power that we need to get through versus making it fair for consumers.

Q755 Chair: Yes. Do you accept that current forecasts suggest the CfD will continue to deliver cheaper power through solar than alternative mechanisms?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I won’t get into the specifics, but the job that I see is that we have a tool that I think has been very effective in driving clean power. We will keep deploying that tool where we think it makes sense, but ultimately the balance that we have to strike is how we do that and incentivise the technology that we have, but make sure that it is fair cost and reasonable cost for consumers. That is the approach that we will take to every single technology.

Q756 Chair: I am curious about your answer because your stated priorities are affordability and bills—we all understand that and support it—but if you have a system that is doing that, why would you change it?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I don’t think we are planning to change it, but my point is that at the moment we are trying to think about it. The trajectory looks like this: we have done a huge amount, CfD has been a very effective mechanism and ultimately we have to constantly be stress-testing what it is unlocking in terms of investment versus the cost to consumers.

Chair: Thank you very much. I will pass over to Melanie Onn next, please.

Q757 Melanie Onn: Thank you, Chair. The UK has some of the highest industrial prices in Europe and that is holding back investment and manufacturing, with household bills continuing to be very high. What is the plan to bring energy costs down?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: We do have one of the highest electricity prices and I think it is a travesty and a scandal. You only have to talk to households or businesses and they are bearing the weight of this, so we are incredibly serious about affordability being the No. 1 priority for us. We have to get prices down.

I think there are three parts to the answer. There is stuff that we need to do to provide people and businesses with breathing space, so whether it is the action that we have taken on removing VAT from electricity bills, which is on top of the £150 of cost that we took off bills at the Budget, or it is schemes like the BICS, we are trying to do things to give some light relief, but my view is that is necessary but not sufficient, because we have to drive long-term structural change.

Three things sit behind our high prices. One is our acute exposure to the global fossil fuel market. That is why the clean power mission that we started talking about is so important, because we have to make sure that we have home-grown clean energy at prices that are more affordable. When I look across Europe at the moment and think about the countries that are the least exposed to the current crisis in the Middle East, it is the countries that have diversified their energy mix and have deployed hydropower or nuclear in order to do that. We have to deal with that; that is No. 1 one. For me, that is necessary, but not sufficient, which takes me to number two.

Second is that we have to acknowledge that if you look at the energy system in the round and ask yourself the question, “If what you care about are consumers and affordability, is this the system that you would design?”—that is essentially the exam question I have asked myself going into the Department—my answer is, “Probably not”. We are looking at structural reform across the system that is looking at how we deliver an energy system that is more cost-effective, whether that is on the retail side, the generation wholesale side or on the network side in order to drive cost down.

The third dimension is we have to invest in critical infrastructure if we want an energy system that endures, and endures through the transition that we must make. We have to ask ourselves: what is the fairest way to allocate the fixed costs in the system? Ofgem has a massive bit of work at the moment looking at cost allocation, but thinking about what that balance looks like between, candidly, the bill payer, whether domestic or non-domestic, the taxpayer and the private sector. That is the conversation that we need to have. That is a very long way of saying we recognise that this is a central problem. I think it is completely untenable that we have energy prices at the level that they are at.

Q758 Melanie Onn: The reality is though, Secretary of State, that unless we do genuinely decouple our electricity prices from the international gas market, we are never going to be able to set our own electricity prices. It doesn’t matter if you take off all the policy costs, how are you going to do that when we are stifled by the inability to build out grid? Because we cannot deliver the clean energy that you talk about and we cannot use it and we cannot get ourselves off the reliance on gas.

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Let me take it in two parts. I will talk about decoupling and then I will talk about the genuine constraints we have around generation versus our capacity to build.

We are on the process of decoupling. The first part of it is that we just need to bring more clean power on to the system, so gas drives less of the time. We are doing that and a huge amount of progress has been made over the last two years.

The second part of it is that what we have done with wholesale CfDs is essentially to fix more of the prices of renewables so it is not dictated by gas, so we are doing that. We are now asking ourselves the question: how do you take that forward? Irrespective, if you like, of where we are in the market structure, what are the steps that we can take to make sure that renewables reflect fair prices for renewables in a way that consumers benefit? Yes, there is a problem in terms of our network build. The conversation we just had a second ago I think illustrates that. We are not blind to that because, candidly, everyone is talking about the constraints and the kind of mismatch between what we are doing in terms of generation and where we are in terms of our build rate. I just think we have to be absolutely focused on tackling that. It is something that I am very exercised about and that the Department is exercised about, and we are working across the piece to do that.

I will come back to the point I made: yes, we will put pressure on the system to build out in the way that we think is necessary, but we also have to give the system just a tiny bit of slack because they have gone through a period of underinvestment in maintenance to a period of massive build. If you like, we are in the hump of that gear-out. We recognise that it is the responsibility of Government to do their part to enable that, but it is a hefty thing that we are asking the system to deliver.

Q759 Melanie Onn: Is Government funding that sufficiently?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I would say we are. It is a balance between the things that we can do as the Government and what we are doing through the recovery mechanisms on the bill. At the moment what I am hearing from industry is not that a lack of investment is the problem. It tends to be other constraints, for example workforce constraints.

I was talking to one of the contractors that build out the grid and they were like, “Just getting the workforce to do this at the pace that you are asking us to do this is very tough”. So, there are practical barriers. The thing that we are committed to doing is working in lockstep with industry to do it, because it is absolutely critical for the success of what we are trying to do.

Q760 Melanie Onn: You have said previously that you think that clean power is the only long-term solution to bringing down bills. Businesses are struggling; businesses are citing energy bills as being a serious issue. Do you think that there is a conflict between getting cheaper bills in the foreseeable future, meeting that clean power target by 2030 and looking at security of energy supply for the country?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I think my position is a bit more nuanced. I think clean power is necessary, but not sufficient. The reason I say that is that I think there is structural reform of the market that we also need to do. We have to deliver clean power because that is the thing that allows us to decouple ourselves from international fossil fuel markets. That will do quite a lot of the heavy lifting in terms of bills, but I fundamentally believe that we also have to reform the system and the market.

To answer the question, “How do we get it to work for consumers and how do we get it to deliver on affordability?” we have to do both things, which is pretty hard, but my view is that we don’t get to our end destination unless we do both things.

Q761 Melanie Onn: I only have a couple of minutes left and I just wanted to turn to CCUS very briefly, because there are reports that your Department has to find over £2 billion worth of savings to aid with the Government’s Budget. Potentially that includes cuts to carbon capture projects. Can you give any reassurance that those projects that are already in train, that are critical to the Government’s mission on reindustrialisation, will continue and continue to have government support?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Thank you for your letter, which I will formally respond to. The first thing I would say is obviously that the Chancellor would not thank me if I were to disclose decisions that ultimately he needs to make. There is obviously a Budget process going on at the moment. I think the Chancellor has—

Q762 Melanie Onn: But the decisions relating to projects will be with the Department, yes?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Yes, but if you like, the budget conversation is here. That is for the Chancellor and ultimately he will make a set of decisions and choices about the shape of the Budget.

What I can say about CCUS and hydrogen is that we are very clear that it is part of our trajectory. We started talking about our manufacturing and our heavy industry. There is no pathway to decarbonising unless we can help them do abatement. It is part of our strategy. We will do the work of supporting where we can and enabling where we can, but there is a hard choice for Government at the moment.

I would love to have the biggest budget possible and I would love to have finite amounts of resources. That is just not the world I am in, so we are asking ourselves the question: how do we work with industry to unlock the investment that we need to support to de-risk in the most cost-effective way? That might require us to use slightly different tools, including things like equity, but we are committed to that trajectory. The question is how we do it within the constrained resources that we have and in a way that balances the cost to consumers versus others.

Jonathan, is there anything else you would have said?

Jonathan Brearley: No.

Q763 Chair: Thank you, Secretary of State. You might want to reassure some of the businesses that are using CCUS and hydrogen. Melanie represents one part of the cluster, and I am very close to the other part. It is a real concern to them and is affecting their investment decisions right now. Are you having those conversations with them?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: We are having those conversations. These are key partners for us. We are on a journey together and we recognise our responsibility to give as much confidence. The thing that we have said consistently is we see that CCUS and hydrogen will have an important role in the mix, in the transition. We are working out the best way in which we can support within the resources that we have.

Q764 Chair: Thank you for that. I am going to turn to Sir Christopher in a moment, but you talked about affordability in clean power. Can you explain how clean power is delivering affordability?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Yes. For me, affordability is our central goal. I think clean power does, if you like, the first arc of that, which is if you ask yourself the question, “Why are bills going up at the moment and why are people under so much pressure?” it is because of conflicts miles away that are playing out on volatile global fossil fuel markets. That bit around getting home-grown energy here that we control is absolutely critical to that and that helps with the affordability journey.

The second bit is that piece we talked about, decoupling. If you allow that more renewables and more nuclear on the system drives prices rather than gas, that helps with affordability. I come back to the point I was making: it is the first step and it is very critical. It is necessary, but it is not sufficient. There are other things that we must do to drive out costs in the system.

Q765 Sir Christopher Chope: Melanie Onn asked about investment and you said you were trying to get investment from the private sector. Isn’t the offer from Centrica to provide £2 billion towards the gas storage facility at Rough just such an investment opportunity? Why is it that the Government are not accepting that investment offer from the private sector? Cold water was poured on it by your colleague, Luke Pollard, when responding to the interview that Chris O’Shea, the boss of Centrica, gave earlier on this week.

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I know Chris very well. We are working very closely with Centrica, including on this question. Ultimately the set of decisions that Centrica make are commercial decisions. We have engaged and we are engaging in a process because we are clear that storage is an important component. As we are thinking about all the conversations we are having about the state of the energy system, how we get more generation, also how we get more storage is absolutely critical. We are having a conversation about that in the round. We would prefer to have that conversation in private rather than in the media, but that is within the gift of Chris and other operators.

It is not the case that we are not engaging, we are engaging, but as you can imagine, there are many other propositions as well. The job of Government is to look in the round to say, “This is the outcome that we are trying to get to. What is the most cost-effective and efficient and resilient way in which we do that?” That is the process we are in at the moment.

Q766 Sir Christopher Chope: That all sounds delightfully vague. When is there going to be a firm decision in relation to the Rough application and particularly its application for support for a cap and floor regulatory framework to enable it to recover the costs of its £2 billion investment? Miatta Fahnbulleh: I will try to be as concise as I can. Let me take this in two turns. On the question of security of supply, it is very important to emphasise that we have a very diverse, resilient and robust security of supply. Q767 Sir Christopher Chope: You still have not identified the timescale. If this is urgent, when is this process that you are working going to be complete?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Imminent. I will use Whitehall speak: in due course and imminently. We are engaged in a conversation with it, but my point is that there are two parts of it. There is a question about this year and then there is a longer-term question about storage capacity and the role that Rough can play within that. We are having both of those conversations. For me, the latter is the one that is absolutely critical, but that has to be taken in the round with other conversations about where we can unlock storage capacity.

Q768 Sir Christopher Chope: Secretary of State, some people think that the reason you are not answering these questions and you are sitting on this issue is because you are prejudiced against the whole idea of the production and storage of gas, which is absolutely fundamental to our energy needs.

Miatta Fahnbulleh: No, absolutely not. Just to be very clear, I have been consistent—I am on the record—that gas is part of the mix. We are dependent on it in this transition, so that is not the issue. No one negates that we need storage and that we need gas storage, but you would expect me to look at it in the round in terms of the value for money of that proposition versus other propositions and to have a proper process with the company in question to get to that answer.

Q769 Sir Christopher Chope: Has the process you have described begun?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: Yes.

Jonathan Brearley: We have consulted on options around security of supply. It is the conclusion of that consultation that will give some answers.

Q770 Sir Christopher Chope: When is the closing date for the consultation?

Jonathan Brearley: I will have to come back with the specifics, but I think it is not very long away.

Q771 Sir Christopher Chope: It is all so vague, isn’t it? Anyway, can I turn to something else? There is rising scepticism among my constituents and people in this country about the value or otherwise of net zero policies. It is well summed up in a letter today in The Daily Telegraph from Dr Fiona Underhill, who asks, “How many lives will be harmed by the unaffordable, unachievable and unnecessary net zero fantasy before politicians wake up to scientific and economic reality?” What is your answer to Dr Underhill?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I would say three things. First, it is worth saying that—and we look at this all the time—the majority of the public, seven in 10, support action on climate and the transition to net zero. Part of the reason they support it is because they are living and breathing through it. I don’t think anyone who could have lived through the summer that we have just had, where we saw record heatwaves, we saw droughts, we saw wildfires, and we saw much worse across the world, would think that this is a fantasy. It is not a fantasy. Why it matters is that if we do not get this right, it is people’s homes, it is their livelihoods and it is their communities that will be impacted. We have both a responsibility to people in the here and now and we have a responsibility to future generations.

The second thing I would say is that I am very clear that the job—and we have to be intentional about it—for all of us is that we do this transition in a way that works for people and benefits them. Part of the way that I hope I can counter some of the frustration and scepticism that I see, and I know is growing, is to say, “Look, we will do this transition because we must and it is the right thing, but it has a net positive impact in terms of affordability, it has a positive impact in terms of the industries.”

I talked about the £100 billion of investment that we have unlocked. That is investment in places; that is jobs that we are creating; that is industries and supply chains that matters for the British economy. Success for me is if I can persuade your constituent that this is the right path, not because I say it, but because they can see the benefits of it in their lives and their communities.

Q772 Sir Christopher Chope: Don’t you recognise, Secretary of State, that climate is not something that we can control within our own domestic agenda, it is an international issue? While we are deindustrialising, our competitors are continuing to emit CO2 emissions in very large quantities—so much so that the International Energy Agency do not think we have a hope in hell of getting anywhere near the climate targets, CO2 reduction targets, even by the end of this century. Are we not imposing unnecessary burdens on our own people and industry in terms of costs, while ignoring the reality of what is happening out there and probably needing to adapt rather than to pretend that it is not happening?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: The two things I would say is that of course it requires multilateral action, but within that, every single country that is in the multilateral race has a responsibility. Often people say, “Look, we are just 1% of global emissions,” but lots of 1% add up to about 50%. Every individual country has its own responsibility.

The second thing is that I talk to my international counterparts and they are looking to the UK with envy. We are a leader in this world, but we are a leader in a massively growing economy. China is leading the race on clean power. It is a huge global economy. The thing I say to people who may be sceptical about climate change—I look at the science and the science is pretty clear—is: “Fine if you are sceptical, even if you don’t believe in it, but why would you not want to be part of that global economy that is growing?” Ultimately, I think the way that I and the Government should engage with people, and it is within their gift to be sceptical, is to say, “Look, we need to do this transition in a way that isn’t a burden, where we are unlocking billions of investment.”

You talked about deindustrialisation. My view is that our route to reindustrialisation, which the Prime Minister talks about all the time, is through our sector. There is a massive pipeline on talking to global investors, trillions that they want to invest in the net zero economy, and we can be part of that. Why on earth would we not want to be part of that?

Q773 Chair: Before I move on to Wera Hobhouse, you used the phrase “diverse supply” earlier. Can you spell out what that diverse supply of gas is, because it is different sources, isn’t it?

Miatta Fahnbulleh: I think coming from different parts of our supply chain, whether that is Norway, whether that is LNG that we are importing. There is a range and we are confident that we have the diversity of supply.

Jonathan Brearley: Particularly having worked very closely through the two energy crises, the one we are going through today and the one we went through three or four years ago, we looked very closely at how we are going to get our gas. The UK does have a set of different sources of gas. We have the North Sea, we have Norway, we have LNG terminals, we have import, we have import interconnectors, and of course we have storage. What we have been consulting on is how we maintain that security of supply.

Coming back to the Secretary of State’s answer specifically on Rough, we need to look across the board at the measures we have to make sure we have not only security of supply, but security of supply at the least cost. That is what we will be doing. Very soon NESO will set out its winter outlook, it will talk about the security of supply situation going forward, but as you can imagine, both in this role and in the previous role, gas security of supply is paramount for us because we know the serious consequences were anything to go wrong.

Chair: Thank you for that level of detail. Wera Hobhouse, please.

Q774 Wera Hobhouse: Thank you very much. Thank you for coming, Secretary of State, and for what you have already explained. Directly after this meeting I will chair a Delegated Legislation Committee on the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, with the aim to add thermal energy storage systems or heat batteries as a listed technology under section 100 of the Energy Act 2008. This will enable the Secretary of State—you—to provide support to heat batteries. Great; I am sure a lot of people will be very happy with that, but there are other technologies that are still excluded, for example electric boilers. Miatta Fahnbulleh: It is definitely outcome-focused. We are constantly keeping the technologies under review.
