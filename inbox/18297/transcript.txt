Christine Jardine: Good afternoon. I am an MP from Edinburgh. I will start by talking about the equality impacts of return-to-work mandates. Claire and Ruth, what steps do you believe employers should take to ensure that disabled workers’ needs are properly considered when changing home and hybrid working policies?

Claire McCartney: Thank you; it is a really good question. First, there is no one-size-fits-all approach when it comes to employer approaches to home and hybrid working. They will need to vary depending on the organisational sector, industry, employee needs, and how they support performance, productivity and, of course, employee participation. When thinking about changes to home and hybrid working policies, employers need to ensure that disabled workers’ needs are properly and thoroughly considered.

This includes ensuring that employers meet their legal obligations such as those under the Equality Act 2010, actively consulting disabled staff to prevent disadvantage, and ensuring that line managers have open individual conversations with disabled employees about their preferred working patterns. They should not make assumptions but recognise that, for some, remote or hybrid working can be an important reasonable adjustment to help manage conditions, energy levels, pain and commuting barriers. They should also assess on-site versus remote barriers, so reviewing the physical office environment, addressing any common challenges for disabled workers such as sensory overload, noise, lack of rest spaces, inaccessible building locations, facilities, and any commuting challenges.

They should provide accessible equipment and technology, so ensuring remote and hybrid set-ups are properly resourced including with software, ergonomic desks and chairs, and should have inclusive communication methods. Also, they should continue to review, adapt and improve approaches to make sure they remain effective and responsive to changing needs. That certainly includes conducting regular equality impact assessments and reviewing whether policy changes inadvertently disadvantage, isolate or limit disabled employees’ career progression.

Just on a broader point around flexible working: our Good Work Index data—a survey of 4,000 employees run at the CIPD—says there is an opportunity to do more broad work around flexible working, particularly in the private sector. Line manager support in the private sector should also be an area of focus. In the sample we particularly look at sectors such as retail and wholesale where there is less support; more can be done in those areas as well.

Christine Jardine: Ruth, do you want to add anything to that?

Ruth Wilkinson: From a health and safety perspective, employers obviously have a duty to ensure the health, safety and welfare of employees. With any change, you need to consider impacts: harms, hazards, risks—anything that could impact all workers. When you do your assessment, you will do an organisational-wide view. Claire has touched on some things you would look for from location. A key part of doing those assessments is to consult with workers, so you need to consult with lots of different cohorts. There are worker representatives, trade unions and employee reps. You may already have homeworkers or employees with disabilities and ongoing health problems so you can get some good feedback from a two-way conversation on what impacts and opportunities there might be. It is important to scale that down so that you do those individual risk assessments as well.

Whenever we do any risk assessment it is important to do it at the top level but also at the individual level. Again, Claire touched on this. Naturally some people will have reasonable adjustments. One of those reasonable adjustments might be working from home, flexible working, the times they work, or travel. Conversations will need to happen with that individual and we do the assessment at that level.

When we are thinking about doing the assessments, we would obviously advocate for occupational health and safety professionals to be involved, and occupational health professionals, but also managers should be trained to have these conversations. There will be confidential conversations and conversations to embark upon some of these challenging situations, so it is about making sure they are equipped to have those conversations. Essentially, it is about going through the assessment process and bringing in the expertise of others who can support the process but individualising it and looking at the factors.

Christine Jardine: Julie, how common are mandated return-to-office policies and what have been the impacts on, for example, employment relations, particularly for disabled workers?

Julie Dennis: To be honest at Acas we see very few cases related to home or office working. The last time we counted they made up around 0.1% of our cases, so we cannot really draw wider conclusions about how common these policies are in relation to our case volumes. However, we know that some difficulties arise when general attendance policies come up against the individual needs of disabled workers, a bit like what Claire and Ruth have just been saying.

Our advice line advisers tell us that when people phone our advice line around flexible working and reasonable adjustments, they sometimes come through as the same inquiry; we see confusion between the two. For example, we hear that employees who are seeking a reasonable adjustment in relation to flexible working will be steered towards making a flexible working request rather than using the duty to make reasonable adjustments. At Acas we are very clear that they are two completely separate pieces of legislation and we need to make employers and workers aware of the separate flexible working request legislation and then the duty itself.

The other thing we see at Acas is difficulties when disability related needs are simply treated as a flexible working request. Therefore, if they do not resolve that issue, you may then have increased sickness absence, or issues with the individual’s performance or their attendance. That is where concerns or conflicts within workplaces can start to happen. There is also a difference of expectations between the individual and the employer. The individual may want this type of adjustment and the employer might say, “Well, actually that’s not reasonable,” or, “We’re not able to do that.” Again, as Claire and Ruth have said, it is about having those ongoing conversations to find out what the barrier is. What is preventing this individual from performing to their best at work and what can the employer do to remove that where it is reasonably possible? That is why—reinforcing what Ruth just said—individual conciliation is really important.

In relation to return-to-office mandates, Acas does not take a position on that. It is up to the organisation to do what it wants to with its work location policies but we will flag up—as Claire has talked about—that organisations need to think about how that policy will impact individuals especially if they have a disability. It is around doing the equality impact assessment to make sure any barriers are removed or tweaked. It may be that we have this policy but we may actually do something slightly different for a member of staff because they have a disability. We have a duty to make reasonable adjustments, so flexible working may be something that we can do for that individual.

At Acas we hear that a lot of line managers are really worried about having that conversation because if they say, for example, “Right. Christine, you can have that,” Kim might come along and say, “That’s not fair. Why has Christine got it? I want this adjustment.” It is about being able to justify but not disclose the reason and be able to say there are different reasons.

Christine Jardine: Is there a role for the Government, Acas or the EHRC to manage this and come up with guidance on managing the equality impacts on employers and employee relationships to avoid that sort of situation?

Julie Dennis: There is a very clear role and practical guidance can actually help. As you may be aware Acas is currently consulting on its revised code of practice on flexible working requests. The consultation closes on 17 December. One thing we have heard very clearly from stakeholders is that employers and employees are struggling with the relationship between the statutory flexible working process and the separate duty to make reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act. We have had a very strong message from those stakeholders that we need to make sure in our guidance that we do not blur those two routes, and they have supported us having that very early prompt.

Some people have likened it to a speed hump to make sure that employers pause and consider when they receive flexible working requests, and ask whether the individual wants a flexible working request under this piece of legislation or whether they actually want flexible working as a reasonable adjustment under the duty to make reasonable adjustments. We think that Acas has a particular role to play where it can help employers and employees to understand how these different rights operate in practice and in the workplace. We will make sure that we cover that in our associated guidance, which will come along with our code. I can talk more about that later on in some of the other questions but I will hand over to John to answer the rest.

John Kirkpatrick: I do not have a huge amount to add to that, save to say that we have engaged with Acas on the preparation of the code of practice. It is simpler if there is an authoritative piece of guidance coming from one of those voices that we know others are content with. The other thing to say is that we have some guidance in the wild already. A couple of years ago we published guidance on supporting disabled workers with hybrid working partly because we were concerned about the provision of reasonable adjustments for people when working at home or working in hybrid ways, because of course the reasonable adjustments duty is in place regardless of where the employee is working.

That is also the approach we would apply to return-to-office mandates or indeed any other changes to working patterns. They do not absolve employers from the reasonable adjustments obligations. When introducing any such policies it is important that they think about the implications and make sure that whatever reasonable adjustments are required for employees in whatever work environment are in place.

Christine Jardine: If they do not get this right, could it amount to indirect discrimination?

John Kirkpatrick: Yes.

Christine Jardine: Should public authorities—the Government even—be undertaking equality impact assessments and leading by example in relation to policies in the workplace, in the civil service and so on with regard to people with disabilities?

John Kirkpatrick: Of course public authorities—over and above the remainder of the Equality Act—are bound by the Public Sector Equality Duty and it certainly seems to us that it would be pretty difficult to demonstrate how you are complying with your obligations under the Public Sector Equality Duty if you did not make some assessment of the impact of those policies on those people. Whether that means a formal equality impact assessment is a different question. We do not require that of everybody in every circumstance but—as colleagues have already said—knowing what the impact of a proposed policy is going to be, for example on disabled employees, and thinking about that and making a decision in the light of that is exactly what a public body needs to do.

Christine Jardine: What about the Government? Should the Government lead by example in relation to policies in the civil service? Is it already doing that and if not, should it be?

John Kirkpatrick: Every component part of the Government is also bound by the PSED. We are not aware that they are not following that. I would argue that it is for the Government to decide what approach they want to take to potentially go beyond their obligations under the Public Sector Equality Duty; it is for us to try to hold the Government to account if we think they are not. It is part of our normal programme to work with a variety of parts of Government—central, local, and other—to make sure we hold them to their obligations under the PSED.

Chair: I have questions about workplace health provision. Ruth, the majority of my questions are to you but if anyone else wants to add anything please do. Ruth, you are calling for a national integrated health and work service. What would this include and what would the process look like for a disabled worker?

Ruth Wilkinson: Thanks for this question. I go to lots of meetings where they talk about health pathways and the role of the workplace, and even championing the role of the workplace. From an IOSH perspective, we really champion whole-person approaches because the Ruth Wilkinson at home is no different from the one entering the workplace. We understand that individuals will have their own factors but when you get to work there are hazards and risks in the workplace that can cause harm. There are also things going on at work that can exacerbate ongoing medical conditions or impact disabilities. It is really important that we have that whole-person holistic view.

Likewise, when you look at it that way, you have a whole-system approach. The systems we have at the moment are quite fragmented. Again, I have been in other meetings where they have talked about the role of the NHS but actually the NHS is there to give treatment and a diagnosis. We are not necessarily always preventing, or promoting health—although yes, you have other factors through public health remits and so on. Having worked in health and safety for over 20 years, I have line-managed occupational health contracts and sometimes you will have people present to you and you can support them in a short-term manner, but they actually need long-term support. Although they come to you as an employer, they might then need to go elsewhere for ongoing support. It is quite fragmented so our suggestion is about having a co-ordinated and integrated approach so that there is a clear line or a system where people can enter and be supported to work.

Going back to the Dame Carol Black report in 2008, Dame Carol talked about having an initiative that is a business-led health and wellbeing consultancy service. Sir Charlie Mayfield through Keep Britain Working said that we have never had a workplace health system. I know Sir Charlie is looking at the Hull Resilience Hub. There has been an appetite for the last 20 years.

It is about having a system where individuals can self-refer and can get access to GPs, physio, occupational health, and occupational health and safety. It covers prevention and health promotion support for those returning to work and in rehabilitation. Part of the problem is that not everybody has occupational health. Your small and medium-sized enterprises do not have occupational health provision. They do not necessarily have occupational health and safety professionals who can guide them on that prevention. From our perspective, it is about bringing in that prevention but giving a joined-up approach.

The last thing I will mention is that last year IOSH, as part of Keep Britain Working, did some polling for a policy report called “Fixing sick Britain”. Some 73% of the workers surveyed supported universal local occupational health services—they wanted universal access—and 75% thought workers should be able to self-refer rather than go through their employer, so there is an appetite for that self-referral opportunity as well. It is a mixed bag of everything: prevention, support, return to work, and rehabilitation.

Chair: You mentioned that some employers procure provision. How would this avoid duplication?

Ruth Wilkinson: To avoid duplication, it is about mapping the system and what already exists. It is not about duplication; it is about raising the bar for those who do not have it at the moment. How can we give provision to those who do not have it or have inadequate provision? It comes back to a bit of what I said a moment ago: even though some employers offer occupational health provisions, for example, access to counselling or physio, they are generally short term. It is about asking what the minimum standard we expect to see is, and how all workers can benefit from that. It does not eradicate the need for your NHS and your GPs but it is probably giving a clear definition of what role the different services and professionals can play. If you have this integrated system, what is the role of the GP in that system? What is the role of the physios? Where can occupational safety and health come in? Where can occupational health come in? It needs clear roles, responsibilities and accountabilities.

Chair: Who would bear the cost of this? Would this require an amount of investment from Government?

Ruth Wilkinson: For us, it does not take away employer responsibilities; those who bear the responsibility should bear the cost. There is still a requirement for employers to continue to fund their legal duties. Again, from a health and safety perspective, it is about preventing harm; ensuring health, safety and welfare, and how they do that; and control strategies. There is the Equality Act for those reasonable adjustments. That is still in place but the Government could obviously help with the national infrastructure of that pathway and quality assurance, because there are no standards out there at the moment for occupational health provision where employers can procure what they know is good. That is where this could help.

I know Sir Charlie Mayfield has been looking at costs for employers, which are £60 to £180 per year per employee. Thinking of funding models, I know the city council is funding the Hull Resilience Hub for the first six months but then after six months participating employers are expected to contribute. It is about the employer costing model, but actually there might be things that Government or local authorities could support with as well.

Chair: What is your assessment of the number of disabled people the service could support to either remain in or return to work annually?

Ruth Wilkinson: I cannot put a figure on that one—apologies—because we do not yet have the evidence or the model to support what that actually looks like. From a health and safety perspective, the Health and Safety Executive gives us annual stats. It tells us that in the last year 1.9 million people suffered work-related ill health, which equates to 40.1 million working days lost and a £22.9 billion cost to the economy. There are big costs there. That is the work-related harm; it is the stuff that we can prevent and help reduce at the employer level.

We also have other data that says around 300,000 people with health conditions leave employment each year and the Government estimate that 2.8 million are economically inactive. If we can touch even pockets of those it will have an impact. To get the evidence that we need—again I mention Keep Britain Working—I know the Workplace Health Intelligence Unit is seeking metrics to look at how to support disabled workers to enter and remain in the workplace. That should become the evidence base. I also represent IOSH on the Keep Britain Working BSI drafting panel so I know that is in play.

Chair: A comprehensive and integrated health and work service has been proposed since at least 2008 in Dame Carol Black’s review. Aside from funding issues, what are the main barriers to achieving it, and is the sector ready to deliver it?

Ruth Wilkinson: We had a great report in 2008, and it is about the implementation side of mobilising some of the recommendations and calls to action. For me this comes back to the fragmented approach that we have. If you think about health and employers, it is split across your Department of Health and Social Care, your DWP, your NHS, and local authorities. Employers play a part—some have occupational health provision; some do not. There is a fragmented system; there is no single system that brings all these together. That is a key challenge, but we also have health and employment services operating in siloes. We have previous initiatives that struggled to achieve that consistent engagement. Occupational health is employer-led; not everyone has universal access to it. There have also been impacts on workforce capacity from an occupational health physician and advisory perspective.

Obviously, there is the prevention focus that we have talked about. There is a bulk of ill health that we can prevent at source and that is the bit we really champion. It needs that system approach, which is why we champion the integrated, connected approach to this. From an IOSH perspective, we are the professional body for occupational safety and health professionals. Although we are not clinical occupational health, the professionals assist employers with meeting health and safety legislation. The focus is very much on prevention and it is risk-based, with risk assessments and risk assessment evidence, to reduce harm and health hazards in the workplace. We work with occupational health, HR colleagues and others to get that holistic support for workers to be in work, stay at work, and thrive at work. The system needs to look at all those elements.

Chair: This question is first for Ruth but also for anyone else if you want to comment. What is your assessment of the progress so far of the Government’s workplace health provision pilots?

Ruth Wilkinson: It is still early days in the pilots and the evidence needs to be focused on the outcomes. From the pilots we can see that they are obviously looking at advice, case management, co-ordination, early support, and having consistent provision. Ultimately, the test will be in the evidence and the outcomes.

Just to go back to our plea on prevention, we would suggest that when the outcomes are looked at we do modelling to prevent work-related injury and ill health. Is it actually reaching SMEs? That is another test that we would like to see in there. Is it producing timely and competent support? We talk about early intervention, but are we being really clear and articulating what that is? How can employers do that? What support can you get? Does it involve workers and disabled workers to support safe, healthy and good work? That is part of the messaging from Dame Carol Black about good work. It is not just about supporting people to work; it has to be good work. IOSH champions safe, healthy, sustainable work that accommodates people’s needs. Although I cannot talk about the outcomes of it at the moment, if it has those elements in the test case for the outcomes, we are on to a holistic system that can support.

Claire McCartney: It is encouraging that there are so many employer vanguards. There are over 250 across different sizes and sectors, so that will be really helpful for learning going forward. Similarly to the points made, we will need a proper evaluation of all the activity and continued partnership in consultation working with employers to make sure that the new approaches adopted are really impactful and well supported. We are also involved in the BSI standard and one of our senior members is also chairing that standard, which is great. Just to emphasise that investing in good people management practice up front is needed in terms of prevention, and we have various pieces of research to support that. It is important to just remind people of that point.

Kim Leadbeater: I am going to ask a few questions about the Access to Work scheme. I will start with you, Ruth, and then come on to Claire. There has always been a tension between raising awareness of the Access to Work scheme and keeping it financially sustainable. How would you recommend that the Government deal with this tension? Are there any ways of achieving it within a constrained budget?

Ruth Wilkinson: In the health and safety profession, speaking as the professional, we support employers, and sometimes employees need Access to Work support, and sometimes they will need reasonable adjustment. Those adjustments can take any shape through the nature of the work, the workplace, and what we need to do.

People’s knowledge of Access to Work is limited for different reasons: they may not know it exists, it is not actively promoted, or they simply are not aware of it. We need to come back to that system approach; it is just about being clear of what all the parts of the system are. If everybody is playing the part that they can—you have the employer piece, and you are leaning on the professional expertise of health and safety professionals, occupational health, and HR colleagues—we can prevent and support, and create enabling environments.

Some who need that support can come in at that point, and then people can be siphoned off to Access to Work. It is about using the expertise that is there because some of it is already in place. We can then link to the reasonable adjustments, and it is about building the awareness and the knowledge of what people need to do under those reasonable adjustments: making sure employers are aware of what they need to do and how they can go about it and understanding the approach from Access to Work. I suppose it is also looking at how we can prioritise prevention and early intervention as part of doing that, and how we can reduce any duplication across the system that is in place.

Again, through health and safety legislation, we work getting as low as reasonably practicable—cost versus risk. Although there is an element of cost in there, it is about still doing what you actually need to do that is proportionate to support people. We understand the financial envelope that sits there, so let us make sure everything else is working and then people can get the support they need in a timely fashion through the right routes, and that they have mechanisms to support them if they are moving across different employers.

Kim Leadbetter: Claire, do you want to add anything to that? There is evidence that the scheme is covering costs that ought to be borne by the employers. Is that something you have experienced and, if that is an issue, how can it be addressed?

Claire McCartney: I would add to what Ruth said that there is substantial value and potential in Access to Work but at the moment the figures show that it is only really supporting around 1% of working disabled people according to the “Pathways to Work” Green Paper, which is very small indeed. Feedback from our members indicates that many organisations are unaware of the service so I completely support that there should be greater awareness.

There is scope for the service to more effectively support people with a disability who are already in employment to improve retention. We think there is potential to reform Access to Work to include advice for employers on making effective reasonable adjustments for disabled employees more generally.

In terms of the cost, that is the real difficulty—the real challenge. From an employer perspective, I would say that employers have had to absorb significant costs over the last two years, given the mixture of rising employment costs associated with new legislation. There are also more costs going forward, so at this point I am not sure that putting those costs on to employers would be the best approach.

Kim Leadbeater: When you said you are not sure that people know about it and it has been shown as a problem, is that about employers or employees, or both?

Claire McCartney: Both. From an employer perspective, there is low awareness, and from the employee perspective, there is much more we could do to raise awareness.

Kim Leadbeater: Do you have any ideas about what we could do to raise awareness?

Claire McCartney: We could use different employment channels and Government channels to get to employees, so putting employee information on the gov.uk hubs. There is also Acas, which already provides that information, but perhaps we could get it out more generally through different employer networks as well.

Julie Dennis: I would agree. Over the last few years, Acas has done quite a lot of work around reasonable adjustments and we know that a lot of reasonable adjustments are low cost or no cost at all. We are aware that when you go on the Government website and look at Access to Work, it takes you straight through to apply for Access to Work rather than signposting to guidance. We have excellent guidance on our website that actually gives employers examples of where they can make those low-cost or free adjustments. Hopefully, if we can signpost people that way it would take the pressure off Access to Work. Those free or very low-cost services could be picked up from those larger employers, which would free up Access to Work to support smaller businesses that actually need the money to recruit and retain disabled workers.

Richard Quigley: When you say no and low cost, what is the typical cost to a business of an adjustment?

Julie Dennis: Nobody knows the answer to that question, Richard. When we looked at this a few years ago, there was this magical figure of £75 and we went down a rabbit hole. We asked all colleagues who sit along here and someone went, “We don’t know where that comes from.” One thing Government could probably do is find out the actual cost, because if we can tell employers the cost is quite low, that might encourage them more—but at the minute it is this mythical figure.

The TUC recommends that the Access to Work scheme gives jobseeking disabled people an indication in advance of the support they would be granted to enable them to do the job, so you give them a heads up before they start. I suppose there is a cost implication to that, is there not? Do you agree that that would be beneficial for disabled jobseekers and employers, and would it work in practice? That is open to anyone. Claire, do you want to have a go?

Claire McCartney: Could you repeat the question?

Kim Leadbeater: Is there a way of having the conversation in advance with somebody who is going for a job to say, “Well, if you get this job, this is what might be available to you.” You are pre-empting what might happen by maybe having that conversation in advance.

Claire McCartney: It would be helpful. It would be good practice from the employer side of things to say, “These are the types of adaptations that we can do,” and share examples. That is good practice. We have inclusive recruitment guidance and we would have prompts within that to ask people regardless of their circumstances whether they would need any reasonable adjustments as part of the recruitment process and also further down the line. Transparency around those sorts of things would probably be very helpful.

Ruth Wilkinson: We are in the same position that Claire just mentioned. I have interviewed people and they are really open and honest and they just want to know how they can do the role and be supported in that role. To be honest, employees are more socially aware now and they look for employers that will look after their health, safety and wellbeing, and what they will get. They are asking these questions more at the interview and recruitment stages. We would support having an open conversation at that time and people would know—if they are going to be travelling for work or if they need to be doing certain things—what is there to support them, as you would know if there was health and safety needed in safety-critical roles, what things are expected, and what the employer/employee contract and transaction is. That fits part of it.

That is what I meant when I said, “Use the expertise that you have,” because actually your health and safety and occupational health professionals can all help come up with what that looks like through reasonable adjustments, before there is a need to jump across to the scheme, which is there for certain support that is needed.

Dame Nia Griffith: I want to come on to issues such as rheumatic conditions, where you can be fine one day and then have an awful flare-up where you are in terrible agony. That can often mean losing credibility: people think, “She was walking around fine and now she’s saying she can’t do this or she can’t do that.” I want to explore how we deal with those conditions, and how employers deal with them. Is there sufficient support, sufficient legislation and so forth to cover them? John, I will start with you. What do you feel? Do you think we should have to clarify to employers what is required by people who have those conditions? Again, there are conditions such as ADHD or autism that can manifest in many different ways.

John Kirkpatrick: We think the law is pretty clear. The conditions you describe can be covered by the Equality Act: they can constitute a disability if they meet the definition of disability, which is that they have a substantial long-term adverse effect on people’s ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities, even if they do not affect people’s ability to do their jobs in the same way all the time. As I look at all the various bits of guidance on the law that are already out there—including the Government guidance—they all say that. Our old guidance and our newer guidance on the definition of disability cover it, so we think the law recognises this issue.

Interestingly, the new services code has a specific example related to a fluctuating condition that relates to the conversation we were having a moment ago. The implication is that a reasonable adjustment for these conditions is focused on the individual. What is reasonable for that individual in those circumstances and for that employer will vary quite a lot, which potentially makes it difficult either to say what it will cost in any generic way or to say, “This is what will be available to you.” Part of the point of the reasonable adjustment provision is that the employer needs to agree with each individual employee what is reasonable in those circumstances to address the disadvantage. We would urge people to think about it in that way; but it is clear that such conditions can and often are covered by the provisions of the Act.

Dame Nia Griffith: You provided additional guidance in relation to the menopause; is there not a case to do the same for fluctuating conditions?

John Kirkpatrick: As of now, probably not. We deliberately issued additional guidance on the menopause because we felt that it was not well understood. It is less clear to us that the existing guidance is inadequate in relation to periodically fluctuating conditions.

Dame Nia Griffith: Is there anyone else who would like to make a comment on this particular issue? No. Thank you.

Richard Quigley: The wonderful team behind the Committee note that a growing proportion of Acas conciliation cases are around potential disability discrimination claims. Julie, what does your research suggest is driving that?

Julie Dennis: That is a good question.

Richard Quigley: I cannot pretend; I did not write it.

Julie Dennis: Sometimes we wish we knew the answer but I am going to try. As you may be aware, early conciliation cases can show us where problems are arising but they do not explain wider labour market trends or allow us to say that a particular issue is causing the increase. What our wider evidence shows is that there are recurring issues around reasonable adjustments and disability related flexibility, which include delays, unclear processes, difficulties understanding which route to use, and problems putting appropriate support in place.

We have seen those numbers increasing over the past 10 years: disability discrimination claims now make up 25% of early conciliation claims, so we have carried out two pieces of research around that. Last year we did a conflict prevalence survey, which found that 68% of disabled employees whose condition had a substantial impact on their day-to-day activities reported experiencing some form of workplace conflict, compared with 44% of working-age people without a disability. Those are stark figures.

Because of that finding we commissioned further qualitative research looking in more depth at the experiences of 30 disabled employees who had already participated in the original survey. That research told us that conflict is often less driven by the disability or the condition itself; it is more around how organisations respond to the individual’s support needs. Requests for support were particularly likely to become contentious where conditions were invisible or fluctuating—as we have touched on—or poorly understood.

Reasonable adjustments play an important role in this. The people our researchers spoke to described difficulties securing flexible working, delays or refusals around specialist equipment, and adjustments that were informal, temporary, imposed without consultation or treated as an inconvenience. These are common experiences, as we know from the people who phone our advice lines. We also found that routine workplace processes can become important points of friction; flexible working requests, sickness absence management and performance discussions can provide opportunities to identify and address support needs but if they are not handled well they can make the problem worse.

At Acas, we look at how we can resolve that. Line managers are key; conflict is less likely to escalate when managers respond promptly, engage appropriately and openly, work in a collaborative way, and have an understanding of the disability. They need good interpersonal skills to be able to have those conversations. We know that it also works to have effective practices, including explaining the support available, discussing adjustments, having clear processes on how to put them in place, and making sure that they are agreed and sustained over time. We cannot explain why we are seeing this increase in disability discrimination EC cases, but our research shows where disability related conflict can arise and what causes it to escalate. Our main message is to make sure that managers are equipped to have those everyday conversations.

As I said earlier—it also came up in the Mayfield review—managers are nervous of saying the wrong thing. Acas encourages all managers, regardless of who they are managing, to have everyday conversations with their people. We know that it is hard when they are busy but the simplest way is to sit an individual down and say, “Is there anything I can do to enable you to perform to your best? I’ve recruited you, you’re amazing and that’s why you work for us; is there something that may be making it harder? Are there any barriers I can remove?” You are not even talking about disability then and it may create an environment where that person feels safe to disclose.

The other issue we find at Acas is that people do not feel safe to disclose that they have a disability, especially if it is hidden and especially if it is fluctuating. They feel that if they disclose it, they will face a detriment. The onus is on these organisations to equip their line managers with the skills to create a psychologically safe environment where disabled workers feel that they can perform to their best and do an amazing job, as we know everyone in the workplace can.

Richard Quigley: Thank you, that was very comprehensive. John, you have said that the commission is engaging closely with the Government’s Disability Unit following concerns about the reasonable adjustment process. Can you describe what that engagement looks like?

John Kirkpatrick: We talk to colleagues in the Disability Unit of the Office for Equality and Opportunity on a periodic basis and on a range of issues. There is not a huge amount to say. They have raised with us the question of the operation of the reasonable adjustments duty, and they have probably been talking to my three colleagues to the right as well; those colleagues often have more detailed information about individual cases or illustrations of what is going on.

We know, as do others, that there are sometimes delays in the provision of reasonable adjustments. A number of issues have come up in this conversation; the only one I would add is that it can take time to do this process properly. A reasonable adjustment will not necessarily be what the employee first asked for, or what the employer first thought was the right answer. Having the discussion is part of the way of finding out whether there might be a good low-cost option: perhaps a change of working pattern rather than a physical adjustment to the work. If the adjustment relates to equipment, you have to work out what equipment, go out and buy it and so on. There are a number of understandable delays or periods of time within the process that one has to recognise.

Richard Quigley: Contrary to other studies, the DWP has suggested that the system is “largely functioning as intended”—I think that was the phrase. Did the commission have any role in this research? What is your assessment of its robustness?

John Kirkpatrick: The factual answer to your first question is no; we did not have a role in the process. We do not spend our time marking the Government’s homework. We have spent some time—as has the Committee—thinking about the various bits of research that are out there. It is probably the case that most parts of the picture are reconcilable, but it is clear that there are some circumstances where delays appear to be long and unsatisfactory; there are others where the system works as designed. The relative levels of those are, as you say, a matter for research. I would not want to opine on which research is better at this stage.

Richard Quigley: Claire and Ruth, some of our previous witnesses have called for a roll-out of workplace adjustment passports. That seems to be a very sensible idea. Do you think that a national scheme for adjustment passports is workable? What impacts do you think there would be?

Claire McCartney: We definitely recommend adjustment passports as good practice. They allow workers to record their needs and preferences in a live document that can be transferred between managers without having to constantly update people. If employees are happy to have adjustment passports, that would make sense.

In terms of the roll-out, it would be about ensuring that employers are properly educated and aware around the circumstances and how to properly support the adjustment process. Julie has already spoken about the importance of line managers: they need to be educated around this as well. But it sounds like a good approach.

I would also mention that we produce guidance on disability confidence specifically for line managers who are managing disabled employees. It would be helpful if we could do more to get that out to organisations and employers, alongside the Acas guidance on reasonable adjustment.

Ruth Wilkinson: In principle it is definitely something to support, but it comes back to what John was mentioning a moment ago—it is very individual. When you have adjustments put in place, they relate to the job you are doing, the conversations you have had with your employer, and the nature and context of the work. If you have a passport, that should help fast-track those conversations. You can take it with you and embark upon a conversation; you have an amount of information that to a degree you are willing to disclose, share and discuss. However, the conversations still have to happen with another employer. Adjustments will still need to be considered in the context of the new work, where you are working, the work activity and the circumstances. There is also a confidential piece in how that information travels.

Coming back to Julie’s point about psychological safety, employers need to be offering good work with a good culture where there is no stigma or discrimination, and where people feel that they can openly share and bring these passports with them. IOSH supports it in principle but these surrounding factors need to be considered for it to be effective and to enable the right conversations and the right adjustments to be implemented.

Richard Quigley: Would you see it as more of an enabler than an answer?

Ruth Wilkinson: Yes. A passport alone is not going to solve the issue because the exam questions are different when there is a change of employer. The job has changed, as has the place where they are working, how they are getting there and so on. It is a brilliant way to enable or kick-start a conversation, but line managers still need to be trained to have that conversation and the adjustments need to be the right adjustments.

Chair: John, the TUC and others have argued that the commission should be taking a more proactive enforcement approach in relation to the workplace reasonable adjustments duty. What is your view?

John Kirkpatrick: We are aware of the view that the TUC and others take. Some of what I am about to say will be familiar to the Committee from our previous interactions but I am going to say it anyway. We have a broad remit but our budget has not moved in nominal terms in a decade. We can fulfil our statutory obligations, which include enforcement measures and other things across our remit, but our discretionary funds for specific enforcement activity are constrained now and have been throughout my three-year term at the commission.

In dealing with that challenge, we try to be as strategic as we can in the way we engage in enforcement and litigation. For instance, we intervene in cases where we perceive system-wide problems that can be addressed through setting a precedent, generally through the higher courts rather than tribunals. An example is our work on sexual harassment in the uniformed services. We broadly try to operate strategically at a system level. We are not funded or equipped to support individual cases and therefore we do not, on the whole.

However, in the past we have run legal support funds where we have identified a broad-ranging systemic issue and sought to support a portfolio of cases. The most recent related to race discrimination in the workplace; the one before related to disabled people’s access to public transport. With themes of that kind, and in those circumstances, there are occasionally opportunities where sums of money that are relatively modest in the great scheme of things can make a difference to enforcement and legal precedent. If we had more money to play with we probably could and indeed might seek to do more along those lines.

We have been very grateful for the fact that the Committee has recognised our pleas in the past and added its voice to ours in trying to persuade Government that with a little more cash there is quite a lot more we could do. This is a good example of the number of ways in which we might make a difference in this area if we were able.

We are in the middle of a three-year strategic plan at the moment and will shortly start thinking about our next strategic plan. The issue of reasonable adjustments in the workplace was a close contender when we were putting this strategic plan together. We have not chosen to focus on it in the current three years but it is certainly conceivable that we might going forward. We will be looking at the evidence that this Committee’s investigation unearths and stimulates very carefully as we think about preparing that new plan.

Chair: I am glad to hear that you will be looking carefully at our recommendations and report, but what other data and evidence do you think you would need to make this a priority in your next strategic plan?

John Kirkpatrick: To go back to what I call the strategic approach—it may sound like a cliché—it is always important to have evidence of the breadth and scale of the problem. It is evident to us that a significant proportion of the incoming intelligence we get about Equality Act issues relates to disability.

In our current strategic plan, we are focusing on the rights and entitlements of disabled people in slightly different circumstances. As the Committee is aware, we are doing more work on disabled people’s access to public transport, and we are trying to make sure that the Department for Work and Pensions takes proper account of learning disabilities in the way it assesses people for benefit entitlements. We have also worked with festival operators to try to make festivals accessible to disabled people. These are not workplace issues, but we are tackling them.

The reason we have gone after those things is that we have seen broad-ranging and substantial system-wide patterns; in the case of festival operators there is a particular operator or a particular employer. That is the way we tend to operate in our enforcement context. If we see strong evidence that, for example, a particular substantial employer is consistently falling short of what we expect in this area, that might be territory that we would be interested in, as we have been in other contexts.

Chair: I understand what you have said about the resource challenge: that is clear. I also understand you to be saying that you can choose your strategic priorities. How feasible is it to have more robust enforcement within your existing envelope and what impact might you foresee that having on disabled workers?

John Kirkpatrick: I can only repeat that it is not very feasible within our existing resource envelope because there is not a huge amount we can do.

Chair: You could put it in your next strategic plan.

John Kirkpatrick: If we put it in our strategic plan, the next question is, “Well, what might we do?”—that might be a better way to answer it. There might be a variety of things we could do. Our first action whenever we have to take on new obligations, responsibilities, changes to the law and so on, is to make sure that the guidance is right and that awareness of employers’ obligations is where it should be. For example, we followed the recent passage of the worker protection Act with work on what it meant; you will have seen that in the press recently encouraging employers to think about their forward-looking anticipatory duties in that area.

In such instances, we start with making sure the guidance is there, but there is also a fair amount of work that we and indeed colleagues along the panel here can do to enhance public understanding—that is real work. We can be out there promoting and promulgating the guidance and telling the story about what the law requires, and alongside that we can think about legal support. As I say, we can also intervene and we can take cases on our own. We can conduct inquiries, usually into a specific, narrow area in order to keep it manageable. We can conduct research in those areas and make an evaluation of what makes a difference. As you have heard today, and will no doubt have heard in the inquiry so far, the evaluation base is not always great. There are a variety of things that we would certainly think about as our response if we were able to do so.

Claire McCartney: From our perspective, we would like to see more support provided to employers around complying with their legal obligations and improving HR and people management capability. We know that most SMEs fall foul of employment regulations, not because they are necessarily bad employers but because they are time-poor, resource-poor and knowledge-poor. We would like to see more support in the system, which is why we have previously called for a doubling of the Acas budget around this. We need more support provision—particularly for SMEs—rather than naming and shaming. We want support so that they are able to do the best they can in supporting people, particularly disabled people.

Julie Dennis: As I said in answer to Richard’s question, we and especially my team have shifted our focus around disability over the last two to three years because we know that—particularly with small businesses—there is that knowledge gap. We have been doing some amazing work jointly with colleagues at the DWP, where we did a campaign around reasonable adjustments. We have also been doing further work focusing on neuro-inclusion.

We know it is about getting that guidance. We have a broad range of guidance on the Acas website; we just need to get people to come and see it. How can we drive that traffic to us? We know that if managers have skills that can reduce conflict, they can deal with issues more informally and—fingers crossed—that will reduce the number of early conciliation claims coming through to Acas and employment tribunal claims going through to the tribunal service, which is also facing a massive increase. Prevention is key: that is why our Acas strategy, which is coming up to its second year now, has shifted in emphasis to that prevention and education space.

Chair: Can I ask you to describe how Acas intends to address the interaction between the new flexible working provisions and the reasonable adjustments duty in its forthcoming statutory code of practice? If you can be relatively brief in your answers that would be appreciated; votes are expected soon and I have a couple more questions to get through.

Julie Dennis: Equality considerations have been built into the redrafting of our revised code of practice, which is looking at flexible working from the outset. They have also informed both the structure and the content of our new code. As I said earlier, the key thing we have tried to do is to prevent those two legal frameworks becoming confused. As we know, statutory flexible working requests and reasonable adjustments are separate rights with different legal tests. Our amazing Acas team that is writing the code is going to address that in three main ways.

First, we are going to keep the legal distinction very clear; the new reasonableness test for refusing a flexible working request does not reduce or replace the employer’s separate duty to make reasonable adjustments. A flexible working request might be refused under flexible working, but that does not mean that the employer does not now need to consider that request under a reasonable adjustment if that individual meets the threshold for disability.

Secondly, we are going to help employers recognise where those two rights might interact in practice. People may not necessarily use the legal terminology that we all talk about here, but that does not reduce or replace the requirement for the employer. The code is trying to encourage employers to remain alert to the need to make a reasonable adjustment in requests for flexible working, rather than a flexible working request; when they are going through that process they need to keep that in the back of their mind and to think about it through the consultation process around flexible working. Where relevant, employers should consider how those two issues will be handled, whether the flexible working process itself needs to be adjusted or whether reasonable adjustments need to be considered separately. They should also revisit the position if new information comes along during that conversation.

Thirdly, we have built wider equality safeguards into the code, which have come from stakeholder feedback. They include making sure it is clear that there needs to be a meaningful consultation and individual and evidence-based decision-making, and that they need to consider adaptations and alternatives, accessible processes, appropriate privacy, and accompaniment when employees are coming for those conversations. Whether the decision is yes or no, a clear reason needs to be given. Those safeguards are relevant across all protective characteristics, not just disability. One important thing I have talked about already is the issue around disclosure; we are aware that people may not disclose.
