Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Cat McLaughlin and Dr Gwen Rees.

Chair: Good morning, everyone, and welcome to this meeting of the Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee. We return this morning to our inquiry into the future of farming, and we will be looking in particular at the animal health and, indeed, commercial implications of the 2026 bluetongue outbreak. Welcome, Cat and Gwen, to the Committee. For the benefit of those who are following our proceedings and for our own official records, can I ask you to introduce yourselves, please?

Cat McLaughlin: Thank you very much for the welcome and the invitation. My name is Cat McLaughlin. I am the chief policy adviser for animal health and welfare at the National Farmers Union.

Dr Rees: I am Dr Gwen Rees and I am president of the British Veterinary Association.

Chair: Good morning, both. The bulk of our questioning this morning will be on the handling of the bluetongue outbreak, but, inevitably at the moment, there is always something else coming over the horizon. You will have seen the reports of the outbreak in Wales, and over the border into England, of Shamonda virus. Do you have any thoughts to offer us on how that should be handled and whether there are lessons to be learned from our experiences earlier in the year?

Dr Rees: The first thing to say is that it is obviously a worrying time to be a farm vet or a farmer. On the back of this year’s bluetongue outbreak, to have Shamonda now confirmed is an additional worry for everyone. There is still a lot of uncertainty—it is very new and was reported yesterday—but there is a feeling among vets that it was only a matter of time before we would be picking up Shamonda in the UK. We knew that it was in the Channel Islands and in Ireland. The fact that these cases have been picked up is perversely reassuring, in that the surveillance is doing what it should.

Cat McLaughlin: It is a concern for livestock keepers and farmers. In the NFU we have been trying to raise awareness about it for the last couple of months. I still think it will probably come as a nasty shock and surprise to an awful lot of farmers. I am sure some of them are not aware of it. The very fact that there is no easy treatment and no easy control mechanism will probably concern them as well, because it is just something else that they are going to have to try to deal with.

Chair: It does feel like there is just wave after wave. We have had avian influenza, we have had bluetongue, we now have Shamonda, and on the continent we still have, I think, a foot and mouth outbreak in parts of eastern Europe. We have African swine fever, which we thought was being held back by a fence down Germany but now seems to have reappeared in Spain. It does, I guess, emphasise the importance of vigilance and traceability in terms of livestock movements and the rest of it.

Cat McLaughlin: One of the things that we need to think about as well is the country’s capability to deal with a situation where we have several big exotic diseases all converging at once. As the NFU, we are very concerned that our capability just is not there to deal with it on several fronts.

Chair: We all talk about food security, but, to pick up the news this morning about the hacking of freezers, we realise that there is now a cyber-security aspect to food security as well. That is why we are here.

Let me come to the impact of the bluetongue outbreak. Cat, let me start with you and ask the most general, broadest question. Tell us what the impact has been to the farmers financially and emotionally, to the animals in terms of animal welfare, and to the sector as a whole. Then, Gwen, we will maybe get your perspective from the profession’s point of view.

Cat McLaughlin: The impact is much wider than just confirmed premises. Farmers have faced animal deaths, severe disease, reproductive losses, milk and growth losses, extra labour, veterinary costs, and disruption to breeding and sales. We also, obviously, have some losses that will probably only be known at the next lambing and calving times. We have also had lots of farmers under severe emotional pressure and stress. I do not think that is something that we can ever forget. We have done various meetings around the countries, especially the hardest-hit areas, and the emotional turmoil that this has caused on farms has been really stark and quite distressing to see and hear.

In terms of the financial cost, the NFU does not have any big, average figures that we can give you. We have about half a dozen case studies, and I am happy to send any costs and information from those case studies to the Committee afterwards. But some of the financial losses have been substantial.

Chair: But as you say, if the impact is on fertility, we will not really know until next year’s lambing and calving the number of lambs and calves that are born compared with previous years and, indeed, whether they are going to thrive and grow.

Cat McLaughlin: Absolutely true.

Chair: Gwen, I should, at the top of the meeting, have reminded the world that I am married to a vet and my son is a vet. That is duly recorded. From the point of view of the veterinary profession, tell us the impacts of what you have been through this year.

Dr Rees: When it comes to the animal health and welfare question, which as vets is one of the key things that we are thinking about, this year the clinical signs and mortality have appeared to be markedly more severe than in previous seasons. We have been seeing far sicker animals, and we have been seeing higher levels of death, particularly in sheep in the south-west, where the outbreak was at its worst. The situation escalated very rapidly in that area, which put a huge amount of pressure on both the private vets and the Government/APHA vets in that region to respond to what was a very rapidly evolving situation. It was very difficult. Vets were faced with a huge number of sick and dying animals, as were the farmers and keepers, and there was very little that vets could do in the face of that outbreak beyond supportive treatment. There is no direct treatment for this. You can support those animals and hope that they get through it, but that is a very difficult position to be in for a vet—and for a farmer as well.

It was a bit of a major animal welfare crisis at the time—and that was the acute phase, during the outbreak. As Cat has said, what we are now seeing as a big concern is what the impact and the implications will be next spring, in particular for fertility. It is known that it has affected fertility of rams significantly during breeding season, so we may well be looking at a lot of farms with very low numbers of lambs in the spring, which has a massive financial impact on those businesses. On the calving and lambing season more broadly, we are not quite sure what to expect, but it could be significant for a big number of farms.

Chair: It is a matter of public record, I think, that you have seen big changes in the veterinary profession in the last decade or two around the availability of large-animal vets and mixed practices. To what extent do you think that changed profession on the ground is able to meet an acute episode like this where you have highly localised high incidence?

Dr Rees: It is an important question. With regard to bluetongue and this specific outbreak, I do not think there was a shortage—that is my understanding—of private vets. They were working extremely hard under very difficult circumstances, but I do not think the changed demographic of the profession had a direct impact. When we talk about the broader disease preparedness picture in the UK, there are fewer farm vets now than there were, for example, in 2001 during the foot and mouth outbreak, and vets will have less experience, perhaps, of having worked in mixed or farm practice, so if we needed to draw on vets from across the profession, they may be less prepared than they had been. It is something that the BVA have been doing a lot of work on.

Chair: If you go back to foot and mouth, which I am guessing was well before your time but unfortunately not before mine, there was a whole cohort of people who were able to come in from other parts of the profession, and maybe from retirement, part-time working and whatever else. Are those people still there if we need them?

Dr Rees: It is a question we are asking this year as the BVA. One of our policy priorities this year is around disease resilience and preparedness, and what that looks like in the private veterinary sector outside of the initial Government response. I think there is a piece of work to do to ensure that vets understand what would be required of them and feel confident and capable to do it.

Chair: And 60% of your profession is now working in practices that are owned by corporates. Do you think the owners of those practices would have the same attitude towards that of public service?

Dr Rees: I would hope so. You would have to ask them. I would not be able to answer for them, but I would hope so.

Chair: Cat, in terms of farmers working under pressured margins, you are probably seeing fewer vets on farms around the routine work than previously. Us parliamentarians have to look at the balance between what a private business, as a farm is, should be carrying and where there is a public interest in stock disposal, for example. If the Government says, “You must dispose of your animal in a certain way,” then it is reasonable to say that there should be some assistance given for that. What would your members be looking for by way of direct help from Government in the management of an outbreak like this? There is different practice across different parts of the United Kingdom. There is help for farmers in Scotland and Wales, but not in England.

Cat McLaughlin: Yes. I am just wondering where to start answering that, because it was quite a broad question. In a disease outbreak, as you say, it comes down to understanding the risk, understanding what we are dealing with and then looking at the balance of public and private roles. I think for farmers, we would expect, and I think it is fair of us to expect, that the policies are right, surveillance systems are right and the services that we need to work with those policies are all there. Certainly, I would not expect farmers to end up having to pay or contribute to anything that is a public-good aspect of a disease outbreak.

Farmers themselves obviously have to take responsibility for the health and welfare of their animals, and that does mean looking after them, keeping track of their health and welfare, helping with the surveillance aspect of picking up diseases, obviously vaccination and some of those preventive health issues and farm biosecurity. That all comes under what a farmer can do to manage and maintain a good attitude and response to a disease risk.

When it comes to private vets, we have seen that there are some areas of the country where farm vets are short on the ground. The one thing that we have got going in our favour is that a lot of farmers now have a very preventive response with their private vet, so they have their private vet going on farm on a regular basis, doing lots of preventive work and a lot less of the firefighting work. That will probably help, because we can try to encourage farmers and vets to have that really strong working relationship when there is not a disease crisis. We can probably help with that type of thing.

Chair: That would be people who are in high-health schemes, farmer education schemes and the rest of it, and for that there is a commercial premium.

Cat McLaughlin: Yes.

Chair: This is not the first bluetongue outbreak that we have had. Will you compare and contrast what we have had this year with previous years and previous spikes?

Dr Rees: This is, I think, the third year of BTV-3. In the first year it was largely contained, in the second year it was spreading, and I think there was an understanding that this year would be worse. However, it has been behaving in a slightly unpredictable way across Europe and it has done here, so the severity has, I think, taken some people by surprise. But it is not a surprise that it is worse this year, and the geographical spread is not a surprise.

There is a feeling among vets that some of what we have seen this year has resulted from the snapping out of the RZ last year. Those animals in the south-west and in Wales were vulnerable from that moment to spread of the disease. They were vulnerable anyway, but it will probably have accelerated the disease’s being at the door. So there is an extent to which it was predictable and predicted—that is why there were big pushes for vaccination last year—but there is also an extent to which the speed and severity have taken people by surprise.

Cat McLaughlin: I was working for the NFU back in 2006 to 2008, when we had bluetongue-8. The big difference that we have got to compare is the way that the policy was enacted. Bluetongue-8 was a lot more highly regulated. As Gwen said, we snapped out of out of zones quite quickly with bluetongue-3 this time, whereas bluetongue-8 was a lot more controlled, the vaccination programme was a lot more controlled—it was done with a regional roll-out. There were complete differences in how we responded to bluetongue-8, but I think for the farmers on the ground, the shock, the severity and the health and welfare aspects were fairly similar.

Chair: So as you see it, it was predictable and it was predicted, but somehow or another we still seemed to be caught by surprise. That is no criticism; it just feels like that is how it was, but it does lead on to questions around communication and messaging, and Sarah is going to lead that questioning.

Sarah Bool: As you said, the severity increased, but the risk was foreseeable, although probably unpredictable. Cat, you were just talking about the need to do more prevention rather than firefighting, and obviously vaccines are a key part of that. Despite the fact that you had the #BattleBluetongue campaign and everything last year, why did not it translate into earlier vaccinations?

Dr Rees: It is in a way, for many, the million-dollar question. There were some valiant efforts to communicate, particularly on vaccine uptake, prior to this season. There were some really good resources put together, and that messaging was there, but it did not penetrate, I suppose. It is partly the nature of communicating risk and discussing risk in uncertainty. Farmers and vets are always balancing risk and benefit, and it is very difficult sometimes to invest in a just-in-case when your margins are extremely tight. The vaccine is not cheap, and the decision to vaccinate just in case was an expensive one for farmers. Therefore, it was a hard sell. It is a difficult thing to say, “We don’t know that this will definitely be a problem, but if you want to protect yourself, you should vaccinate.” It was the same on the continent: vaccination rates increase massively after there has been a big, bad outbreak.

It is a very complex picture around communication. Vets did, I think, try very hard and find it frustrating, but also very much understood that the decision to vaccinate or not is one of many decisions that farmers are making every day, and they have to balance risk and uncertainty. In this case, I think there are a lot of farmers who wish they had vaccinated sooner.

Cat McLaughlin: I do not disagree with any of that. Uncertain local risk, cost, fertility concerns, timing and confidence were all things that farmers were having to try to manage and balance themselves. We are in interesting times with communications. A couple of years ago we were not having to battle some of the social media claims. There was some misinformation flying around as well, which all undermines confidence. But what we have done really well this year, I think, is the bluetongue hub, with the Ruminant Health & Welfare group. That was a collaboration of all of industry—AHDB, the vets, farming groups; everybody—all coalescing the messages so that there was one single place for information. To give Government lots of credit, they were firing their comms into that as well. It was a really good place. But still, a lot of farmers were having to think, “Can I afford this vaccine? Can I get hold of the vaccine?” We had all the supply issues as well. As Gwen says, it was really complicated.

Sarah Bool: I want to explore that. As you said, there was a lot of misinformation about the infertility and the productivity impacts. Do you think we have now tackled that in the messaging? Do you think we have dispelled that myth, or is that a message that anyone listening right now needs to hear very clearly: “Don’t be frightened of it”?

Cat McLaughlin: I do not think you can put out the one truth enough, to be honest. I do not think we can say that misinformation was the sole cause or the sole problem, but as trusted organisations and trusted individuals—let’s face it, farm vets are recognised as trusted individuals for farmers—we need to have those grown-up conversations about what exactly vaccines do. They will not stop infection. They can prevent some of the problems and they can make it a less bad situation. The more that we can get that message out—there will always be misinformation; we will never be able to get rid of it totally—the better.

Dr Rees: I agree with all of that. We are in a different world now when it comes to vaccine scepticism, and that has perhaps changed people’s understanding of vaccines and what they do. We were dealing with a new vaccine, as well, with very little information, particularly last year, on efficacy and safety. We knew it was safe; it was licensed—well, it was allowed to be used in the UK. We have had an increasing picture of how effective the vaccine is, so we are fairly confident now, but when vets were first having these conversations with farmers, there was a lot more uncertainty about how valuable these vaccines would be, and perhaps that initial narrative is difficult to change. That will always be the case with new medications or vaccinations.

Again, it was a very complex picture. We are very confident now that vaccinating is absolutely the best thing that can be done, but sometimes you are trying to close the stable door after the horse has bolted. Trying to reverse a narrative that has already taken hold can be very difficult, and it can make that conversation between vets and farmers quite difficult.

Sarah Bool: When you were talking about bluetongue-8, you said there was a regional roll-out. How different was that from the way we have approached BTV-3? It seems fairly different.

Cat McLaughlin: The vaccination process was a lot more regulated and controlled. It started, the vaccine—it was a new vaccine as well—was released on a regional basis, and basically we rolled vaccine up the country. I am trying to remember; I basically followed it, doing lots of meetings around the country. We started off in the south-east and the east coast, and we gradually moved vaccine as we hit various trigger points. This was all done very much with the Government saying when the trigger points had been hit and then releasing licences for vaccine to be used by farmers in the new areas. Over the course of probably nine months to a year, the vaccine was gradually rolled out, and ultimately we got ahead of the disease with the vaccine campaign. So yes, it worked—it did what it needed to do—but it was also a very costly process for the Government and DEFRA.

Sarah Bool: Sometimes it is a false economy with these things, if you do not do them right. Getting ahead of it would have been ideal. Do you know why they didn’t do it this time—or do you have suspicions?

Cat McLaughlin: We probably cannot answer for the policy, but it was probably the cost issue.

Dr Rees: There has been a slight legacy from what happened with the vaccination for BTV-8 that has impacted people’s decision whether to vaccinate this time. It was almost a victim of its own success. There was an element of pushing to vaccinate and people spending money to vaccinate, and then it was not a problem, partly because—

Sarah Bool: Because they were vaccinated.

Dr Rees: Partly, perhaps, yes. That is something that our members have been saying: when they are having these conversations and trying to talk about vaccinating, there is a feeling that, “Well, we spent a lot of money vaccinating last time and didn’t need to, perhaps.” That has been a tricky one to navigate. It ties in with those conversations about risk and uncertainty; they are really hard conversations to have.

Sarah Bool: I guess that overall, the cost of not doing it is potentially far higher than the cost of doing it, so it is probably better to do it. We can say, “Look, the vaccine is new, but it is trusted.” You flagged the bluetongue hub. If we want to send a message to any of our farmers just to make sure that they are looking there for all the information, I assume there is a website. Are there leaflets as well?

Cat McLaughlin: It is the Ruminant Health & Welfare group website, and then all the industry groups and veterinary organisations amplify the same messages on our own websites and through our own newsletters and articles and so on.

Sarah Bool: We as a Committee want to try to help wherever we can to spread the correct information.

Chair: We are now going to look at test results and how they were communicated, and other aspects of communication.

Henry Tufnell: In peak outbreak in August, I think there were delays of about three weeks with testing. Could you set out what impact that had on disease management and on the response overall?

Dr Rees: There was a time lag between cases being reported and the tests being submitted, so between suspicious cases and those cases being finally confirmed. What we had in August that changed in September was that in September APHA, or the Government, started reporting suspicious cases, and therefore we had a much better picture of what was happening locally at that time. Before then, the picture of confirmed cases was two to three weeks old by the time those cases were added to the map, which meant that communicating the risk in those areas, in an evolving situation that was changing almost daily, was very difficult. You are trying to communicate that the risk is very high and yet the map is not reflecting it because it is reflecting two to three weeks ago. It did take time, I think, to fully appreciate the scale and severity of what was happening in the south-west at the time because of that lag in confirming those submissions.

Henry Tufnell: So the changes that happened in terms of private vets being able to diagnose after examining affected animals—is that what you are talking about in terms of the change from August to September?

Dr Rees: No. Sorry, I should have been clearer. What happened is that the heat map that was published was updated to include suspected cases that had been submitted, and that was a huge step forward. I have vets still coming up and saying that that changed everything when it came to having those conversations with farmers and trying to show, “Look, this is what is happening.” It was much more real-time and much better at communicating what was happening now. Having that heat map has been a huge step forward for everyone.

Cat McLaughlin: As Gwen said, that heat map was really important. It comes back to how farmers are balancing and managing risk. Having those suspected cases mapped showed them the neighbourhood risk and put it into a different kind of quantity for them. But yes, on the delays that we were seeing in the peak, I had some farmers who were reporting that they were waiting a lot longer than three weeks for their test results to come in, certainly down in the south-west. That is a horrendous position for a farmer to be to be placed in. I get that there were reasons for it—again, it comes back to the ability to have this kind of surge capacity when needed—but if you as a farmer know that you have a problem, you have reported that problem and you are now left having to deal with the aftermath and still have no clear confirmation of what you are dealing with, it feels as if it becomes an even worse problem and, as I say, it all adds to that emotional stress.

I would like to think that we can review and take some lessons and think about exactly what happened and why there was that delay, and at what point in the process those delays started to be kicked into the system. It would be really good if we could come up with a different way of doing things that made it a more streamlined situation, and look at some of the barriers that built those delays into that reporting process, because it was not helpful at all.

Henry Tufnell: How do you think those delays could be impacting on other diseases at play and the impact of those circulating?

Cat McLaughlin: From the NFU’s point of view, we are worried, because bluetongue could look like lots of other exotic diseases. You could end up with reporting fatigue kicking in, and farmers assuming that it is bluetongue, even though everything is notifiable—and we have been telling farmers and reminding them of their of their responsibilities. I just hope and pray that we are not missing things. Winter surveillance is coming up and I am crossing my fingers that that winter surveillance, which is done a lot less passively, shall we say, does not identify some other things that have slipped under the net.

Dr Rees: I agree. Vets have foot and mouth, in particular, at the front of their mind as a concern whenever they are looking at bluetongue. Even in those regions where vets were being called out and spending their entire day seeing sick animals with bluetongue, you have to remain vigilant for foot and mouth. It is a very difficult thing to differentiate between the two, and that is a very big responsibility being placed on that vet to report correctly, but also to identify any suspicion that it could be foot and mouth and not bluetongue in that scenario.

Henry Tufnell: If I can come back to my prompt in respect of the changes to testing policy, you have been quite vocal on that. What are the trade-offs in terms of whether that should remain in place? How do you view that change in policy?

Dr Rees: The change in reporting—

Henry Tufnell: In terms of private vets being able to do it—

Dr Rees: Yes—the move away from needing a confirmed diagnostic test in order to confirm disease and confirm on reporting. We understand why that decision was made. Given the extent to which bluetongue is now here, pragmatic decisions have to be made on reporting. It is not that the BVA necessarily disagree with that decision, but the decision was communicated at very short notice and without much consultation with the stakeholders. We were made aware maybe 36 hours before the changes happened that they would happen, and it was very difficult then to communicate that to vets who were suddenly very worried that the ball would be in their court around deciding whether this was foot and mouth or not. The communication was patchy and difficult in that time when the transition happened. We do not yet have good information on the impact that that change in reporting has had on the levels of reporting of disease. It was not necessarily the wrong decision, but perhaps it could have been communicated and consulted on better with the vets and the farmers.

Henry Tufnell: How would you view it for future outbreaks? Is that something that you would—

Dr Rees: It would be interesting to see the impact that moving to online reporting has had on the number of cases being reported before coming to a firm conclusion. If it is a barrier to reporting, then that is something to take very seriously. If it helps with reporting, then again, that is a good thing. We would want to see some information about the impact before coming to a conclusion on that.

Cat McLaughlin: It comes back to that concept that we need to have confidence that, in in a disease outbreak, we have sufficient capacity in the reporting process, the Government vet networks and the labs to deal with we need to do. I hope that we do not end up doing this type of big policy change in the middle of an outbreak just to deal with lack of infrastructure. I would like to think that it had been done with a lot of thought, a lot of understanding, a lot of training and a lot of support for private vets and farmers so that they felt confident in what they were doing and it was not just a reaction: “We have no more—we can’t do this the way—we’re going to have to suddenly change overnight.”

Henry Tufnell: Thank you. I am a Welsh MP—I represent Pembrokeshire. The Chair has alluded to the different approaches in Wales, Scotland and England. Farmers in England have to pay for vet fees and investigations, which is not the same as in Wales and Scotland. I would be grateful if you would comment on how the different approaches influence testing behaviour, whether they leave farmers disadvantaged, and the overall impact on disease control.

Cat McLaughlin: Ultimately, animal health and welfare is a devolved policy area, as we all know, but exotic diseases do not respect those boundaries and borders. I always think that while I understand that devolved areas and devolved Administrations—Scotland, England, Wales and Northern Ireland—have different policies, I do not think we are always very good at sharing some of the reasoning behind those different policies, or thinking about some of the farmers who farm either side of a border. That is where you start to get some difficulties coming into the system.

In terms of equity when it comes to paying for official tests, I would like to see a bit more of an equitable position across country borders. I hope I have not just shot the Welsh farmers in the foot because they would all end up having to pay for it as well, but we do need more of an equitable system. I would have liked to see English farmers in the same position as Welsh and Scottish farmers and having those tests supported.

Henry Tufnell: They all test in the same place, right?

Cat McLaughlin: No. Well, we now have the new lab capacity. We still have Pirbright, which is obviously the primary one, but they have opened up Moredun and—

Dr Rees: Weybridge.

Cat McLaughlin: They have Weybridge, but they were also allowing—I think it was Moredun in Scotland and one of the Welsh universities were going to start. You would need to check with DEFRA and APHA, but I think they were trying to add a bit more testing capacity to the system.

Dr Rees: When it comes to exotic disease control in the devolved nations, it is, as Cat says, a really difficult area because policy is devolved but the disease does not necessarily know that. It can create mixed messaging and it can create a feeling—perhaps in Wales farmers feel very glad and grateful that they are still able to test and get confirmation of disease. I mentioned earlier that there are implications of decisions that are made across borders on countries the other side of the border. With the snapping out of the RZ last year, that essentially meant that Wales had to become part of the RZ as well, and we may be looking at a very different picture of disease in Wales because of that decision that was not made in Wales. There is a feeling from vets in Wales and from our members that they are in control of some policy but not all policy, and it is a very difficult thing to navigate. Again, it comes down to that feeling of equitable policy decision making, which does not always translate into what happens on the ground.

Chair: Let us flip the telescope around. We are sitting here in Westminster with 20:20 hindsight, looking at what happened. Let us look at it from the on-farm perspective. You had a situation where, as you say, it was predicted but somehow or another we did not make the emotional connection about how bad it was going to be. It happens. It hit, and possibly there was an element of guilt in there about not knowing what was happening. You had a delay, where people were left still not knowing and not having that final element of certainty. Into that mix, you throw in a regulatory change, which surely has an impact on the relationship between the vet and the farmer. With hindsight, you are brewing the perfect storm here, aren’t you? I do not know that there is necessarily a question in that, but it does feel that we are seeing this as some sort of exercise in Government comms in the same way that you would say, “You should wear your seatbelts,” but it is an awful lot more involved than that. Charlie is going to look at the question of vaccine availability.

Charlie Dewhirst: We have covered some of the challenges we faced last year with later uptake. It seems that there was a point when we did have sufficient availability of vaccine, Europe was then hit with a spike, which led to high demand over there, and vaccine was then distributed in Europe. There is a finite amount of vaccine produced each year. What is your reflection on that? What do you think we can do in 2027 to try to ensure that we do not end up in the same situation where Europe is hit first and all the vaccine gets used there?

Dr Rees: It is a question that a lot of us have been asking ourselves. The trouble with vaccines is that it is such a complex supply chain and it is a very difficult thing to stockpile. They have a short shelf life, and it is very difficult to massively increase supply at short notice, which is what we saw this year. By the time the extent of the outbreak was realised and farmers and vets were clamouring to get the vaccine, there were delays in being able to get it. Having spoken to our vets and to the vaccine manufacturers and suppliers, a lot of what needs to happen is better predictability of the demand for vaccine. That is why it is going to be so important to push vaccination going forward—to get vet practices and farmers to get their orders in so that the manufacturers have the confidence that they will be sold if they come to the country and those animals have protection ahead of next season. Those would be my core messages on what we can do.

Professor Horton: I concur with what Richard said about our preparedness. It is surprisingly difficult, as you are probably aware, to prove the links between weather and climate and emerging diseases. It is suspected, but there are always confounding factors. The exception, of course, is vector-borne diseases, where viruses have to, as part of their lifecycle, get into an insect. It is not only the fact that climate change or climate effects can allow mosquitos and other insects to get to new areas that they were not in before, as you alluded to, but we have very good evidence from laboratory studies on the impact of temperature changes. Even for bluetongue, we had midges already that could transmit it, but a small change in temperature can have a massive impact on how quickly the virus can replicate in the midges. Twenty days would have been average for bluetongue, and it has shortened to three days.

We are alert for new diseases, such as West Nile virus, which is a threat—we have laboratory contingency plans that are maintained and tested for diseases like that—but also for the increased impact of diseases that we know about already. Bluetongue this summer is a good example of that: the weather conditions conspired, with mild overwintering, which climate can have an effect on, and then extreme heat. Yes, we are alert—that is the short answer.
