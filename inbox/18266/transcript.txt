Cath Sezen: Good morning, everyone. I am the director of education policy at the Association of Colleges.

Ben Rowland: I am Ben Rowland, the chief executive of the Association of Employment and Learning Providers. Chair: I should put it on the record that Ben and I have known each other for many years as friends.

Chris Loveday: Hi, I am Chris Loveday, the chief operating officer at City of Portsmouth College. I was formerly at Barton Peveril College.

Chair: I will begin our questioning this morning. Will each of our witnesses please describe the overall impact that artificial intelligence and educational technologies are having on the further education sector, and the role that you see for FE in preparing students for a future in which AI and technology are a major feature of life?

Cath Sezen: I suppose it is still relatively early days, particularly for AI. In total, we have just under 40,000 enrolments in digital qualifications, across 16 to 18-year-olds and adults. About 20,000 of those are at level 3 for 16 to 18-year-olds, to give you an idea of where the bulk of enrolments lie.

Colleges are very keen to be at the cutting edge. We have a lot of sector expertise in college. Staff are utilising AI to support in preparing lessons and making lessons more engaging, and to support students with SEND. I think that that is very important. AI is also having a positive impact on workload. There are questions about how we best support all students towards the future in terms of AI and digital technologies, not just those 40,000 who are doing digital and AI qualifications. That is something that we need to bear in mind.

A last thing on this particular question is just to consider the role of what I am going to call the digiTECs—digital technical excellence colleges. We have a range of colleges of technical excellence, and five are in the digital area. They are focusing on that point about how they can support not only staff, but students to gain the necessary skills, looking particularly at things such as AI. I think that that is very important.

Chris Loveday: AI in particular can have a real impact on education through many lenses. The first one is obviously the workload for teachers, but I think it is important we understand that it can also have a hugely positive impact on the workload of the operational teams and the business services that support educational institutions.

We have a need and an obligation to train and educate the young people at our establishments on the safe and effective use of AI, particularly in and around what is appropriate for academic use, and understanding the challenges around misinformation, disinformation, bias and hallucination. Finally, there is the impact that it will have on teaching and learning and assessment.

There are pros and cons to all of these angles, but the important thing is that we do not lose sight of the fact that, whatever we do, it needs to be differentiated for both the staff and the student, because people will approach this from different levels. Most importantly, we have to continue to provide an opportunity for those who do not wish to engage with the technology as an approach. It is a very challenging environment, but the benefits and wins significantly outweigh the risks and losses at this moment.

Mary Meekings: I am here bringing the perspective of the staff. It is interesting to hear some of the reflections already. They are really diverse: UCU will have members who are designing this tech and are really involved in developing it, members using it at work and embracing it, and members with real concerns about what it means.

For me, that is exemplified by what I am told by FE staff—that it is different in different departments. It is not even college to college where we are seeing different levels of adoption. We have one department really embracing it and encouraging their staff to use it, and then we have a department in the same college rejecting the same bit of tech. So we have to be quite careful about how we talk about that in terms of those diverse ranges.

We are also hearing educators saying that they feel like they are teaching themselves how to use this technology at the moment—it is great to see Chris nodding along with me; it is good to have a shared understanding about that. There are massively different levels of confidence in using the tech, and we have staff telling us that they are using their own money to access some of the tech as well. We really need an urgent look at those points.

The worst-case scenario we have heard about is where humans are almost completely removed—where you have AI being used to develop a learning tool, the student is then using AI to respond to it, and then the marking is being done by AI as well, so you just have computer-to-computer interaction. I don’t think that is anything any of us here would want to see.

I want to pick up on the workload comments. That is not substantiated by evidence at the moment. We have a workload survey coming out and FE workload is through the roof and going up year on year. I completely share the aspiration—we want to direct this tech so it does help with that very real problem—but it is just not what staff are telling us at the moment in terms of their experiences of it. We know staff are using it, so there is clearly an issue there, because workload remains a real issue.

Our central concern, and this is shared by other education unions—we have been working alongside them—is that we need to see AI enhancing rather than undermining the quality of education and the professional autonomy of educators. The relationship between staff and students is key. Whatever happens next, that needs to be retained.

Chair: Ben, we have heard about the perspective in colleges. What about your members and the impact that AI is having for them?

Ben Rowland: I would answer the question in two parts. The first is the impact on institutions; the other is how learners are benefiting from this.

We recently completed a study with one of our partners, Ufi VocTech Trust, on our members’ adoption of AI, which I will share with the Committee. That is obviously quite timely. Reflecting on that, they have made the furthest progress on back-office operations—timetabling, scheduling and so on; next is teaching—preparing teaching materials; and they say learning comes next. Last, in terms of the positive impact, is assessment, for lots of the issues that have already been raised, which we have some numbers on as well.

That is the impact on institutions. Among my members, I think there is a cautious and growing optimism around their ability to use it. They are typically very practical, outward-facing organisations—sometimes quite small—and they are looking to see how they can shave a bit off here, shave a bit off there and improve a bit there, so they are naturally quite experimental. I have to say that they are well supported by the software providers, many of whom they have been partnering with for many years. Lots of the AI innovation is going on in those partners as opposed to in the training organisations.

I think there are two types of learner. One is those who are doing the AI and digital programmes. That is a real beacon of success across the sector because apprenticeships in digital, AI and cyber-security are long-established and are often partnering very deeply with people such as Microsoft and Oracle who provide significant educational resources. We can be quite confident that apprenticeships in the digital space, including modern AI and cyber-security, are successful and mature.

There is a separate question about those learners who are not on digital programmes and how it incorporates into all their learning. We find the same thing that has been hinted at already: where teachers are unsure themselves and do not know how to use AI, it is very hard for them to engage in teaching young people how to use it.

Jodie Gosling: The Government are introducing significant post-16 qualification changes and reforms, including V-levels, level 2 pathways and changes to English and maths. How might the expansion of AI and educational technology affect the success of these reforms?

Cath Sezen: I think that is a really interesting question. In terms of T-levels, we are seeing digital T-levels being updated to incorporate more AI. They are also being consolidated, which I think is a good thing because it is about making sure that young people are very clear about the qualification that they are taking and where it is going to lead them.

It is interesting that there is going to be only one digital V-level. You could argue that perhaps there needs to be more than one because it is so wide in terms of the occupations and impact. However, in terms of having a V-level, it does mean that you can mix and match. You could perhaps be doing some business qualifications as your other two V-levels, and then you could do a digital V-level. I think that offers an opportunity because, going back to what we have all been saying, it is not just about those young people who are going into a digital career; we are all going to need digital and AI. That is one of the things that we need to consider.

Realistically, to go into the industry, you probably need to have level 3 and above, so you also need to look at those higher technical pathways at levels 4, 5 and 6. However, when looking at level 2 and below, it is very important that those AI and digital skills are incorporated. I think it is something we have to consider as we move forward.

One of the key challenges that colleges always talk about in this space is the ability to move quickly enough in terms of qualifications. We have quite a regulated landscape here. I am not commenting on whether that is positive or negative, but the regulatory aspect means that sometimes, by the time the qualification is on the table and ready to deliver, there are new technologies that have come on board. One of the things that we need to consider is how we can speed up the process of getting qualifications on the table. When you have a lot of reform going on, that is quite challenging.

Ben Rowland: I think the exciting bit is that AI can really help in both of those. For V-levels, T-levels and anything where the vast majority is in the classroom, creating the experiences out of the classroom is the challenge—we know that is the case for things such as work experience—so simulation becomes really important. Flight simulators have been around for decades and are a crucial part of pilot training. Those simulators have never been around for other occupations because they have been just too expensive, until now. That bit is really exciting. You want to say to a young person, “We’re going to give you some experience—if not quite the experience—of what it’s like to be on a building site or railway track, or in a hospital ward.”

These technologies exist now and there are a couple to call out. One, which is well established in the college network, is Metaverse Learning. It is providing simulations where people can move around different environments, get feedback on what they are doing, and so on. You can imagine that being easily brought into pre-16. Another one, funded in part by our partner Ufi VocTech Trust, is called Bodyswaps, where you can literally step into someone’s shoes so that you can give feedback and then swap and be the person receiving your own feedback. That really brings it to life in an emotional way: “Gosh, okay, I know how I should be giving feedback, and it’s not like that.”

On maths and English, an interesting thing is going on. Again, a joint member is the Activate Learning group, which is using AI as a maths and English tutor for those who are borderline. This might be an interesting example of taking a human out of the loop, because one of the problems with English and maths for people who have really struggled through school and are having to do resits is feeling that they are being judged by yet another human being. Taking the human out of the loop and allowing a computer to be the person guiding you through what could otherwise be a really awkward interaction—because you are so painfully aware of your shortcomings in English and maths—might be a way of overcoming some of those barriers. There are some really interesting opportunities there.

Jodie Gosling: Chris, I can see you nodding, so we will come to you next.

Chris Loveday: I think this is really interesting, because for me, there are two lenses for looking at this. The first is the curriculum content. Whether it is V-level, T-level or English and maths, does the curriculum prepare the young person—the learner—for the world in which digital technology, including AI, exists, and how does the learner use it within that curriculum? If it is a technical or vocational qualification, they might be able to use some augmented reality and simulation, but also, for a business-related qualification, they might be learning how to use this technology to achieve outcomes within the curriculum.

The second lens is the student experience. For me, this is something we can often lose. I am talking about understanding how a student may engage with the technology, teaching them how to engage with it correctly and, most importantly, not losing the students who do not have access to it, through digital equity.

One of the biggest concerns I have around the evolution of digital and technology, including AI, in education is that the digital poverty gap widens to become a chasm and is no longer a gap. For me, there are four key pillars: the use of digital technology with a full-size qwerty keyboard; access to a device; access to the internet; and access to a safe and secure premium large language model.

We heard Mary refer to staff potentially paying for access to AI systems. The risk is, first, that that is not controlled within the organisation’s tenancy and environment and the data is potentially going into the public domain. I don’t believe young people are aware of those gaps, and if they cannot afford the paid version, they are using the free version. So the risk is this: what intellectual property, what data and what information are they sharing with that free version and how are they exposing themselves?

Those are the two lenses. The first is the curriculum content. The second is the student experience, digital equity and how we bridge that gap.

Mary Meekings: The first thing on the changes to the curriculum and the Government’s agenda is recognising workload. This Committee will be well aware of the system that we have at the moment in further education—a system that is really under strain. I spoke about workload in my last answer, and it is a really big problem, so any changes need to be managed extremely carefully and by really engaging with educators so that they are ready to embrace those and take them forward. I think that is the only way that that is going to work.

The concept of taking humans out of the loop is interesting. I think that in the future we will be screaming to bring humans back into the loop. Again, I think it is about getting a balance and really working that through.

I found the talk about digital equity really interesting, because it could flip the other way. Students could have too much exposure to machines, and then parents and teachers will be looking again for the human experience as the gold stuff in the system. I think we need to be a bit careful about how we talk about that, because we could accidentally create a digital underclass of people who are solely being taught by machines, and that then becomes the problem in our system. I think we need to be really careful about how we embrace that and take it forward.

Clearly, my view is that the way you do that is that you work with the staff and with their unions to work out what is happening on the ground. I think putting those guardrails in place is really important for the next step.

Jodie Gosling: Does AI present different opportunities and challenges across academic, vocational and technical pathways? If so, should the use of AI vary according to qualification type?

Mary Meekings: I think so. Someone needs to be accountable for it as well. That is the really important thing, so that we could not just say, in a blanket way, that this is how it needs to work in every college, university or school. We have to have that human accountability, so we know that someone is responsible for it.

Ben Rowland: Vocational training is a bit easier. For academic teaching, the tried and tested method for a couple of hundred years has been to have asynchronous written tasks, which AI has completely blown out of the water within a couple of years, whereas vocational training is all about wanting someone to do something. Proving that you can do something is less threatened by that fundamental change to academic learning. It is more about the opportunities side for FE and vocational education.

Cath Sezen: It is really important that any organisation does an audit about what works best where. The most important thing is that you are putting your students and staff at the centre of all this, and then building from there. We have already talked about Bodyswaps and the virtual reality opportunities that offers. That is a great idea.

I totally agree with Mary that the most important thing is having AI and digital technology that augments teachers, rather than replaces them. In terms of the work that is being done at Activate, I totally agree because it offers the opportunity for personalisation, perhaps for a young person who already feels that they have failed, in terms of English and maths, and just wants to sit down and focus on their own.

The key is thinking about what your strategy is as an organisation and how you best support students and staff to make sure that you help everybody reach their potential.

Chris Loveday: To echo Cath’s point and maybe elaborate a little, education is not one size fits all, regardless of qualifications. It is important that institutions recognise their level of preparedness to embrace both technology and artificial intelligence.

At the moment, if you do not have a robust infrastructure or if you do not have reliable wi-fi that can support student use, you can have all the VR headsets in the world, but they become unusable. It is about understanding your institution's position, where its baseline is and where you want to get to, and then moving towards it. You have to ask what problem you are trying to solve with technology to understand if technology is a solution or not. We sometimes lose sight of trying to solve a problem rather than chasing technology.

Manuela Perteghella: The interim Milburn report recently warned that, without policy intervention, as many as one in six young people in the UK could be not in education, employment or training within five years. Is AI likely to make this situation worse, or could AI be leveraged to help reverse this trend? If so, how?

Cath Sezen: There are probably many things outside the remit of digital and AI that will help young people who are currently NEET. Focusing on the remit of digital and AI, for many young people it is about some of those skills: digital literacy, critical thinking and knowing how to utilise the technology effectively. As I have said, those skills are important not just if you are going to work in cyber-security, but if you are going to work in the health sector. We need to bear those things in mind.

On the other hand, we also need to bear in mind that some entry-level roles may be changing. That is not going to be the case in certain sectors. For example, in hair and beauty and in catering and hospitality you always have those entry roles because you are providing a service. But things are going to change in some sectors. We have seen changes in retail, for example, with things moving online.

We need to prepare young people, which is really important. We need to prepare them for the future, but we also need to take a step back and look at what happens earlier in the education system so that we actually prepare them for the future from a much earlier stage by providing vocational and technical options, which would also include elements of AI and digital. I am pleased to see that the curriculum and assessment review is looking at the importance of those literacies to support young people.

In terms of NEET, there will be some positives, but there are also some challenges that we need to bear in mind.

Ben Rowland: On the slightly broader question of the role of technology in whether we move from one in eight being NEET to one in six, getting a hold on social media is a real problem. A reason for young people being most at risk of not engaging in the labour market is a lack of confidence and self-belief. They are more likely to be hidden at home, staying at home and protecting themselves from the outside world, and social media becomes the thing they do. How we handle social media is a very big part. I am sure Alan Milburn will have something to say on that.

If the Milburn review is to talk about much better outreach to people who are stuck at home and do not know how to get out, as I imagine it might, the quality of information and how they use that information is important. We do not know who these NEETs are. We say there are nearly a million, but there is no list of a million people nicely organised across local authorities—it is a survey-based figure. We need to get hold of information, manipulate it and use it to direct this very scarce resource back to outreach work, where the human should definitely be in the loop. How do we equip those people to go and find young people with the right interventions at the right time, amplified by smart use of technology?

There is a finite and diminishing number of entry-level roles. It is really important that whatever recommendations Alan Milburn makes do not damage employers’ engagement with the apprenticeship system, including for older apprentices. The slightly counterintuitive point is that sometimes the best way to help someone who is NEET get into a job is to help the person who is already in that job and train them up. The idea that somehow apprenticeships should only be for young people is misguided, because older people need to be pulled up. That then creates an entry-level vacancy into which the young person can go.

The final thing I would say is that there will be huge opportunities in AI and digital, particularly around cyber-security, which is the corollary of any good deployment of AI. Our AI and digital apprenticeships are really strong in this country. We should lean in behind that and help them to flourish.

Chris Loveday: Building on what Ben has just said, prevention is better than cure. It is about the power of digital. In relation to the Milburn report, we are currently working on this at my college. We are aware of the factors that drive young people to become not in employment, education or training. Through school data and our college data, we can identify those at greatest risk and proactively target support at those young people on their journey to try to stop them becoming disengaged in education. The earlier we do that, the better.

At the moment, one of the benefits of this technology is that we are able to use live data, rather than historical data, with the triggers contextualised to our organisation and our catchment. Portsmouth is one of the highest-risk areas for potential NEETs. We are proactively trying to intervene earlier to support learners to stop them becoming disengaged and leaving the sector. Through trend analysis of student cohorts, we understand that students who arrive from a certain socioeconomic position or a certain postcode with an average GCSE score and doing a certain type of subject typically do not succeed or typically need more intervention. By using that type of trend analysis of historical data, we can target the student, intervene meaningfully earlier and allocate the resources directly to the right people at the right time. That is what we are working towards. That is something where technology will excel.

The role of technology in keeping young people in education through practical use is very different from identifying them before they become disengaged and trying to re-engage them, identifying the barriers to their learning and helping to break them down so that they want to stay in education, employment or training. It is essential that this technology helps to guide the strategy rather than the operational changes for each institution to target those young people.

Mary Meekings: That targeting sounds great. I am mindful that we are talking about so many people. We are umbrellaing the term “NEET”, which many of us do not really like—maybe we can think of something else to categorise it. The targeting is needed. Again, it is about how we direct the technology, rather than the technology itself being the solution.

I am also really mindful of the pace of change. If you have not been in education, employment or training for a couple of years, the tech is pulling away from you. Cath spoke about the importance of having this earlier in the education system, but I would also point to later in the education system, which means looking at our adult education system. We know there are grave concerns about the cuts that have been made to adult education, but surely that is going to be a key part of the retraining tools.

That would be a plea to the Committee: really look at the role of adult education and work on that. Again, I reiterate the role of educators in this. It is great if we can have that targeted use and the tools to support it, but human intervention is going to be critical.

Manuela Perteghella: Are there particular groups of young people who are more at risk of being left behind by AI-driven changes? If so, what can be done to ensure that they are not excluded from employment, education or training?

Mary Meekings: I go back to what we were saying earlier. There should not be a sense that having access to the technology equals benefiting from the technology. There is a risk that we create a digital underclass of people who almost have too much exposure to the technology, so we need to be careful about determining it by saying, “The amount of technology equals the amount of benefit.” I think it is about trying to get that balance right.

Ben Rowland: It has always been the way with technology that you need a certain degree of confidence and self-belief that you can learn a new thing. Young people who have had the privilege or benefit of being in that position are the ones who are most able to take advantage, and the inverse is also true. It has always stretched the opportunity and capability divide. If you want people to be competent and comfortable with AI, it is about their underpinning education and the self-belief they have in themselves generally, not just about AI.

Cath Sezen: For me, it goes back to something Chris said earlier about equity of access. It is really important that we fund our organisations effectively so that they can update and provide those resources. Education should be your point of access, particularly if you do not necessarily have good wi-fi or the resources at home. Lots of young people will have a phone, but that is not necessarily what is needed when you are trying to teach people how to interact in a digital and AI world. I think it goes back to the point about equity and providing facilities for young people, notwithstanding the point that you obviously need to make sure that they are very well aware of how to use that technology appropriately.

Chris Loveday: The point about too much exposure to technology is absolutely valid, but having led a secondary school during the covid pandemic—where our reaction was to fast-track technology to people and then train them to use it in a way that was meaningful—I can tell you that technology is here and is prevalent in education. The digital divide is one that really poses a risk. Typically, the students who are excluded from education, employment and training are those from a lower socioeconomic background who typically do not have access to the technologies.

To the point that it could create a digital underclass, I agree that too much technology is wrong, but we cannot govern or regulate that at the moment. What we can control is the fact that we know that a lot of education is delivered online—whether through a platform or a digital solution like Teams, or using Google for submitting homework.

We must give students the access, literacy skills and confidence that make it accessible and convenient. For example, if you say to young people, “Okay, you don’t have internet at home. If you stay until 6 o’clock every night of the week, we will help you at the school,” they are not necessarily going to engage with that solution, so the risk is being perpetuated by the lack of technology availability and the lack of digital equity.

To the point about what happens if we achieve digital equity, it is important that we do not go too far and lose the thing that we inherently crave as individuals, which is human contact. I think there is a balance to be found, but the risk of digital inequity, for me, is far more prevalent and far more dangerous than the risk of a digital underclass with too much technology.

Peter Swallow: I want to delve a little deeper into this distinction, which you have helpfully set out, between a digital poverty gap and, on the other hand, there potentially being a risk of too much technology. I want to think specifically about how we can best develop inclusive pedagogies to support learners with special educational needs.

I am sure we have all heard parents and teachers being really positive about the potential of AI to support the learning of young people with special educational needs. There is also a lot of concern that there might be an overreliance on it. It would be good to have your expert view on how we get that balance right.

Chris Loveday: For me, it has to be operational-level again—based on the organisation. We built our own large language model for staff and students that was multilingual by design. It was accessible by design, with voice to text and text to voice. It had light and dark modes, and a colour-blind mode. We designed the tool to be accessible, but it was always designed to support the teaching and learning in the classroom—it was there to support the teaching and learning, not replace it, and the young people still needed a TA, for example, to support them.

The risk is that education is at a bit of a precipice, where recruiting staff to roles like TAs and LSAs is more challenging. Typically, the entry requirements are lower, yet these people provide the one-to-one support, helping young people with great need to navigate a curriculum that potentially the LSA or TA themselves do not understand.

Technology can play a role in supporting teaching but, speaking to the point raised previously, the risk is becoming dependent on it. It is important that the organisation, the institution and the individual have judgment. They get to know the young person and they get to regulate the use and the method of use, which is where guardrails are so important.

I advocate the solution of building your own model so that you can build in guardrails, so that it does not become overfamiliar and so it does not help you build a relationship or a bond with the technology, and that it remains precisely what technology should be—an enabler and facilitator, not a replacement for humanity.

This is where technology is now a risk. It is about making sure those guardrails are in place and that human oversight remains in the loop at all times, so that we do not see young people being adversely affected by relationships with a technology or dependence on a technology.

Peter Swallow: Presumably you are doing ongoing evaluation of the effects of the AI tool you have developed, particularly for students with SEND. What is your data telling you about the impact it is having on their learning—positive or negative, or whatever it may be?

Chris Loveday: It is very early in adoption at the moment. What is interesting is that the more a grown-up tells a young person, “This is a great technology”, the less they want to use it, so not promoting it works. Like anything, it is about human engagement. If a young person is curious, interested and finds the benefit, then they engage, but for others I think there is a real apprehension.

A lot of our SEND students are not technology experts, so to give them more technology without human intervention is a fear. It is about the member of staff sitting with them and going, “Look, this is how we can use this technology.”

Where I have seen the technology excel is in English as an additional language. With people who do not speak English as their first language, the ability to converse in their natural language and have it converted to English, or from English to their natural language, has really helped EAL students to excel. Also, those with visual impairment have benefited, but it has to be a visual impairment that light or dark modes can support. Like anything, it has its place, but it is very early days to be able to say meaningfully what impact it is having.

Mary Meekings: I think we all agree that the guardrails that we are talking about are potentially not quite in place. That is what we need, and it is certainly what teachers are looking for.

From an equalities perspective, it is always quite concerning if people are being categorised and monitored and having decisions made about them. I think we would all want humans to be involved in those judgments and perspectives, rather than handing that over to the machines.

Of course we want digital inclusion, and we want everyone to have access to those things; I don’t want to suggest otherwise. The point we are trying to make is that we do not want to pass over our responsibility and accountability to those machines. That really is where Government can help by stepping in, looking at the balance and putting guardrails in place. Particularly with SEND students, we have a real responsibility to make sure that people are not further disadvantaged by judgments that might be made by the—I don’t want to say “technology”, actually; I specifically mean the AI.

Ben Rowland: One of the areas that is more advanced in the use of AI is creating tailored teaching materials. There is a solution called TeacherMatic, which a growing number of colleges and ITPs are using. That really helps teachers. Late at night, it might be, “It’s 10 o’clock and I need some fresh Quizlet cards now,” and it will help them to do that. But it can also be useful for saying, “Five of my students have needed particular extra help—can you remodel these questions or this material so that it suits them?” or “Can you just check that this is okay to work with for them?” In many cases, it says, “Yes, this is fine,” so they go in the next morning confident that it is going to work. Charlotte Armstrong: To give a brief bit of context, HEPI produces its generative AI survey, which surveys thousands of students on their AI use. It has been running for three years and that is where a lot of my data will come from.

Are universities doing what is needed at the moment to provide the skills and the training? It is not just about how students use AI to study. There is an ecosystem here: how are students using AI to study, how are staff using AI to enhance the learning progress and how are students learning the skills that will be useful for their future careers. They are all very closely related, but there are differences in what is required to deliver the skills in relation to each of those things.

Universities are doing a huge amount at the moment. I am rarely in a meeting that is not in some way focused on the impact of AI on what we do and how we do it, but the solutions we design will change. We have just heard some really interesting things from colleagues in further education. The students we have at the moment have not come through with that earlier-years exposure to AI, and the training in AI skills. The students who will arrive with us in the years to come will have had much more of that. That will mean we will have to shift, as that wave comes through, until we hopefully reach something approaching a steady state. That steady state will have to be adaptable to the changes in AI practice as we go through.

What are we doing? There are all sorts of things going on in terms of supporting students with upskilling. Science and engineering are a great training ground for this. We have colleagues working in engineering who are using AI tools that are absolutely deeply embedded in their teaching. The students are using these tools, for example, as part of the design cycle to work through: they have a range of specific tools targeted at each part of that cycle, not just generic LLMs and “How are we doing it?”

Peter Swallow: If I can push back a little, employers will say that AI digital skills are obviously really important, but they will also highlight a lot of other skills that have nothing to do with AI, except for the fact that in a world dominated by AI, they are more valuable than ever: communication, collaboration and the ability to analyse something and judge whether it is accurate. Those are not necessarily uniquely scientific skills; they are much broader than that. Are you also empowering your students with those skills?

Professor Banks: Absolutely. The answer that I gave before was specifically to the question of how we are helping to upskill people in relation to AI. Really usefully, the whole AI debate is causing a lot of deep thought around what the purpose of education is and what the value is for somebody coming through a university education. It is really helping to focus attention on the fact that the output from a university education is not the degree that is awarded but the person who emerges at the end, their set of skills and the way that they have developed through those three or four years, or one year if it is postgraduate. That causes a shift in focus towards asking where the added value is and how we are supporting students with that.

It has to be very much rooted in a disciplinary or interdisciplinary knowledge base. We do not want to just teach skills without rooting them in the knowledge associated with the discipline; those two go hand in hand. You will have AI skills coming through, and you will have skills that have variously been described as soft, transferable and so on. They will relate, but you will have to have an education that builds in that mixed economy. Certainly, at King’s for example, we are looking to a future where we embed AI skills training in the disciplines but also root that knowledge base in the analogue skills so that the cognitive process is not lost.

Peter Swallow: That is really helpful. Lewis, what are students saying about the skills that they are developing on their courses?

Lewis Wilson: Charlotte touched on it very briefly. It is that game of cat and mouse where universities’ policy is too inflexible and does not keep pace with how rapidly AI is changing, and where the way that students are using it is unfortunately always outpacing lecturers. The real problem is that universities are under-resourced to be able to deliver and prepare students properly for the skills that they need when they enter the workforce because there is just no money in higher education.

Michael Webb: I see a very similar picture, particularly to Simon’s description of the work at King’s, across the sector. When you say, “Are universities doing it?”, it is more complex. The intention across the university sector is very much to prepare students for that AI workplace, and top-level mechanisms to make that happen are in place pretty much universally, but the capacity to deliver on those is much more variable. These tools cost money and staff will be under different pressures in terms of workload and so on, so by the time it reaches students, their experience will be very mixed.

Peter Swallow: Can we take a step back along the food chain to think about research and innovation? The Government have identified AI as a strategic policy for UK research. What do you see as the potentials for enhancing the quality, impact and global competitiveness of UK universities’ research?

Michael Webb: I will start with Jisc’s part to play in that. Our expertise is around infrastructure, so network connectivity and cyber-security, both of which are hugely impacted by AI. You cannot have escaped seeing all the cyber issues caused by AI in the news. We need to have a collective, sector-level response to support the institutions. Obviously, cutting-edge AI research requires fast, reliable connectivity, which, again, we have in the UK and need to continue to invest in. We have the core. We need to continue investing in it.

Charlotte Armstrong: The main point that I want to make about research is that it is a live discussion in institutions. A lot of the response so far has been so focused on students and responding to assessment and things, so that research has often not been the first point of concern because there has been an element of firefighting as the first response.

On research, the difficulty is that research is frequently very collaborative by its very nature—not only collaborative across institutions, but across nations. When it comes to regulating rules around how much AI use is acceptable—the methods but also the intellectual property and data security around that—it becomes increasingly difficulty the more partners that you have involved.

That is one of the massive stumbling blocks that institutions are facing at the moment when they are deciding how they should react to AI in research.

Peter Swallow: Is there also that distinction between different disciplines in terms of the amount of AI-powered research that is going on between different faculties? Have you seen that as well?

Charlotte Armstrong: I do not know. It is not something that we have done much investigation into. Professor Banks may be more equipped to answer that.

Peter Swallow: We will go to Professor Banks, then.

Professor Banks: It is discipline-dependent. It is having an enormous impact on research. It is having a potentially very positive impact in terms of providing a way to accelerate some of the ideation process in generating and testing out ideas.

In some areas, it is also just having a very direct application. One can see all sorts of practical benefits in the sciences for bypassing some of the more labour-intensive aspects of the chain that runs through a research project. But it is crucial that that “human in the loop” element is there, and that there is human ownership of decision making, authorship and so on.

The area in which it is causing the most concern at the moment seems to be mathematics, for obvious reasons. Recently, there was the publication of over 700 proofs of mathematical theorems that have been out in the ether for sometimes decades without much progress, or with incremental progress.

An LLM solved or was purported to have solved—because there has been challenge on some of them—such a large number in such a short time. That has caused a great deal of thought and discussion among the mathematical community about what the implications are for what maths research is. That is particularly focusing on questions around the PhD level, which is in the sweet spot.

Of course, that then extends out into the question of which discipline is next. Where is the language of a particular discipline likely to be under threat, as opposed to aided by this?

There is a huge amount going on. If anything, the aspect that we are probably most in need of at the moment is a discussion around what regulation of AI looks like in that context, and what the UK’s role is in terms of how it positions itself relative to that idea of a regulatory framework in relation to research.

Lewis Wilson: I will just say that PGR students sometimes feel the pressure or cannot use AI because of the researcher that they are working most closely with. It is about student choice and allowing them to either use it or opt out of not using it.

Jodie Gosling: Turning to staffing, and the skills that higher education staff are going to need in the future, how is AI likely to reshape higher education staffing over the next five to 10 years? Will universities need fewer people or will staff just need a different skillset to address the needs of students?

Charlotte Armstrong: As was said in the previous session, there are varied levels of enthusiasm and adoption among staff. One recommendation we made, as part of our survey, was the importance of integrating AI into the curriculum. That cannot simply be about teaching students; it also needs to be about equipping staff with the skills to teach students how to use AI. There needs to be a conscious effort to allow staff the time and space to have that training, so that they can assist the students most effectively.

There has been some discussion, slightly adjacent to the education sector, around AI potentially being able to teach students, which concerns me. We recently made a 20-year compilation of our academic experience survey. That dataset contains 206,512 responses from students over 20 years. The most important driver of student experience is relationships with academic staff and the quality of the teaching they receive. The idea that AI might mean that we do not need staff to interact with students as much is incredibly concerning and needs to be pushed back on as hard as possible.

The other thing I want to raise briefly is some research done by Queen Mary University of London, alongside Erasmus University Rotterdam, University of Brazil, Campinas, and McMaster University, Toronto. Medical students were provided with virtual case studies. Some were simply provided with the cases; some were given AI feedback; some were given feedback just from lecturers; and some were given feedback from both AI tools and lecturers.

The students who had received feedback from both human lecturers and AI tools performed best in the assessments. The students who had received feedback simply from AI tools performed the worst, but were most confident in their answers, which is really concerning. That is just one study, but it shows the importance of having a combination, taking and using the benefits of AI but ensuring that human feedback and interaction remain a key part of the process, so that we can be critical of what AI produces.

Professor Banks: Inevitably, staff will have to be upskilled across the board. We are not talking about a world in which only science and engineering graduates need these skills and therefore need to learn them at university; it will be across the disciplines. There will be a change in baseline expectation about awareness, exposure and so on in the skills for staff. Will it change requirements for the number of staff? I would be surprised; I have not seen evidence of that at the moment.

In my experience, staff tend to use AI to do new and different things to help enhance the experience of students, and to provide additional resources and ways of doing something. They are, therefore, effectively taking their time. They might introduce an efficiency over here, and then build on that to do something different and new over here. They have not reduced the overall time. If anything, a lot of us probably spend a bit more time.

An interesting study that came out of Harvard recently looked at using AI tutors as part of a human-led course. That would be humans teaching the course but using a specifically designed AI tutor. The results from that look very promising. There is that partnership, seeing AI as an assistant that can help and accelerate students’ learning. I do think that there is a question around course administration.

To come back to the further education part of this morning’s discussion, I think a conversation is needed about the application to marking, and the way in which trust is going to change as people’s understanding of how the models work, what they do and what their role in marking might look like in future changes. We need to be open to that. A very good point was made about marking being a way to understand your students; that is less relevant if you are marking anonymously, which is the most common way in the university sector. There are ways that we have not yet explored in detail, partly because of lack of trust and partly because of lack of evidence base.

Jodie Gosling: Lewis, is it a concern for students that the dynamics might change in respect of teaching staff?

Lewis Wilson: Definitely. I have heard of one horror case, which I will briefly outline because it is so egregious. I was speaking recently to a maths student who had been to a lecture, and the lecturer had made all the slides through Grok’s AI—X, formerly Twitter. At the end of the lecture, all the students were a bit confused and wanted to ask some questions, and the lecturer said, “I’m too busy. Ask the questions to Grok instead.” That speaks to the wider point I made earlier about the lack of resourcing in the higher education sector. It definitely concerns students, especially when frontline staff are being cut from universities.

Another thing that we have touched on briefly was replacement or enhancement with AI tools, rather than frontline members of staff. At the moment I am seeing across the sector the introduction of chatbots so that students have a place to ask questions and all that sort of stuff, whether about university processes or the course. I am really concerned about the legal need around reasonable adjustments. The University of Bristol v. Abrahart case showed that if a student says to any part of the university, “I need a reasonable adjustment—I’m disabled,” the university needs to see that through and make sure that reasonable adjustment is met. What is the obligation on the chatbot to disclose that to the university and make sure that that reasonable adjustment is made? A lot of students are worried about other pitfalls and that they might start slipping through the cracks. It is a really scary prospect.

Jodie Gosling: Michael, Jisc has said that some teaching staff feel a “loss of craft” as aspects of teaching and resource development become more automated. To what extent is AI changing what it means to be a teacher or a researcher, and how can that be mitigated?

Michael Webb: I will start with the marking side, because we focused on that a lot last year, exactly for the reasons Simon gave. There are potentially benefits, because it is time-consuming, but also because human marking is not perfect, which we sometimes forget in this debate. It is one of the areas where people fear a loss of their professional identity, as marking is very much at the core of what they do.

Interestingly, we found that, at the moment, the mechanics around checking the stuff outweigh the time benefits for high-stakes stuff anyway, so it became a secondary issue. It would have become a core thing if we had found out that it saved 50% of the time—the loss of professional identity would then be something we need to talk more about—but we actually think it is now really only for lower-stakes stuff.

It is similar to things like the lesson plan debate we heard about earlier. We need to understand that the use of AI is very varied. We tend to think of it as, for example, putting in a prompt and a lesson plan coming out. That would clearly mean you are losing your craft, and people are pushing back on that. Actually, there are all sorts of ways that you can use it. I particularly hate using word processors to format stuff—it takes me ages. I just want my AI tool to collect my thoughts, so I tell it how to do it and hand over the formatting. To me, I am keeping all my craft, and it is taking away the bit that I do not like and that does not add value. The training we do at Jisc for lots of staff says, “Use AI for the bits that you don’t value doing and keep the other stuff for yourself.” We can then keep the craft.

Jodie Gosling: How much variation is there across the higher education sector in the uptake and effective use of AI by staff? What factors are driving the differences?

Lewis Wilson: It definitely varies from lecturer to lecturer, from department to department and from course to course. Students doing PPE, which is effectively three different courses, see such variation across different schools in how they are being taught and in what is allowed and is fair usage of AI. It really comes down to the whole academic freedom debate—so many lecturers are anti AI and so many are pro it. It needs to come back to the point that students should be allowed to have the choice of whether they use it, rather than necessarily others.

Michael Webb: I want to pick up on that point as well, because this is one of our latest hot topics. We have monthly practitioner meet-ups, and the right response to people's ethical objections to using AI is a live issue. A staff member might say, “I wish you to use AI in this assignment because you will gain these skills.” What should the response be if students say they have an ethical objection to AI? The same goes for if a staff member is required to deliver it to meet the learning aims, but they object—how do we deal with it? That has added to the complexity of very uneven adoption and skills, so unfortunately this space is getting more complex as the public debate becomes more polarised.

Jodie Gosling: Is further support or training needed to solve that, or is it just about the ethical discussion?

Michael Webb: There are different things that need to happen. First, we all need more training on some of the details so that our objections are well placed and well informed. Some of them come from misinformation, so it would be better if there was more education.

If we put aside the ethical discussion and move on to the fundamentals of how to use it, more and continual training needed is absolutely needed. That is the real problem: AI is moving so fast that the training you received last week might not be so useful this week. It is about investment in the initial training and then keeping everyone’s skills up to date so they can continually apply it in whatever they do.

Charlotte Armstrong: I agree with everything that has been said. I want to reiterate the last two points. Staff and students who do not want to use AI are currently getting lost slightly in the discussion around how it is being used. We have seen a rapid rise in the number of people who are concerned about AI use, particularly for environmental reasons, over the past three years—that has completely shot up in our responses. I would imagine—we do not know yet; our next survey is about to go out into the field—that that will continue to rise with. My main things to highlight are the need to think about those who disagree, our response to that, and the importance of training. I really agree with what was just said.

Professor Banks: It is very difficult to quantify the level of variation. It seems clear that there is a great deal of variation across institutions and across subject areas, but actually putting numbers on that would be a challenge.

On the training aspect, there are some really good training tools out there already—Jisc, for example, has recently published some, and there are others. Sometimes there might be a lack of awareness of what is available. Sometimes there is a sense of not being able to see the wood for the trees, in that there is so much. There are so many guidance documents and training tools, and they could be brought down, for a practitioner, to the level of somebody who wants just to upskill and get a basic fundamental level of knowledge to be able to move forward and do their job.

The other thing is the slight fear of missing out. Do I understand this big scary world of AI and what it means for me? Is it easier to set it to one side, focus on the known, and hope that this will clarify over time and I will join it then? For both staff and students there can be a sense of feeling, “I ought to be doing something with this. There’s a real opportunity over here, but I don’t quite see my way in.”

Manuela Perteghella: We have heard that AI has profound implications for traditional approaches to assessment—for example, coursework. How can universities ensure that assessment remains reliable and educationally meaningful in an age of widespread AI use?

Professor Banks: Universities have a crucial role in certifying the students who leave them, but it is important to remember that we are certifying the human being. Ideally, you want to be able to say, “This person has this level of knowledge, this set of skills,” and so on, and you want to be able to do that with confidence and to be able to stand behind that certification.

I think there is a wide and growing acceptance of the fact that some of the traditional assessment practices in higher education are seriously threatened by the realities of what generative AI is now able to do. That is necessarily going to cause a really significant rethink of the way we assess. It is already happening—those discussions are already under way—and some of the changes are moving forward.

For my part, it comes down to a question of the balance of risk. There has always been some risk associated with remote assessments, but the ability to cheat—for want of a better word—the integrity of those assessments and the balance of their weight overall has been managed and acceptable. I think that balance is shifting. We are going to have to bring a lot more assessment directly back in, and we need to do that in a way that does not lose the genuine benefits of those remote assessments—the authenticity, the skills and so on. It is going to be a very mixed portfolio, in which there will absolutely be a place for examinations, for orals and for practical classes. It will be a suite.

The other aspect to highlight is that that has to be done in a way that designs in the inclusivity from the start. You cannot retrofit that afterwards. You also cannot redesign assessment without redesigning your teaching to go along with it. The two are not separate; they are inherently linked. You have to think about it end to end: if we are assessing differently over here, how are we teaching differently?

Lewis Wilson: On academic integrity and assessment, I want to touch on how academic misconduct is being flagged somewhat disproportionately for international students who do not necessarily have English as their first language. They sometimes therefore use translation tools, or even tools that are recommended by the Government, like Grammarly, which integrate AI without them necessarily realising it, so they are being flagged unfairly. They also perhaps use practice essays to model their essays, without realising that the sentence starters or the structure mirrors an AI’s way of speaking, meaning they get flagged for AI. I think AI statistics are definitely overinflated, particularly for international students.

I do agree with Simon about needing to develop and change, but I do not think the answer is necessarily more technology to try to catch out people who are misusing AI. When I was a student, something that I saw at my institution, the University of Sussex, was the remote proctoring of exams. An AI camera is filming you the entire time and monitoring your movements, what screen you are looking at and all sorts of different stuff. The concern around your personal data being sent off to one of these big American firms is a different concern, but that can affect disabled students who maybe have tics, and it can lead to the false flagging of misconduct.

It comes back to the wider issue of assessment design needing to be collaborative, and speaking not only to employers but to students to make sure that assessments meet the workforce needs and look like students want them to look like. Charlotte Armstrong: We found that 65% of students say that the way that their institution has assessed them has changed in response to AI, so we are definitely seeing shifts in institutions. Anecdotally, we have some students who said, in terms of the qualitative responses to that survey, that they felt like assessment had been made harder in response. I think the main thing to think about with assessments here is the fact that employers want students to be able to use AI. AI will be a key part of much of the workforce; students recognise that. Particularly if you have integrated a degree of training and teaching as part of the course and particularly subject-dependent AI skills as part of the course, that is something you can assess as part of your coursework and as part of an education institution.

It is about just stepping back and thinking, constructing AI-aware assessments, and factoring in whether and how AI impacts what you are trying to find out about what the student understands.

Michael Webb: I agree with that; I will just add some detailed points. First of all, on the technology landscape I think we have to remember that it is changing rapidly again and—unfortunately—that is going to make this whole area even more complex. We have agentic AI now, which can control your computer and use all the applications that you might normally use. It is no longer just about writing essays. We have wearables coming along very quickly, which are going to make supervised assessment a lot more challenging. With glasses today, we can spot them; the next generations will not be able to, so it is going to get more complex.

In our armoury, broadly we have supervised education, which we talked about and which today is safe; it is going to be challenging tomorrow, so let’s not put all our eggs in those baskets. We have then got a mixture of advice and spotting, which is really the predominant method at the moment. It is an interesting one. This is where the students are told how to use AI and the people marking, either with technology or through some guidance, try and make judgments as to whether there is cheating or not. This is probably the most challenging area, to be perfectly honest. There is a lot of pushback against AI text detection tools. The problem is that people are even worse at spotting it than AI. The emerging technical solutions now are, first, around process monitoring.
