Mike Reader made representations.

Q1 Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Backbench Business Committee. We will be considering applications for debates in the main Chamber and in Westminster Hall on a Tuesday and Thursday. The first application is from Mike Reader, for a 90-minute debate in Westminster Hall on a Tuesday morning, on children’s safety on online gaming platforms. Over to you, Mike.

Mike Reader: Thank you, Chair. Everyone in the room who has kids will know the name Roblox, which is a major platform with millions of users. Fewer, though, will know that online gaming is one of the main places where adults first make contact with children. The Government’s own research shows that 40% of offenders sought to contact children online first, and did so via online gaming.

We have often debated social media restrictions in the House, but we have not specifically related them to gaming, which works differently. It involves voice chat, in-game currency, user-generated worlds and lots of pathways to taking children into off-platform private messaging.

The Government have committed to bringing in new restrictions, and have already started with major platforms, but this debate would give us a chance to press the Department on scope, age assurance and the off-platform problem before regulations and rules are written. I have cross-party support from Labour, Conservative, Lib Dem, SNP, DUP and independent Members. I would be grateful for the Committee’s support.

Q2 Will Stone: Thank you for bringing this forward. Westminster Hall debates are governed by the answering Department. Which do you have a preference for, DCMS or DFE?

Mike Reader: We believe that the preferable Department would be DCMS. It is currently bringing in some of the legislation to deal with online protections, so it would be the most appropriate.

Q3 Will Stone: Your application mentions the Government response to the “Growing up in the online world” consultation. Do you have any indication of when that is going to come out, timeline-wise, so we can plan?

Mike Reader: The Government have made some responses already, and those have primarily been platform-specific—Roblox is one specific platform—but in terms of an overall response, no, I do not.

Q4 Will Stone: Is there a specific date you are looking to have this debate by?

Mike Reader: I would be happy to work with the Committee on that, but this side of Christmas or early in the new year would be good.

Chair: Thank you, Mike. The Clerks will be in touch with you in due course. As Will said, we can allocate on a Tuesday morning only when the answering Department is the relevant one. We await the rotas.

Jim Shannon made representations.

Q5 Chair: Next up is our season ticket holder, Jim Shannon. This application is for a debate in Westminster Hall on ending the unhealthy homes and buildings crisis in the UK.

Jim Shannon: Thank you very much, Mr Chairman, for giving me the chance to come and ask for another debate. The debate is about ending the unhealthy homes and buildings crisis in the United Kingdom. Fortunately, I have added some other names to the sheet. We did not have as many Conservative Members, but we now have Sir Edward Leigh, John Cooper and Rishi Sunak. I think that makes four Conservative names on the application form, along with many Lib Dem and lots of Labour Members.

Why is this important? The debate will highlight the urgent need to end the problem of unhealthy homes and buildings in the UK. I believe it is time for the Government to embed health and wellbeing as a core preventive principle across any future legislation, to ensure that our homes and buildings do not continue to cause or exacerbate poor health and wellbeing.

The Government have committed to building 1.5 million homes. That is a large target, and within it there is, I believe, a historic, once-in-a-generation opportunity to adopt a holistic approach and end the blight of unhealthy homes and buildings. When it comes to the warm homes plan, the expansion of social and council housing and the updated decent homes standard, we must ensure that investments in new and existing homes are fit for future generations. We have that opportunity right now, with the Government specifically targeting large building projects across this United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland.

The debate will urge the Government to shift from reacting to poor conditions to preventing illness before it starts. Here is one of the great things about it: the approach does not require burdensome new regulations, but rather the strategic alignment of housing, health, energy and planning around shared outcomes. That has to be an opportunity that we should take advantage of. Thank you very much.

Nesil Caliskan made representations.

Q10 Chair: Next up is Nesil Caliskan, with a request for a debate in the Chamber on Islamophobia Awareness Month. Over to you, Nesil.

Nesil Caliskan: Thank you very much, Chair. I am seeking a debate in support of Islamophobia Awareness Month, which takes place each November. The purpose of the debate would be to consider the prevalence and impact of anti-Muslim hatred in the UK, the contribution of British Muslims to our society, and what more can be done to tackle discrimination. It would be a timely debate, given the continuing concerns about religiously motivated hate crime and the wider public discussion about community relations. The debate would provide Parliament with an opportunity to address an issue of growing concern to my constituents in Barking and Dagenham, but I know that anti-Muslim hatred is also a concern for many others around the country.

Religiously motivated hate crime impacts different communities of different religions. It is a scourge on our society, as I know Members will agree. There is a substantive body of evidence demonstrating the harmful impact that prejudice, discrimination and negative stereotyping can have on individuals and communities. The debate would allow Members to share experiences from their constituencies of anti-Muslim hatred and discuss the Government’s response.

The timing is particularly important, given that the debate would coincide with Islamophobia Awareness Month. Holding the debate in November would maximise public engagement and provide an opportunity for Parliament to demonstrate leadership in challenging religious hatred and promoting community cohesion.

I appreciate the pressures of time; although I am asking for the debate to be in November, it is an ongoing issue across the country, so I would welcome it being held at any time. The principle that people should be able to practice their faith free from hatred is one that I know commands support across the House. I have secured support from Members from different political parties, which reflects the broad interest that people have, and I believe we will ensure a well-attended and balanced debate.

Q11 Martin Vickers: Nesil, as you have recognised, the chances of a November debate in the Chamber are very limited. Would you be prepared to have a Westminster Hall debate, or would you prefer to wait?

Nesil Caliskan: A Westminster Hall debate would be perfectly sufficient. My preference would be November, in recognition of the importance of the month, but as I say, I suspect December will demonstrate the same challenges for this issue.

Q12 Chair: The other issue is that, although there is no problem with the very large number of Members you have on the list, we require a balance between Government and Opposition Members. As your list stands, it is acceptable for a Westminster Hall debate, but you would need to supply probably another four or five names from any of the Opposition parties to balance it so that there would be a balanced debate across the Chamber, were we to take this forward for the Chamber. Without prejudging what colleagues will say, if you want a Chamber debate, you will need to supply extra Opposition names to make this a live application. As has been mentioned, there is not much time to go before the end of November, so you would need to do that very promptly for us to even consider this application for the Chamber.

Nesil Caliskan: I should say that the omission of those names from the official Opposition is my fault. It is largely to do with time. I am confident that many Members would do that.

Q13 Chair: I understand that. I think this is the first time you have been in front of the Committee, and obviously you need to understand our processes so that we can get a balanced debate. The application will go on our list, and if you can supply those extra names to the Clerks, it will enable us to consider it for the Chamber.

Nesil Caliskan: Thank you; that is very helpful. I just wanted you to have confidence that I am confident that there would be cross-party support.

Chair: I am sure there will be. The Clerks will be in touch with you shortly, but please do get in touch with them when you have names.

Nesil Caliskan: Thank you very much.

Samantha Niblett made representations.

Q14 Chair: Last on our list is Samantha Niblett, with an application for a debate on disabled children’s social care in Westminster Hall on a Tuesday or a Thursday.

Samantha Niblett: Thank you, Chair. Disabled children’s social care is simply not working well enough for the families who depend on it. Families rely on respite and short breaks, personal care, specialist equipment and adaptations, yet even when a child has been assessed as needing support, workforce shortages can mean that that help never arrives. Access has also become a postcode lottery, with families facing different thresholds and levels of support depending on where they live. Too often, parents are seeking practical help but encounter an intrusive and complex system that can leave them feeling scrutinised rather than supported.

The Law Commission has proposed a simpler and fairer legal framework, with SEND reform and wider social care changes firmly on the agenda. Now is the right time for Parliament to consider what happens next. A Westminster Hall debate would allow MPs from across the House to bring forward their constituents’ experiences, scrutinise progress on the Law Commission’s recommendations and press the Government for a clear timetable for reform so that disabled children and their families get the rights, support and certainty that they deserve.

Q15 Jonathan Davies: You mentioned in your application that you wanted the Department of Health and Social Care to be the answering Department, but many of these issues sit under the DFE—in fact, the Law Commission review that you mentioned was commissioned by the DFE. Is it definitely DHSC that you want to answer, or would you rather it was DFE?

Samantha Niblett: It is a tough one. I am talking specifically here about disabled children and the social support they get. It could be a toss-up between the two. I am thinking specifically about a young chap in my constituency, Alf, who has cerebral palsy and is desperately trying to get support from Derbyshire county council. The support services there are lacking. It could be either Department, but there is a specific focus on social care.

Chair: Okay. Thank you very much, Samantha. The Clerks will be in touch in due course.

That concludes the public business of the Committee. The Committee will now retire to consider the applications and allocation of time in private.
