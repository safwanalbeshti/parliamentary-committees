Examination of witnesses

Dr Florian Ostmann and Michael Birtwistle.

The Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the International Relations and Defence Committee of the House of Lords and this further session in our discussion and potential report about AI governance in multilateral organisations outside the UN system and international fora. You are very welcome to this meeting today. It is live on parliament.tv. A transcript will be taken of the proceedings, which will be sent to you in case there are any small corrections that might be made to it. Members of the committee will ask questions and should declare any interests they have to declare before doing so. I therefore declare that I am a senior counsellor with the Cohen Group in Washington DC. We are very grateful to you for giving up your time in order to inform us.

To begin the questions, a variety of organisations is already meddling in or discussing the whole question of artificial intelligence. The G7, the OECD and Global Partnership on AI are all involved in the idea of governance, which is what we are particularly looking at. How significant are these organisations within that global governance debate and how do they co‑operate together? Many people say they are just a talking shop, soaking up a lot of time, but not producing very much. Give us your views on how these organisations are dealing with the whole question of governance.

Dr Florian Ostmann: Thank you for the invitation to be here. By way of introduction, my name is Florian Ostmann. I am a distinguished policy fellow in the Data Science Institute at the London School of Economics. I have worked in the AI governance space for about 10 years and was previously at the Alan Turing Institute leading its work on AI governance and regulation. I have also been a member for a number of years of the OECD and the Global Partnership on AI’s community of experts.

With regard to the question, my short answer is that all the organisations and fora you mentioned—the G7, GPAI and OECD—have played a really important role in AI governance, and continue to do so. One point to start with is the fact that there is a lot of co‑ordination between these three fora. Rather than thinking of them as independent actors in a busy landscape, it is worth thinking of them as concentric circles, as it were. Obviously, the G7 brings together seven countries, while the OECD includes all G7 members and an additional range of countries. You may be aware that a couple of years ago the Global Partnership on AI and the OECD entered into an integrated partnership, which means that the Global Partnership on AI in a sense is now a bigger circle comprising OECD members and an additional number of countries. As for memberships, there are subsets of each other. We often see work being initiated in one forum and carried over to a larger one. For example, one of the most significant outputs from the G7 has been the Hiroshima process and the reporting process around advanced AI systems. That was initiated in the G7 and is now taken forward by the OECD.

I also emphasise that, while the AI governance space internationally has become a lot more crowded, all three of these fora were among the first movers, as it were. The work in the OECD on AI started in 2018. Similarly, in the Global Partnership on AI, discussions on this started in 2018. By comparison, the EU AI Act negotiations started three years later. In the G7, we have seen attention to AI matters for a long time in communiqués.

One important feature of the work in all three fora is that, generally, output takes the form of non-binding instruments or outputs. That implies limitations. I want to be clear in expressing the view that there is a need for binding requirements in certain areas of AI governance, but these non-binding instruments play a really important role, first because in some areas non‑binding instruments are sufficient and are the right tool. The fact is that both the OECD and GPAI pursue a mode of working that includes a lot of stakeholder input; they are both characterised by a strong commitment to multistakeholder risk management in how work is developed, and that underpins to some extent the uptake of the outputs, even though they are not binding. There is an important role for binding instruments in their own right, but, in so far as binding rules are needed, this non-binding work can play an important role in providing a foundation, for example, in the case of the OECD, in forming policy-making and regulatory rule-making at a national level and working towards alignment in that. We have seen the OECD AI definition reflected in national policies in a number of jurisdictions, including the EU AI Act and the UK’s approach to AI regulation, but also at international level, in thinking about emerging discussions around transparency requirements and incident reporting requirements. In a sense, the OECD has done important work in developing the frameworks that are now broadly agreed by an important range of stakeholders that can then be adopted and turned into binding requirements.

The Chair: Thank you so much. Mr Birtwistle, thank you for coming at relatively short notice. Would you like to introduce yourself?

Michael Birtwistle: I thank the committee for having me here today. My name is Michael Birtwistle. I am the associate director for law and policy at the Ada Lovelace Institute, which is an independent research institute with a mission to make AI and data work for people and society. We have been a fairly active participant in global fora for a number of years; we have been a participant in some sense at all of the AI summit series, and recently in New York at the UN General Assembly on global dialogue.

I agree with a lot of Florian’s remarks. Our starting point on this question is that it is probably helpful to distinguish what different kinds of contributions you might make to the idea of global governance. Normally, at national level, by “governance” we often mean binding rules, processes and the incentives that follow. This is not a particularly rigorous way of breaking it down, but it might be useful today: you have the normative role that global institutions can play in making recommendations, setting binding rules sometimes, setting terms and standards, and potentially providing venues for legal redress. Around AI, most of those exist in the softer, not harder, form. You have epistemic and knowledge-based roles where you are doing scientific assessments, monitoring and measurements, and technical evaluation of models—and then you have the political and strategic roles where you convene agenda-setting and some of the things Florian referred to. One space and role that has been more challenging for some of these institutions is the operationalising of principles: building, implementation, guidance and tools, disclosure and reporting, regulator co‑operation and capacity building, some of which is in train. Looking at some other comparators that you might have at international level, we do not have a verification or inspection role around AI compared with, say, the IAEA. You do not have an incident response process as you do with CSIRT for cyber, and you do not really have cross-border liability redress enforcement regimes.

The first point is that global governance does not always mean enforcement, so having distributed networks of collaborative AI expert bodies can be very helpful and make a contribution. I agree with Florian that the G7 into OECD process through things like the Hiroshima principles has been a very useful contribution. It also has some limitations. For example, if you are thinking about its role in transparency, the commitments to publish data are useful in theory, but the information is often very heterogeneous and difficult to compare over time, and more work is needed to make sure that that can be usefully consumed, for example, by national regulators so they can improve their understanding of their objective regulation in AI.

My final observation is that the role of those bodies has changed over time. The G7, for example, was much more in a normative space in 2023 and has passed on the baton to OECD for operationalisation. It now plays a much more agenda-setting role, and that conversation has moved more towards things like economic security and resilience. I suppose the roles have changed in response to the geopolitical picture.

The Chair: Do you think that the non-binding or voluntary rules that have been agreed could be the basis for binding rules eventually?

Dr Florian Ostmann: I think they can, in an important way. There are certain examples of work that can be thought of as the building blocks for binding requirements in the sense that they are not a blueprint for how to formulate the regulation, but they provide the element of how things should be done. For example, we have the Hiroshima transparency reporting framework. I agree with the limitations, but it is an important first step that commands broad agreement. If regulators or countries at the international level are thinking about introducing mandatory transparency requirements, that would be a natural starting point for thinking about what those should look like. Similarly, an important area currently is the question of incident reporting. The OECD has done important work on a conceptual framework for what incident reporting should look like. If mandatory incident reporting requirements were introduced, they can draw on that work.

The Chair: Mr Birtwistle, do you agree with that?

Michael Birtwistle: Yes. Processes such as the Hiroshima reporting framework are useful for establishing a floor of agreement around AI transparency and accountability. Obviously, it does not provide the hard incentive mechanisms that are needed to make its addressees comply with the principles. To achieve that will probably require co‑ordinated action internationally and regulation at national level, but I suppose you see the DNA flowing down from some of those agreements into things like the EU AI Act’s code of practice and Senate Bill 53 in California. Some of the wording and consensus on terms and what you are measuring set by those non-binding processes can eventually flow down, but it requires political will at national level to implement it.

Baroness Crawley: You talked about the G7 Hiroshima process and work that the EU is doing. Last month, we had the Dublin declaration on AI. Where do these recommendations come together? How do you get both the G7 and EU’s very recent recommendations coming together to form something? You talked about it trickling down, but there must be more than just hope that it will do so.

Dr Florian Ostmann: One way is to think about the timeline for these different things happening. When the Hiroshima process started, the OECD had already done its work in establishing principles and policy recommendations. Those were developed before generative AI entered the scene. Then there was a sense in the policy community that there was a need for a framework to address the specific risks around frontier models, so it was natural for there to be a sense that something additional was needed. My view of the history is that the G7 provided an opportune forum for that, but then, as we touched on, there was follow-up work and a path to integration. There is no disagreement between the OECD community and G7 that these parts need to be thought of as elements of a whole. In fact, the OECD later updated its principles and policy recommendations.

Similarly, we see calls from a number of countries around controls for frontier AI. That was initiated by Finland and Norway, and there is the declaration you just mentioned. I think they are all reactions to the identified needs in response to further advancements in AI development, so there is a logic behind them being introduced, but there is also reason to assume that over time they will be integrated. I do not think there is any interest from any of the stakeholders introducing those among like-minded partners for that to lead to misalignment, but it is a contribution to building the same edifice, as it were.

Baroness Crawley: We do not know who will lead that integration.

Dr Florian Ostmann: We might come back to that question. Of course, in the G7 and G20 a lot depends on the presidency. The UK has an interesting two years coming up, with the G20 presidency next year and the G7 presidency the year after. Maybe there is an important role there for the UK.

Baroness Blackstone: Could you give the committee an overview of the part played by the UK in discussions on AI in organisations like OECD or bodies like the G7? What particular capacity does it have, technical or other? Does it help shape the agenda? Tell us a bit about its role and how effective it is.

Michael Birtwistle: It has changed over time. The UK did quite a lot in the run-up to 2023. It was a founding member of GPAI and obviously the host at Bletchley, and I believe it co‑ordinated with Japan on the Hiroshima principles. I also believe it is quite an active participant in OECD sub‑committees and the technical work that goes on there. Since about 2025, we have seen a bit of a realignment in its posture to align with the US. It declined to sign the Paris summit declaration in February 2025 at the same time as it renamed the AI Safety Institute the AI Security Institute and narrowed its scope. It framed its future contributions around bilateral relations with the US in terms of the US-UK prosperity deal. Very recently, at the UN General Assembly, it did not sign the middle-powers statement put out by a number of nations.

It has made other contributions. It drove the international AI safety report, up until the UN’s processes were set up to carry on that baton. That was a helpful contribution at many levels. It was not comprehensive, and did not cover some notably contentious AI issues, such as climate, data protection and copyright, but nevertheless it was an important evidential contribution. It endorsed the Delhi declaration. In April of this year, Liz Kendall, the then Secretary of State, gave a speech on how the UK wanted to work with middle powers—France, Germany and Canada—which, in practice, meant closer work on AI standards and joint investment.

I should also mention the enormous technical contribution that the AI Security Institute makes on a narrow set of matters—extreme risk and understanding the propensity for extreme risk with AI models—but there is a disconnect between the Government’s publicly stated ambitions, in the way they say they want to work with middle powers, and what they have been signalling during their participation in these global fora to date.

Dr Florian Ostmann: I agree with that overview and assessment. I mentioned the middle-powers statement, which was initiated by Norway and Finland. It is noteworthy that the UK decided in the end not to sign that, but there is a history of engagement. From what I know and have witnessed, the UK Government are very active at the table in the OECD and G7. The question is the extent to which that translates into decisions and outcomes that push the boundary and advance progress. As Michael said, certain geopolitical factors have become more prominent in the past two years.

I will slightly expand on the role of the AI Security Institute. The UK provided a proof of concept there, and has the strongest institute of that kind by far. It plays an international leadership role and is also the custodian of the network of similar institutes around the world, which I believe includes 10 countries. That is an interesting point to add, in the sense that that coalition of countries is not identical to any of the groups we discussed. It is smaller than the OECD and wider than the G7, but it is an important coalition. It is focused on technical understanding and advancing safety research rather than rule-making, but of course that has a really important role to play in informing decisions about rule-making and governance.

Setting aside the role of the UK Government in international fora, it is worth recognising that there is a history and strong record of UK experts and non-government stakeholders being very actively involved in making important contributions both in the OECD and GPAI. For example, UK experts have been prominent members, including as chairs of working groups. UK experts have also been involved in other international contexts, such as European standard-setting bodies that developed the standards to support implementation of the EU AI Act. Some UK experts have played a leading role in developing the code of conduct or code of practice for general purpose AI models in the EU. In terms of broader UK expertise and thinking beyond government, there is an important history and line of continuity.

Lord Grocott: You have covered a good deal of my question already, thank you. The picture that emerges from the evidence we have had so far is very much of a myriad of different international and national organisations all looking at matters connected with the governance of AI, with little co‑ordination as far as anyone can see. One of our earlier witnesses said it was pretty tricky to expect agreement on the answers when there is not even agreement on the questions.

To distil this, where might be the most productive venue for discussions of this sort and maybe decisions to be made? Is it at the level of the UN, which seems to have a pretty fundamental problem in that the two superpowers, the US and China, are at different ends of the spectrum as far as governance of AI is concerned? Or is trying to find some kind of co‑ordination and agreement best done to an extent through like-minded organisations such as the G7? Where could we as a country most productively place our priorities?

Dr Florian Ostmann: My general answer would be not to think about it as a matter of choosing between these different fora, but to recognise that they all have a role to play. We should not think of AI governance as being guided by the idea of ideally having a single forum where all matters of AI governance are settled or agreed, let alone a single international forum that includes all countries. Depending on the AI governance problem, different stakeholders and a range of countries need to be at the table. Currently, at the sharp end of frontier AI risks, it is clear we need an agreement that includes China and the US. Potentially, there is a lot to be got out of an agreement that focuses just on China and the US. There are other matters where broader agreement is needed. There is also the point that agreements reached in smaller fora can, over time, find their way into work that is happening in broader fora like the United Nations. For example, the OECD’s work informed debates in the United Nations.

Having said that, fora like the OECD have a number of advantages. The first is the pace at which dialogue can advance and, associated with that, the ease of finding agreement as a result of a smaller number of countries being there and them having a similar policy outlook. There is the fact that the OECD has a strong mechanism for engaging non-government stakeholders, which I think strengthens the work. Also, the OECD—this may be a contrast to G7—has an institutional structure that allows work to continue over time and is not dependent on, for example, changing presidency. The OECD is not necessarily the only body of that kind, but organisations that have those features lend themselves particularly well to thinking about operationalising or implementation, moving from principles to practical questions.

Going back to what I started with, a clear limitation of the OECD is the fact that it does not include China or ‘Global South’ perspectives. Clearly, there are AI governance areas where the inclusion of both of those, or one of them, is critical. I mentioned that US and Chinese agreement is critical. That does not mean those discussions must happen at UN level, because discussions there move more slowly, simply because of the number of countries involved. There is a need for dialogue that involves the US and China. The urgency of some of the questions is such that that will not happen in the UN but needs to happen in a different forum. Again, perhaps the G20 is the forum to look at for that purpose, among others. The UK’s role as the G20 chair next year might be an opportunity to do that.

Michael Birtwistle: I am thinking what I can add usefully, because I agree with a lot of that. It is very much the case that the most productive venue depends on which problem you are trying to solve. You have a fairly tight coupling at the moment with questions of mitigation of frontier AI risks, which is often where the conversation about global governance goes, but a lot of these fora are also talking about dependency, sovereignty and the strategic risks to countries being dependent on technology controlled largely by another country. That will inform, in part, which of these venues is most useful.

The other matter that might help is thinking about what problems in the AI governance space require an international solution. We ran a workshop at the UN General Assembly as a fringe event to highlight some of the things that fell out—basically, things that do not work if you do not do them at an international level. You have questions such as cross-border externality. If a harm in one jurisdiction falls on people in another who have no say, does the harm-causing actor sit outside the jurisdictional reach of where the harm lands? There are regulatory arbitrage questions about whether uneven protection in one place means that you push everyone towards the weakest regime? I can write to the committee with the full framework, but that gives you a sense of a discriminator to decide which of these forums, given the properties that Florian talked about, are well set up to solve those issues. Some are more tightly focused on solution spaces, be they technical standards, developing evaluation frameworks or so on, that are actually able to understand and address those risks more directly.

Lord Bruce of Bennachie: What we are trying to achieve in governance is minimising risks while maximising opportunities and protecting sovereignty. Different players will have different emphasis on that. To what extent should countries broaden participation to try to have it as wide as possible and take account of the wider range of interests? Alternatively, should they concentrate on those countries that have the most expertise and are best able to come up with the best solutions—in theory, if maybe not in practice? The global partnership has been expanding, but I notice from the list that I have at the moment it has only one African member: Senegal. The AU has its own initiative on AI, and there is a South African-based organisation. Africa has an interest in this, but is not involved.

To repeat, to what extent is it appropriate for the expert countries to lead the way and co-operate, or to broaden it out to ensure that all the wider range of interests are covered? Indeed, are those mutually exclusive given there are limited resources? You said you participate in all these various organisations, but only so many people can attend so many conferences so many times, so it is a constraint.

Michael Birtwistle: There are very good arguments for broadening participation. I am conscious that we are not focusing on the UN today, but there are very good arguments for participating in the UN processes. Most of the fora that we have been talking about today represent only a fraction of the world. The UN has the international human rights law mandate. It also carries the mandate for international peace and security. It has 193 member states versus 46 for GPAI. We are at the stage where the question of how we ensure AI produces the outcomes that we want is getting very highly technical. Understanding how AI systems work, why they produce particular outcomes and having visibility of that will increasingly sit in technical processes and evaluations of them. The forums in which those sorts of things get decided have historically been relatively narrow and seen very high representation rates from the companies that those processes are seeking to set the evaluation terms for. You are potentially moving from a model where the companies, in safety terms, are marking their own homework to one where they are writing their own exams.

There are other reasons. There is this idea of cross-border impact, and the statement yesterday that France, Canada and a bunch of the companies signed up to explicitly acknowledges the idea that AI impacts are cross border. One way in which that can manifest is you could have a South African foundation model that is fine-tuned to the local language, and the safeguards put in place around refusals to build weapons would be demonstrably, measurably worse. If you do not include all the countries that have an interest in building sovereign models, there is increased risk across the system in that sense.

If one of the goals that countries like the UK might pursue is some form of sovereignty, you need viable alternatives to a lot of the current AI stack, and that means building with trusted partners that want to avoid the risks of rent extraction and strategic dependency. Open source is about nine to 12 months behind the frontier. That might even be an AISI stat. For some things like cyber, it may always be the case that you need the frontier, but for many other purposes open-source fast follow will be a viable strategy for countries to pursue, and that broadens the pool of people with whom it makes sense to collaborate.

Dr Florian Ostmann: I agree with everything that Michael shared there. Going back to some of my earlier comments, the answer depends on the issue that we are trying to solve. Should we try to broaden the circle, or focus on a narrower circle?

Thinking about the narrower circle, we started the session talking about fora that bring together like-minded countries in the traditional sense, the G7 or the OECD as a larger community. If we think about the reasons why Governments might look at working with smaller circles of countries, it is worth reflecting on the fact that those are not necessarily circles of like-minded countries in that traditional sense. As we touched on, there is a need in certain areas for dialogue and agreement that involve China and the US. Those two parties are critical. You will not get that through the OECD or the G7. That may well mean that, rather than looking for that agreement to happen at the UN level, there is a need for a smaller forum or venue for those discussions.

Increasingly, there is also the question around the role of middle powers as a group, which is again separate from the fora that we touched on. We touched on the call for control for frontier AI as a middle-power initiative. There is an important question of the role that middle powers as a group coming together can play in acting as a catalyst and maybe also to some extent pushing the US and China to come together for the discussions that need to happen in the frontier AI space. Diplomatically, there is a delicate balance there. If you think about the G20 as a forum that includes both countries and a range of middle powers, in terms of agenda setting, to what extent is there an opportunity to highlight those questions and try to make progress?

There is an interesting dynamic at the moment on the US side in the sense that the US Government are more hesitant to engage in those discussions than US companies. That also provides an opportunity and, going back to an earlier point, highlights the importance of a multi-stakeholder approach in those discussions, rather than relying on fora that purely bring together government partners, which then may mean that discussions do not progress or are shut down at an early point. Having fora that include industry voices in this case, and of course civil society voices, can make a difference in terms of what the agenda looks like and how far discussions may advance.

Baroness Crawley: You just talked about the US. My question is about the influence of the US on the future agenda for AI. Given the US’s leading role in AI—and that is not to be disputed—how dependent are these very important international and minilateral initiatives on US participation? Perhaps, if you have a judgment, you can talk about whether the midterm results in Congress and the Senate would make a plausible difference in the US’s influence on the future of AI.

Dr Florian Ostmann: Starting with the last point, which is an interesting one, it is too early to say to what extent the midterm results will have an impact on the current Administration’s position. It is clear that there is a strong contrast between the current Administration’s position up to this point and popular opinion in the US. If you look at popular opinion, there is strong support for AI regulation across a number of areas. Coupled with that, there is also the fact that, as I mentioned, US industry and, in particular, the leading AI labs are calling for stronger forms of governance and regulation. They are also, in a sense, in contrast to the federal Government’s view. Given that constellation, it would not be surprising if there is a change in the Administration’s position, or at least a change in position with the next Administration.

Without doubt, there is a dependence on US involvement in the fora that we have talked about. The extent of US engagement makes a difference. In terms of the exact impact, it is hard to answer that at a general level. There is a distinction between existing work continuing and new work starting. New work starting often is more dependent on parties agreeing to it, but even that depends on detail. Not all new work being started is dependent on consensus. It may be that the US Government are less reluctant to support initiatives that are non-binding, as they have been in the past. In fora such as the OECD, I do not think there is US support at the moment for binding measures at the international level. There is a hope at least that that will change on the frontier end of risks, as well as the cybersecurity-related risks that are relevant to national security. If you think about AI governance more broadly, it is clear that with the current US Administration there will not be an appetite for binding rules. In so far as work continues and new work begins in the fora that we have been talking about, it will continue to be in a non-binding domain.

As I mentioned at the beginning, that does not mean that the work is not meaningful or does not have impact. The leading AI labs in the US are taking a stance, are supportive and are calling for greater governance measures. The fact that measures are voluntary may not make that much of a difference for the time being. All major AI labs in the US are participating in the Hiroshima AI Process Reporting Framework even though it is a voluntary initiative.

Michael Birtwistle: On the question of the midterms, the only thing I note is that there has been some interesting analysis of how AI generally and data centres in particular appeared to be a factor in voting patterns. That might well be something that shapes political discourse domestically in the US and therefore where the Administration decide to go. I agree that it is honestly a very difficult problem to route around if the US continues to be reluctant to engage. That explains some of the focus on standards. The development of standards has historically been a forum in which opposed countries can co-operate more. There is less normative or political charge to the debate on that. There is some leverage in the market power, procurement, investment and industrial strategy behaviours of large economies in this and in those interested in developing alternatives. If that economic demand signal starts being driven towards safe alternatives, perhaps sovereign alternatives, that may also change the behaviour of the companies or the US Administration, because it will be clear that that is where the market is moving.

Dr Florian Ostmann: Starting with the last point, I am aware that the question of lethal autonomous weapons is a topic that has come up in previous sessions of the committee. There is a question of risks associated with technical design of systems and, correspondingly, questions about technical solutions to those. Then there is the view that fully autonomous weapons should be prohibited on the basis of the risks that may be associated or as a matter of principle, because we think in military decisions there should always be a human who is accountable. That has been driving a lot of the UN debate—the question about the autonomy of weapons. If the view is that we need to ensure that there is human involvement in decision-making, we need to think about both the human-in-the-loop question and the technical layer. At the technical layer, as I am sure you are aware, there are concerns around the loss of control over systems as they become more advanced. Michael Birtwistle: I am trying to think of what I can add to that, because we do not study laws at Ada. Dr Florian Ostmann: Part of what you are getting to in your question is that when it comes to human involvement we cannot think of a human being in the loop as a purely formal requirement and that being sufficient. There are crucial questions around the competence of the human in the loop. In the absence of the competence or the ability to make informed decisions with sufficient understanding of the system, a human either interfering or deciding one way or the other, even though it is well intentioned, might lead to worse outcomes than a human not being involved. There are related aspects. The fact that we need to think about competence in humans being involved is well established in the field of responsible AI and applies to the use of AI in any domain. Of course, the stakes are particularly high in the military domain.

Michael Birtwistle: There are very interesting parallels from the Data (Use and Access) Act that passed through this House last year, and some of the amendments laid to that in the section on automated decision-making, some of which have sort of been reflected in the ICO guidance since. The Chair: Apart from autonomous weapons, which you have dealt with, can you be more specific about the areas that need to have a multilateral or international approach to them in terms of regulation? Michael Birtwistle: Certainly, there is frontier model risk. We have published previously on the concept of a global governance floor, which we sort of break down as having three elements. One is substantive content on which you agree as nations: what AI must and must not do, what protections need to exist everywhere regardless of jurisdiction, and what capacities and enablers are needed to do that. Dr Florian Ostmann: I will add a couple more specific aspects on the question of frontier AI risks. To think about the current landscape of policy proposals that fall into the category of things that require broad international agreement and certainly agreement between the leading AI nations, one is the need for red lines: an agreed prohibition on the release of models that have certain capabilities that pose unacceptable risks, and associated with that an evaluation ecosystem. There is some progress, with Anthropic and OpenAI now having committed to having external evaluators, but we need to get that right. There is a question of what kinds of access external evaluators will have and whether the results, the findings, will be meaningful and reliable enough. In order to ensure that and that evaluation results in different countries are comparable, they need to be put on a regulatory footing. So, we need agreed red lines that certain things that are considered unacceptable, up to the point where those capabilities can be managed and the risks mitigated.

Another more ambitious but equally important agenda in the policy space—Michael alluded to this earlier—is the need to shift the burden of proof for systems to be safe on to developers. At the moment, we are dealing with a narrative. Look at the OpenAI incident. The point is often made that, if you compare what happened there with what might happen in other industries or in a non-AI space, clearly that would have had legal ramifications. Evaluation needs to be the first step. Red lines need to be the first step. In order to have the guarantees that we need for severe frontier risks not to occur or materialise, we need a regime that puts an expectation on developers to establish and provide guarantees of the safety. They thereby take on the burden of proof, rather than our relying on other parties or a good faith effort in dealing with systems that we ultimately do not understand.

Michael Birtwistle: To very quickly build on that, there are two categories when we talk about frontier model risk. Even when you are talking about last year’s model, technically not the frontier any more, the safety of those things is important because they are built upon within, for example, the UK so heavily.
