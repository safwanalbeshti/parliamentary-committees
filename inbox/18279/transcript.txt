Witnesses: John Healey MP, Dharmesh Nayee and James Bowler.

Chair: Welcome to the Treasury Select Committee on Wednesday 11 November 2026. We are delighted to welcome the right hon. John Healey MP, Chancellor of the Exchequer, to explain his Budget of two weeks ago. First of all, Chancellor, may I congratulate you on your appointment? John Healey: Thank you.

Chair: The Chancellor is joined by James Bowler, the permanent secretary to the Treasury, and by Dharmesh Nayee, the director of strategy, planning and budget at the Treasury—the man who puts the Budget together in detail, under the guidance of the Chancellor.

Before we go into our main session, a really important issue arose on Budget day itself that was outside your Budget statement, Chancellor. The Treasury has signed up to not giving out specific individual tax rates, but Bloomberg knew very specific information: that the change to the bank tax was going to be temporary and less than 8%. That was a very specific tax leak. Mr Bowler, you have been permanent secretary for a while; was the leak of information about bank taxes the worst type of breach you can think of, given that it related both to the duration and to the level of a single tax on the listed banks?

James Bowler: Bloomberg had the shape of the bank surcharge, as you say, but not the exact detail—they had that it was less than something and temporary, but not the exact measure. That is deeply regrettable and frustrating, and the right thing to do is to have a proper investigation into how that came about.

Chair: We appreciate your letter on that.

James Bowler: Yes, I wrote to you on that. In answer to your question, it is important that we can assure you and the Committee that Budget security more generally has improved very significantly.

Chair: We will come on to that, but do you think this is the worst type of breach that could happen?

James Bowler: No, it is not the worst, and it does not compare to the situation we had last year.

Chair: But it was undeniably inside information.

James Bowler: It was undeniably that—it is certainly the case that it appeared that the markets moved a little bit on the release of that information, hence why we are taking it so seriously.

Chair: We have gone round this a few times, but this time we wrote to the FCA as well as to you, and the FCA waits for you to do your inquiry before perhaps looking into it. It seems that the Treasury is sometimes rather reluctant to involve the FCA in these types of issue. Are you afraid of its powers, and of what might happen in the Treasury if it were to investigate?

James Bowler: Categorically not. We work very closely with the FCA, we speak to them about this, and we will share the outcome of the investigation with them in full. We are not afraid to do that, and I am sure we will come on to it. Although this instance is deeply frustrating, I do think that our security around the Budget improved significantly at this event.

Chair: It was notable that you talk in your letter about not having briefings in advance of the Budget, but that just before the Budget came out you widened the circle, within the Treasury, of who had information. The story ran at 10.45 am, just a couple of hours, nearly, before the Chancellor stood up. Joining the dots, the information seemed to come out at around the time that that circle would have widened and more people knew. Is that a fair summary?

James Bowler: Yes. What I said to you in my letter is that we now have market-sensitive information in the Budget in very tight compartments of very limited numbers, and we hold them at that. By necessity, and as a factual description of how a Budget is put together, those compartments have to come into the Budget documentation, the distributional analysis and other things, and that happens in the last 48 to 24 hours. By necessity, that widens as we move from holding that information extremely tightly to being able to deliver a Budget for Parliament on time. That is what I was referring to there.

Chair: At that point you widen it out; do you have a list of the names of those people?

James Bowler: Yes, we do. I do not want to give the impression that you let go of any security; it is quite the opposite. Any Budget information handling remains very sensitive and on a named-person basis. We have rules right up to the delivery, when the Chancellor stands up, including avoiding the situation we had last year, when the OBR Budget was pre-released. We had to deal with that, which we did successfully this year.

Chair: Will you look at the communications of all those individuals both before and after—

James Bowler: Yes, I have asked the Government Security Group in the Cabinet Office to do just that. I commission them and then they do it.

Chair: Do you instruct them? Do you know whether they will look at everyone’s individual phone records, both landlines and mobiles?

James Bowler: I have and will be talking to them about that. They ask me who would have been aware, I tell them and then they investigate.

Chair: Do you know whether they will look at phone records?

James Bowler: I do not know exactly what they intend to do. We have obviously been through this before. It is important that I do not give a running commentary. That is their modus operandi.

John Healey: Dame Meg, can I—

Chair: I will come to you in a moment, Chancellor. Before I do, permanent secretary, do you think there is any chance that it could be coincidence that Bloomberg knew both that the tax on the banks was temporary—which was new information to everybody at that point—and that it was less than the 8% that had been floated and mooted in the sector? Could that possibly be coincidence?

James Bowler: Coincidence? I think that is unlikely.

Chair: Chancellor, you were very public about making sure there was very tight security around the Budget. The Committee would agree that that was a welcome move, especially after what happened last year. How frustrated are you that this information leaked out?

John Healey: First, I welcome coming in, Dame Meg, because this is not just a matter for the permanent secretary. You have released the exchange of letters, so you know the action that is being taken. You know that the Government Security Group is investigating this. I will ensure that the results of that investigation are shared with the Committee, which is quite rightly taking an interest in this.

This could not be more serious or more important for me as Chancellor. It is not just that any leak of Budget information is serious; it is that there are real-world impacts, not just moving the markets or the gilt yields. Last year, with the speculation, kite flying, leaks and briefings, we saw people cashing in their pension pots when they did not need to. We saw people cancelling and changing investment plans when they did not need to. It could not have been more serious.

I was really determined as Chancellor, coming in to lead this Budget, that we introduced not just the recommendations from the review that James Bowler led after the last Budget, which concluded in February, but what I would describe as national security-style information controls. These were steps that had not been taken before in relation to the Budget, including methods of handling information; digital security; personal signing and undertaking, so that people in the compartments understood the requirements on them; and the automatic watermarking of documents. Those sorts of steps have allowed us, quite properly, to conduct a tighter control of the Budget process, reduce that sort of speculation and reduce the impact we have seen, not just last year but in previous years.

Chair: We have seen less come out, but do you agree with the permanent secretary that this was a less serious leak than last year?

John Healey: I am dismayed that it happened at all. It is absolutely right that it is fully investigated. The Committee will be briefed on the results of the investigation. Frankly, I do not want any leaks out of the Budget process. I take them all seriously, particularly if they are market-moving in this respect. As the permanent secretary said, this leak was at a late stage, on Budget day. Some Committee members have been involved in Budget processes, and will understand the way that, even with restrictions, the circle of people who must be briefed and know—the permanent secretary has indicated some of the ways that that was done on Budget day itself—inevitably broadens and the risk increases, but we want to try to get to the bottom of this.

Chair: I have been around the block on leak inquiries, as have you, the permanent secretary and members of the Committee. They never find the person who did it; do you think you are really going to find someone this time?

James Bowler: That’s not true.

John Healey: Absolutely—that is not always true.

Chair: More often than not, but okay. Do you think we will find who leaked this? Is that your intention, Chancellor?

John Healey: I don’t know. I would certainly hope so. It is hard. If anybody can get to the bottom of it, it will be the Government Security Group. It has not just the permanent secretary of the Treasury but the Cabinet Secretary behind this, so it has, if you like, the full weight of the investigative system, reinforced by the seriousness with which I and the Prime Minister take this. We will see and we will report back to you.

Chair: One of the challenges that we have discussed before with the permanent secretary is that on the one hand you want to keep everything tight, and you did that with military precision this time around—that was the aim—and on the other hand if you are not testing things with the sector, things can come as a surprise and can be untested. How do you intend to try to balance that in future?

John Healey: I think we did. Not everybody needs to know everything. At the heart of it was a very tight compartment: it was, essentially, the core decision-taking between me and the Prime Minister, and the very small number of officials who were privy to that compartment. The compartment that was part of the scorecard core of the Budget process was also tight. Clearly there are a wider range of policy officials, in discussion with externals, who develop some of the policy proposals, but that is very different from being privy to the information on which and about which decisions are taken in the Budget process.

Chair: You met with the banks probably a couple of weeks before the Budget. At that point, did you or any of your officials discuss options around a temporary bank surcharge uplift?

John Healey: No. That was not and—as you would expect, I think—would not have been the only occasion that, in the less than four months I have been Chancellor, I have met with, in this case, banks and financial sector leaders. It is part of the job.

Chair: One of the challenges is that when you then have to explain to people what is in the Budget, more people are involved. Do you think there is a chance that someone just overstepped by trying to give some hint about what was going on, and did that inadvertently? I say “inadvertently”; it is still very serious. Do you have any assumptions on that?

John Healey: To be honest, I really do not want to speculate on that, and I do not feel I can. This was not a straightforward case of information going from Government and into the hands of journalists for publication. Journalists have a job to do. They dig around; they put stories together. Sometimes those reports are not entirely accurate; sometimes those reports are based on pieces of information. They are doing their job. Sometimes it is quite hard to pin down specifically whether leaks or briefings are unlawful, and out of order, and those that are constructed stories based on bits of information. This warrants the investigation that has been launched into it. It is specific and, if I may say so, as a Committee you are quite right to focus so strongly on it.

Chair: Thank you. We look forward to having that shared with us in due course. Do you have any idea, permanent secretary, of the timescale?

James Bowler: No, I don’t.

Chair: If you can keep us informed on that, well that would be very helpful.

James Bowler: Will do.

Chair: I should say for the record that if any Committee members are on their phones, we sometimes use that as a method of quick communication in meetings. We do not do that for any other reason. Forgive us if there are moments when we pick up our phone for a quick exchange.

John Healey: For your reassurance, Dame Meg, I have left my phone out of the room.

Chair: I am glad to know that you are fully focused on the questions in front of you, Chancellor.

John Glen: First, Chancellor, I congratulate you on securing your position after so many years in the House.

John Healey: Thank you; that is very kind.

John Glen: Can I turn to the Budget measures on economic growth? Helen Miller from the IFS was very clear with us that of the 98 measures in the Budget, the OBR’s economic and fiscal outlook indicates that it found no measures that would move the dial on growth. I do not want to get into speculations on the growth forecasts of different organisations—the IMF, OBR and so on—being up or down this year or next year. What measures in your Budget do you think will move the dial on growth, given that none of them were scored?

John Healey: I can, Mr Glen, and I certainly will. If you permit me at this point, I also want to set out the context of the Budget. I can do that very briefly, but then I can come to the four measures that will certainly help to drive growth, with specifics.

I went into the Budget process, less than four months ago, with really deep concerns about the global economic storms that we faced, which had not been reflected in the OBR’s spring forecast. Now, the OBR and the Bank of England are really clear that conflict in the middle east is driving rising energy costs, driving debt increase costs and weakening economic growth. That is hitting nations worldwide, but it is hitting us particularly hard in this country. Ending the conflict in the middle east would be the single best economic policy for Britain. That is why diplomacy and pressure on Iran is a whole-of-Government drive, including for me as the Chancellor.

Given the continuing conflict with Iran, the crisis in global energy prices and the huge upheaval in Government bond markets, I took the view that Britain needed a breathing-space Budget, so that is what I delivered. It was action to help with cost of living and cost of business pressures, but also action that starts to make the deeper changes necessary in this country on energy, AI, devolution, housing, defence and national security.

Despite the global shocks—I am coming to your point about growth, Mr Glen—the UK economy has shown real resilience during this period of uncertainty and conflict, and has done since the 2024 election. Government investment is up, business investment is up, productivity is up and real wages are up. All that has been underpinned by a strong fiscal discipline, which has led the IMF to say that Britain stands out among the G7 for our deficit-reduction plans and performance, which I continued in the Budget.

In summary, I am proud that Britain has beaten the growth forecasts for this year. The growth measures in the Budget mean that I aim to see us beat the forecasts again next year. They are, if you like, a boost to business investment and innovation. They are a backing of business founders and fastest-growth firms, and they are the greater confidence to plan for the future. To reinforce that, for example, the announcement today from Fractile, which is a UK-based, UK-started AI chip firm, has been a £500 million funding round, with a contribution from the British Business Bank and from sovereign AI. That brings the valuation of that British-based and British-started company to $7 billion. This funding round is the largest ever European semiconductor funding round here in the UK.

John Glen: I of course acknowledge that as a wonderful success story. Over many years, we have seen that the UK has led in the tech sector in terms of unicorns. I still come back to my question, though, which is that although you have set out the overall meta-narrative, if you like, there were no measures in the Budget that were scored as having a material impact on growth.

You alluded to this, but let me draw you into the issue of founders’ relief. Helen Miller from the IFS was uncharacteristically and passionately concerned—scathing is one word that may be used—about it in terms of the distortions, cliff edges and so on. The Finance Bill published last Wednesday does not contain founders’ relief. Obviously, we know the process, and things could come forward later. Are you concerned about the distortions that may occur—in respect of the rules around the number of years to reach certain milestones, the number of shares to be held, turnover, the number of employees and so on—and that it will not actually have the therapeutic effect that you think it ought to have?

John Healey: No, I am not concerned; otherwise, I would not have introduced it and made the decision. Secondly, I am not concerned because it comes off the back of a consultation on broader tax support that could be offered to support enterprise in this country. Thirdly, I am not concerned because we are doing a rapid technical piece of work, from now until the end of this month, in order to devise the fine details, and we will introduce that into the Finance Bill.

I have a lot of respect for Helen Miller but, to be quite honest, I look more to those who are in the field. For instance, I look to the Startup Coalition, whose executive director looked at the Budget as a whole and at the founders’ relief and said: “The chancellor has delivered the boldest action backing startups and scaleups of any government in years.”

John Glen: I recall the emphasis on finding a suitable quote from a relevant agency in the run-up to a Budget—

John Healey: I’ve got more if you would like them, Mr Glen.

John Glen: Specifically on the CGT carve-out—the 10% relief up to £10 million—is there not a risk conceptually that you will end up encouraging people to sell out rather than to grow their businesses? How would you address that concern?

John Healey: For successful and rapidly growing business, and for the sort of founders who are generally leading these firms, the reinvestment in success is itself incentive for many. What we are looking for with this is to recognise and reward that, with, as you say, the 10% CGT relief and the £10 million lifetime limit, and to encourage them to then reinvest here in Britain. What they have done successfully with one company, we want them to go on and do again with others here.

John Glen: Do you think the VCT change, from 30% relief to 20% relief, has helped in that regard? You inherited that; did you look at reversing it as you did with the ISA allowance?

John Healey: You will remember from the Budget process yourself, Mr Glen, that absolutely everything is in the frame in the early days.

John Glen: So yes.

John Healey: A combination of fiscal constraints and policy choices and priorities pretty rapidly narrows things down. I really wanted us to signal to this particular group, the fast-growth firms and the business founders responsible for them, that we recognise the huge heavy lifting that they are doing in the economy at the moment and the massive potential they have got, so the backing of the founders’ relief in combination with the changes in the EMI system and the range of different enterprise-linked measures in the Budget will help them when taken together.

To finish where you started, yes of course I understand the way that people tend to look at the scorecard as the measure of any Budget. It is correct to say that the OBR in the Budget measures that I set out did not choose to quantify a register to the scorecard, but I am not terribly worried about that because in the months ahead and in the next year or two, I believe that the measures on enterprise, devolution, energy and AI will start to feed through and we will see that impact on growth.

In the end, yes of course the formal figures and the forecasts matter, but actually it is also the business confidence and sentiment of investors in this country that matters. If their belief in Britain and the resilience that they have shown over the past year to beat the forecasts of the expert bodies is reinforced, as I hope it is, by the Budget and not undermined by the risk of renewed or continuing conflict in the middle east, then Britain is very well placed.

As you quite rightly said, we are the most innovative country in Europe and we have a great deal going for us. Part of the responsibility for Governments in particular, and for us as policy makers, is to recognise that and reinforce it.

Ms Minns: Chancellor, the Government are quite clear that they want growth in every postcode. A quarter of postcodes are in rural areas. What Budget measures were specifically intended to increase productivity and private investment in rural economies?

John Healey: You heard me say earlier, in general terms, that there are Budget measures to boost investment and innovation. That isn’t geography-specific. If you look at the increased capitalisation of the National Wealth Fund and the increased support for the British Business Bank, those are UK-wide. Indeed, new investment was confirmed today in big offshore wind projects off Norfolk.

The East and West Vanguard projects will receive £12 billion of new renewable energy investment, with £1 billion of that coming from the National Wealth Fund. Those are the sorts of investments that will, I think, help lift and help generate growth in more places around the UK. That is the purpose behind the declaration that we want to see growth in every postcode.

Ms Minns: So you do think that a quarter of those postcodes in rural areas are going to see growth from Budget measures. I have to say that when I met my local CLA the other week, they were very sceptical that there was anything in there for their members.

John Healey: The Budget measures are designed to try to boost the fast-growth parts with the greatest potential that we have in Britian. That is not confined to city regional areas. There is a particular role for elected mayors that is reinforced in the Budget. If people in rural areas are looking for very specific things in there, the £2 cap on bus fares, which is coming in January, will—and I know this from having spent a lot of my early years in rural North Yorkshire—be invaluable to many of them. But if you want a rural area audit, perhaps that is a bit of supplementary work that I need to provide to you and the Committee.

Chair: That sounds like a near promise, Ms Minns. Well done.

Dame Siobhain McDonagh: Chancellor, you previously backed the expansion of Heathrow as an important driver for economic growth. It is an expansion that could bring £33 billion of private investment. What assessment has the Treasury made of the economic benefits of a third runway, and how quickly do you expect those benefits to materialise?

John Healey: The Government are behind a third runway at Heathrow, and I am part of the Government. Our experience in recent decades is that successful airports are very often an engine for economic growth in the wider area. That is as true of Manchester as it is of parts of London and elsewhere. In terms of the assessment following the consultation period, that is led not by the Treasury, but by the Department for Transport. The Government are working through the responses and the further update information we have from that.

Dame Siobhain McDonagh: Are you as frustrated as I am at how long these things take, while international airports continue to expand? Are you concerned that further delays risk Britain losing investment, trade and international connectivity to competing European airports? Would you like to meet me and my cement mixer down at the third runway so that we can finally get it laid?

John Healey: Will there also be the bulldozer that Boris Johnson rode on next to your cement mixer?

Chair: Chancellor, you have an offer from Dame Siobhain.

John Glen: I thought you were going to ban second jobs!

John Healey: You asked me a direct question. Yes, I am frustrated, and not just about the past four months in this job. I was in Government before, Dame Siobhain, and you will remember that I was responsible for, and took through Parliament, the national Planning Act in 2008. That was designed to try to deal with, for the first time, Britain’s deep-seated problem of delays in infrastructure projects. It produced a number of the important elements that are part of the system now. Yes, it still takes us too long, but I think you have seen over the past couple of years a determination from this Government to do what we can to speed it up.

Chair: Dame Siobhain and her cement mixer might even speed it up a bit more.

Bobby Dean: Chancellor, the Resolution Foundation described the forecast for living standards in the Budget as “catastrophic”. It is set to be the worst decade on record, achieving just a 10th of the growth achieved in the previous worst decade, the 2010s. We have also received further analysis from the Resolution Foundation that says that if you strip out pensioners from working-age families, the story is even worse. Do you accept that the Budget did nothing meaningful to address that trend?

John Healey: No, I do not, but you are right to point to the Resolution Foundation’s view; you are also right to point to the fact that the last Parliament until 2024 was the first ever in which living standards fell.

I pointed out in my opening remarks that real wages have risen in the last couple of years, but there is no doubt that—this is reflected in the figures in the OBR’s economic forecast—the impact of the conflict in the middle east, particularly on inflation, is now putting that under pressure. That is one of the reasons for some of the measures in the Budget—the measures that recognise the pressure on households, in particular on energy costs. That is why I introduced the £100 direct payment into people’s bank accounts for 20 million households across England.

That sits alongside the extension of the VAT cut from electricity bills, the extension of the 5p cut in fuel duty and the removal next year of the gas standing charges from people’s gas bills. There are steps to help relieve the pressure on inflation and, importantly, to try to give a bit of breathing space to households, given what they are facing.

Bobby Dean: But all those measures are taken into account in this forecast. We asked the OBR directly about the war in Iran; you rightly said that ending the conflict would be the single best economic policy. But the OBR already assumes in its forecast that the war will come to an end next year, yet we are still on track for the worst fall in living standards. What else do you think is driving that? What else could you do about it in future Budgets?

John Healey: This Committee, and you, Mr Dean, probably understand this better than any. In the long run, people’s living standards will be lifted by a stronger economy and stronger growth. I hope we will come back to this. Take energy: yes, there was action in the Budget with the £100 household energy direct payment and on other fronts. But more importantly for the long term, there was a confirmation that from this point cheaper energy—not just cleaner energy—is going to be the priority for the Government. There will be an end next year not just to standing charges on gas bills but an immediate end to the practice that we have seen for too long, certainly under previous Governments: of Government policy costs being loaded as levies on to consumer bills. That is finished; I have scrapped the next scheduled one of those—the gas shipper obligation.

Bobby Dean: Chancellor—

John Healey: You asked me about living standards, the costs that people face and the action in the Budget, and I am spelling it out to you.

Bobby Dean: We understand those measures.

John Healey: The other thing is that we will now back renewable energy, which is part of the way we get cheaper and cleaner energy, but we will do that only where it can be connected to the grid and only where it will also bring cheaper energy as well as cleaner energy.

Bobby Dean: I accept that. Could I move on to two choices that you could have made that have been put forward by the evidence that we have heard so far? The Resolution Foundation, again, noted that departmental budgets were topped up by £9 billion. If you look at the cost of living measures, you see that the amount is about half of that—£4.4 billion. Can you explain why you decided to make the choice to prioritise departmental spending over easing the cost of living?

John Healey: It was a Budget that first of all made sure that all policy choices were fully funded over the forecast period, as part of the fiscal discipline that I promised on day one as Chancellor and as part in the end of continuing the task of bringing down borrowing costs and meeting the fiscal rules, which this Budget does and I do. It is also a reflection of the important mix of priorities that are captured in the Budget. Nevertheless, it was also a Budget—I talked about it as a breathing-space Budget—that rejected any argument for across-the-board tax rises.

So there was a strong emphasis on the cost of living help that we can provide now—a bit of breathing space to households—and also some of the important policy measures there for the rest of the Government.

Bobby Dean: That brings me to my second point. The OBR noted that the freeze on tax thresholds is having the biggest impact on people’s disposable income and therefore on their living standards. You said that you did not want to raise taxes, but over the course of this Parliament that threshold freeze is the equivalent of putting about 2% on to income tax.

If you were to take the decision to raise income tax by 2%, you would be expected to explain that at the Dispatch Box, but freezing the thresholds was not mentioned at all in your Budget speech. Do you think it is fair on the public for them to see an effective tax rise without it being explained or justified?

John Healey: The decision on thresholds had already been taken; it was not a fresh decision for me for this Budget. I wanted to reject any arguments for an across-the-board increase in taxes, which I did. The second thing I wanted to do was to make sure that, as a governing party, we could respect and follow the commitments that people voted for us on at the election: that we would not raise the levels of income tax, national insurance or VAT.

Bobby Dean: Do you accept the OBR’s analysis that the freeze on thresholds is one of the primary drags on people’s living standards?

John Healey: I accept that the freeze is there in the numbers: the contribution it makes to the finances, and, in the analysis, the impact that it has on people’s incomes. I am very conscious of that. It is also there in the numbers just how expensive it would be to change it.

Jim Dickson: Chancellor, you have just said that growth was the solution to some of the issues around household incomes. We have heard about the fiscal position for the country generally, and one of the keys to stimulating economic growth is productivity. How did you evaluate measures in the Budget to boost productivity, and how did you decide which ones to include and which ones not to?

John Healey: As a member of this Committee, Mr Dickson, you will be aware that our measures of productivity are imperfect; they are often lagging. What I hope you would see is that the focus of the fiscal and intervention measures—greater investment, the boost for innovation and some of the fiscal measures for the fastest-growth firms—is on areas where Britain particularly leads, such as advanced manufacturing, digital and AI.

On AI, we are really the third leading country in the world. Those are the areas where the emerging evidence of productivity growth is greatest and most encouraging. If we can reinforce that, I think we will see it come through more clearly in the numbers. I do not know whether Dharmesh or James have anything to add, particularly on the technicals of productivity.

James Bowler: I have a few things to add. Productivity grew by 2.2% last year, which is very welcome. It is also worth saying to this Committee, as you will all have followed, that the ONS has struggled with measuring it. There is an excellent box in the Budget document, to which I would draw your attention, on how that has changed. The ONS improved its long-run productivity figure from 0.5% to 0.9%—still below historical averages—but it means we have much more information showing that we are lagging less far behind our international peers than we were.

The last thing I would emphasise is that in the Budget process there is often a fixation on the so-called scorecard: the big table at the back of the Budget. This Chancellor put very significant effort into the non-scorecard, non-fiscal elements of the Budget—what we could be doing, particularly on growth—and how they would impact productivity. That was just as important as the numbers going up and down in the scorecard.

Dharmesh Nayee: If I may add something, one of the areas where the UK has traditionally been weakest is both public and private investment. Over the last two years, we have seen quite strong growth in Government investment—it is up about 17% since the end of the last Parliament—and business investment is up about 7.5% since then. Obviously, that is one of the biggest things you can do for longer-term productivity.

John Healey: Business investment has not been as high as that for at least 10 years.

Jim Dickson: It is said by the IFS and others that investment in AI is clearly potentially a very good bet for improving long-term productivity. You chose to put, I think, £700 million of AI investment into the economy. That represents only 1.5% of what Anthropic itself has invested in producing AI products—$65 billion in its company just in the last year. Do you think that £700 million is enough to make a difference, when we see what other investment is going on around the world?

John Healey: It is. Dame Harriett Baldwin: Chancellor, a lot of the revenues you have scored, particularly in the further-out years, are from accelerating the rate at which businesses pay their tax as opposed to increasing the amount of tax they pay. Do you think that is an accounting trick?

John Healey: It is not an accounting trick. It is a measure designed to try to reduce the amount of debt to HMRC that is built up. The principal case for it is that most businesses will make provision for the tax they may have to pay, but asking them to pay smaller amounts more regularly, rather than a larger amount at a later date, is likely to lead, from a business’s point of view, to more manageable cashflows. From a public accounting point of view, it is likely to lead to less outstanding amounts owed to the Treasury.
