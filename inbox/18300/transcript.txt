Chair: Are the Government’s road safety casualty reduction targets achievable without a significant increase in the scale and effectiveness of road traffic enforcement?

Paul Fermer: In short, no. It is not all about enforcement; it is also about the type of enforcement. On the network, we currently have fixed cameras, average speed cameras and mobile enforcement. Enforcement is required because of compliance, and lack of compliance, but I think there are other ways to tackle compliance. So, it is not all on enforcement, but in order to achieve the targets, my view is that we need more funding to go into the system to enable the police force to enforce more.

Certainly, within Oxfordshire, there is a very strong, willing volunteer community in Speedwatch, and we can make more of that as a country by pushing and embedding it in some of the enforcement regimes.

Angus Eaton: I echo Paul’s words. Enforcement is fundamental to the road safety strategy. It is not the exclusive element of the strategy. We would certainly advocate that it does need to be modernised. Our experience of the fines for uninsured driving, for instance, is that they are antiquated at best and need to be brought up to date. I would support the view that there are many constituent parts to what enables enforcement, as well as who can enforce, and we feel that needs to happen. We work closely with the police, and they do a remarkable job, but there are instances where perhaps local authorities may have a role in this.

Joy Allen: Thank you, it is a real pleasure to be here and to have heard the last panel. I will go slightly off script, because I want to join the two discussions, which I think it is quite relevant. When we do road safety, enforcement on the roads with cameras, all of that money goes into the Treasury and could be used for other things, whereas if it went into road safety, roads policing, that could be a solution to the problem of where the money is going to come from to deliver this ambitious strategy. That is a potential solution I would like to offer.

Chair: In your written evidence, you stated that the targets cannot be met without substantial improvements to resourcing and enforcement, although you have made a very strong case for Departments working together across the piece. What improvements would have the greatest impact on reducing KSIs?

Joy Allen: Most people, from the practitioners and the evidence, would say that speeding is a key area to focus on. Obviously that requires investment in capacity and enforcement. Rural roads are important, as a significant number of deaths and serious injuries occur on rural roads. How do we enforce that if it is linked to speeding?

Chair: That leads me nicely to a question for Paul. In your written submission, you argue that the strategy gives insufficient attention to safe speeds and safe roads. Will you expand on that?

Paul Fermer: I appreciate that the setting of local speed limits is being worked on, but from our perspective we are very aware that lower speed limits do save lives, which is why Oxfordshire has pushed quite hard—locally led, but pushed quite hard—to make 20 mph the new 30 mph in a lot of urban areas. We have been very successful at doing that. The evidence we have is that it has made a difference in terms of safer streets. We are very keen on safer streets, not just safer roads, because we want to make sure that everyone feels safe moving around.

There was a lot of work done about 15 years ago, but there were still significant numbers of A and B roads at 60 mph. We are looking to review and reduce the numbers. We believe that the norm should be 20 mph for urban areas and 50 mph, potentially 40 mph, for rural areas, and that you have to justify as a local authority why it should be higher. At the moment we have to justify and have arguments about why we want to make streets 20 mph and not 30 mph. It should be the other way around.
