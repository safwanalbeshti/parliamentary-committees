Resilience of the food supply chain to disruptions (HC 587)

Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Jim Bligh, Balwinder Dhoot and Harry Metcalfe.

Chair: Welcome to the Public Accounts Committee on Monday 12 October 2026. Today marks the start of our farming week, in which we take the unusual step of dedicating two separate evidence sessions to examining the resilience of the UK’s food supply to disruption, in order to highlight the significance of the issue.

Disruptions to the food supply chain can have a significant impact on consumers, businesses and Government. Overall, food availability has been maintained in the UK food supply chain during recent disruptions, such as the covid-19 pandemic, and the wars in Iran and Ukraine, but the risks are increasing. Today’s sitting is split between two panels, comprising a cast of witnesses with a huge wealth of knowledge and experience of working in this area. On Thursday, we will hear from senior Government officials about the Government’s point of view on the issue.

This afternoon, we look to hear from our witnesses on a variety of topics, including the Government’s ability to maintain our food supply, the preparedness of the farming sector for major disruption, and the Government’s engagement with food retailers, manufacturers and farmers about food supply resilience. I will now do my usual and ask each of you please to introduce yourselves briefly and perhaps to say what your history is on this subject. Jim, will you start by introducing yourself, please?

Jim Bligh: I am Jim Bligh. I am director of corporate affairs and food policy at the British Retail Consortium. Before joining the BRC in January, I worked for the Food and Drink Federation and before that for PepsiCo, so I have a range of experience in the food industry.

Chair: Good to have you, thank you very much for coming. Harry, of course, we all know you—you may not know us, but we know you.

Harry Metcalfe: I am very grateful to be here, Chair. My background: agriculture college, 1981 came out of there; grain buyer, but wanted to get into farming; started in farming in my own right in 1984, first harvest ’85; and it has gone from there. Currently, I have a farm in the Cotswolds, 300 acres, 120 acres rented, and I am next door to Mr Clarkson, whom you might also know in the Cotswolds.

Chair: Thank you very much, Harry. Bal, please.

Balwinder Dhoot: I am Bal Dhoot. I am the director for sustainability and growth at the Food and Drink Federation. I have been there for three and a half years. Before that, I was a civil servant in DEFRA, where I worked on some previous crises and shocks to hit the food system.

Q1 Chair: Thank you very much. I will start with the first question, but before I do, I should declare an interest as a practising farmer and as a chartered surveyor. Everyone take that into account when I ask my questions. My first question is, I think, to all three of you. What are the biggest challenges that farmers currently face?

Jim Bligh: Thank you for the question, and for the excellent Report from the Auditor General’s team at the NAO, which sets the scene really well. Resilience is absolutely critical to retailers. A resilient supply chain is what we require to be able to operate all around the UK. The vast majority of the food that we sell through retail is produced in the UK, but too much of that comes in as imports from overseas, and all of us as retailers have a priority to increase domestic production.

Clearly, there are challenges for farmers, for manufacturers and for us. We have been through a number of shocks over the years, and the NAO Report identifies them very well. They range from covid to Ukraine, the Iran crisis this year and the ensuing problems with fuel, fertiliser, diesel, and so on. What they show is that we have a remarkably resilient system but that that system, as the NAO says, is reliant on the private sector to be able to co-ordinate itself and to move at the right pace so that we do not end up with, for example, a lack of availability in stores and empty shelves, which for us are a mark of serious failure.

There are probably five groups of risks. I won’t go into too much detail in this answer, but they are climate; energy and water; cyber-security; regulation and Government policy and the constant chopping and changing there; and the geopolitical and trade risks that we all face as well. But specifically on your question about farmers, Mr Chairman, what is abundantly clear to us is that farmers are struggling to get planning permission—struggling to be able to put in, for example, above-ground reservoirs, which should then be able to water field-grade crops. Every farmer I talk to says, “We can’t get the approvals from Government quickly enough,” or, “We’re restricted from being able to put a reservoir on our land.”

At the same time, energy costs have gone up enormously. If you are in protected horticulture or in poultry, it is extremely difficult to fund the energy costs, given where they are. Given that food is a critical national infrastructure industry, it is surprising that Government have not taken any steps on energy to reduce costs for those particular industries, whereas it has for other industries around the place. Answering specifically on farmers, I think those things are among the challenges.

For us as retailers, our tax cost has gone up by 20% in the last two years—since the 2024 Budget. We now pay an effective tax rate of 73.5%. The increase in costs is from employment costs, business rates, packaging costs—the full range. It is about £8 billion a year since the election. If there is one thing that worries us about resilience, it is the huge costs that have been piled into the system since 2024—and, to be fair, in the years before that as well, but this has been particularly pronounced recently. It limits our capacity to invest and the capacity of the system to make money; and ultimately, profit is a good thing. Profit in the food system is the only way we will create the investment and the conditions to grow and to change, given all the circumstances that we face and those risks that we face.

Q2 Chair: Thank you very much; that is a really good opening statement from you.

I will now go to Harry for the farming point of view. Farmers have had a pretty difficult year with the drought.

Harry Metcalfe: Well, you say that. Farming is interesting because it is so variable. The definition of a farmer is a food producer, but you can go from a chicken farm to a pig farm, a Lakeland sheep farmer, a Scottish farmer or my sector, which is arable farming mainly—that is more concentrated in southern England. We have all had certain struggles in different ways, but if you are asking me about our ability to maintain our food supply while keeping food affordable, the challenge right now is this. As an arable farmer, I have had four of the five worst harvests since 2020, and 2026 is the smallest harvest of all since records began—people started keeping records in 1984, when I started farming. The frustration from a farming point of view is that the small harvest is all down to climate change, or climate events, as you would term it. We have had it go from flooding to then not being able to establish a good crop and then get to harvest. This year, for example, it was like someone pressed a switch at the end of June and it just went out. You thought it was going to be a really good harvest and you put the combine in—and it’s not there. So the challenge at the moment for farming in my area is, “What on earth is happening? How can I still grow food? Do I have to grow earlier crops or something to take advantage of the climate change that we’ve got?”

I ought to just mention here that the Scottish farms have done really well this year, because they did not suffer that extreme climate; they have had a good year. The only dilemma I have in talking on farming is that it does vary, depending on where in the country you are growing food.

Q3 Chair: I have just come back from the north of Scotland—I have never seen so much grass in all my life. Mind you, they were still trying to harvest oats in October, so there is always a downside.

Harry Metcalfe: Yes, they are very keen on their grain dryers up in Scotland, but then in the Lake district, they have had a fantastic year.

Q4 Chair: Indeed. Bal?

Balwinder Dhoot: I agree with a lot of what Jim has said about how the sector has been incredibly resilient over the last six or seven years. It has been through some exceptional shocks. Our sector is very reliant on farming. They produce the ingredients that go into our products, and we are a massive customer for domestically produced agricultural production. Having talked about those shocks that have played out over the last seven or eight years, my concern would be, “Would we manage them the same over the next seven or eight years?” I see resilience around domestic production slowly being eroded. It is a bit like a slow puncture, in some ways.

We published some trade data recently, which showed that in the first half of this year, the gap between our imports and exports was one of the highest it has been in the last 25 years. That is not an exception. That is a trend that has been happening over the last decade. If you look into that, I think it suggests that production is moving overseas more. That could be because people are sourcing more. It could be from overseas for their inputs here. It could be because factories are being relocated overseas. When a factory relocates, it is not going to be buying British produce; it is going to buy produce from the country where the factory is. It is kind of a signal around what is happening. At the moment, it is probably manageable. The question is: if that trend continues for the next five to 10 years, would we be able to manage the shocks that the food system has experienced in the same way that we have got through the last six or seven years?

Q5 Chair: What is the answer to that question?

Balwinder Dhoot: I think it would be a lot more challenging. The other thing I would add is that we know there will be more volatility and more shocks. We consider what we have experienced in the last six or seven years to be the new normal, whether it is geopolitics, climate or other events. We should be preparing for the same again, if not worse. We should be thinking about that long-term resilience piece. It is important to think about how we react to specific shocks in the moment or prepare for those, but long-term resilience is the key.

Q6 Chair: Would you like to give us any specific examples of how you think we could increase our resilience?

Balwinder Dhoot: Resilience comes down to investment. In the same way that Jim talked about being able to make profit, if you cannot make money in this country, you will stop farming, you will move your factory and so on. How do you have a system that gets the market to invest in production, but also in the things around resilience, such as stronger supply chains, de-risking energy costs and cyber-security? All those things require investment. How do we have a system that allows and encourages that investment? Alternatively, you can have a system that encourages you to import or shift production overseas. I would add that we are not anti-trade. Having a mix of domestic production and trade is really important for food security. The question is the balance, and the trend is signalling where we are heading, which could become problematic over the next decade.

Q7 Chair: I will hand over to Rupert Lowe in a minute, but Harry, from your point of view, not only do you have resilience in the farming field, but you have to look at resilience in the food chain. Have you any suggestions for us, looking forward five or 10 years, about what we ought to be doing?

Harry Metcalfe: My concerns with the food chain are really about whether they understand how we farm, how we have to take a risk on putting a crop in a ground, and this long-term wait until we can actually give them what they have requested. For an arable farm, I have already committed my 2027 harvest. I know precisely what I am harvesting. It is all in the ground. I have already incurred 74% of the cost. There is nothing I can do once it is fixed. I am then reliant on the weather to behave and can then supply to the food chain. Right now, growing food seems to be an incredibly risky business. I am not sure what we do to encourage me to grow more food at the moment, because currently, it is very high risk—that is proven by the fact that four of the worst harvests have happened since 2020. Why would I continue to grow food when it is such a high-risk activity?

I am also doing it on the back of those four harvests. As I said in a recent video, 2025 was my worst harvest since 1984, by a country mile, so although ’26 was okay, all it actually did was make up what I lost in 2025. I need ’27 to come along before I can put investment into the farm, or into anything. Cash flow is a disaster for farms in my region. The challenge for cereal-growing farmers at the moment is cash flow—they just have not got the cash. What I have learnt recently is that the withdrawal of the basic payment scheme has exposed all farmers to the realities of trying to farm at world prices. That has been a real wake-up call for some, especially when you throw a weather wobbly into it as well. That is the real challenge. As I say, livestock farming and Scotland have not suffered this, as it is regional in this country, but a very big region of the UK has suffered this year.

Q8 Chair: You already mentioned the SFI debacle a week ago. Is there anything more the Government can do—not necessarily monetarily, but in terms of longer-term pronouncements—to give you some confidence that what you have done in planting those crops will actually produce a result?

Harry Metcalfe: As far as I know, the Government cannot control the weather, so they cannot really control the big lever for what the yield will be. I am sure we will come on to SFI and the schemes that are currently there. I am a bit of a fan of those, but none of them actually encourages me to grow food at the moment. That is what I would love to see come out of the debate this week: how will DEFRA encourage me to grow food in the very high-risk environment we have at the moment? To be perfectly honest, I do not have the answer, but I do know that taking away the safety cushion of the area payments has exposed farmers to the reality.

I am also very concerned about the tenanted sector of farming. If you have cash-flow struggles, the last thing you can afford is next year’s rent for your landlords. I can see that being a major challenge. I am also a British taxpayer, as I have other businesses, so I do not want to see money being wasted in terms of how we pay our taxes and where they should go, but food security is a really big one and we ought to be concerned. Right now, as a farmer, I am doing everything I can to not grow food. That is just wrong.

Q9 Chair: That is very helpful. Jim, before I bring Rupert Lowe in, do you have any thoughts, five or 10 years out, about what we should concentrate on with the food supply chain?

Jim Bligh: The first thing, reflecting on where Harry and Bal are as well, is long-term investments. We are seeing a lot more supermarkets having long-term partnerships with farmers and manufacturers. Those enable them to weather the storm, as it were, to create the long-term conditions to invest. If we are asking for tens of millions of pounds of investment in new agricultural machinery or new infrastructure on a farm, there has to be some kind of payback over a period of time. That means that you have to guarantee to farmers that the financing will be there over a long time for stability.

As industry and as the major buyers, we can do quite a lot of that—the challenge comes with Government. I will not make a political point, but we are now on our third Environment Secretary since the last election. I have lost count of the number of discussions I have had about food strategies and cycles—honestly, I am a little lost as to what the strategy in the Department is. Then there is the question of policy priorities. Farmers, manufacturers and retailers are in the middle of a global storm. The Government are saying, “You’ve got to fix the roof,” but the sun is not shining. How do we do that in a way that is affordable, enables us to meet our goals, and supports farmers and manufacturers to adapt and be resilient? It is really tricky.

In terms of what the ask is, we can provide long-term investment against a long-term, certain backdrop, but that means the Department needs to provide a clearer steer on what its long-term strategy is. Risk mitigation for the big buckets of risk that we face needs to fit in as part of that: if the climate goes one particular way or we have serious cyber-security incidents, how will we respond to that in practice? That is where the Department has not provided adequate clarity yet and it could be a lot more strategic about how it does that in the decades to come. We know this storm is going to continue; it will not get easier.

Chair: Thank you for all your opening remarks.

Q10 Rupert Lowe: I should disclose that I have an 865 acre farm in the Cotswolds where, like you, Harry, we farm arable land and we have some sheep.

I take your point about the variability of farming across the country, but that has always been the case. Very often up north they cannot harvest their crops because they get too much rain and even the dryers cannot cope with wheat at 25%.

As to what concerns me, it was quite instructive that paragraph 10 of the NAO Report said DEFRA is “less confident about the ability of businesses” in the food supply chain “to withstand shocks without government intervention in the next five to 10 years.” Frankly, the last thing we need is Government intervention, but why do they need to say that? The answer is that they have created that situation: we watched them increase taxes on employment; taxes are about to come in with this UK carbon border adjustment mechanism, which is going to put another tax on fertiliser; and the net zero agenda means that energy is more expensive than in any other country in the world. They basically are doing absolutely everything in their power to dissuade people, to Harry’s point, from planting crops.

I am fortunate that I do not have any debt, but like you, Harry, when you drill crops, pay 700 quid a tonne for nitrogen and have expensive wheat seed, you are effectively drilling your grain into the ground knowing that you are going to make a loss. You are effectively speculating on two things: No. 1 is that the price is going to go up, and No. 2 is that we are not going to get a catastrophic harvest like we did this year, last year and the year before.

On an overcrowded island, I think we should be prioritising growing crops and feeding ourselves. We account for a relatively low percentage of our food. With rewilding and all the other sorts of schemes—I have recently stopped development on grade 1 arable land in my Great Yarmouth constituency—the Government are doing everything they can to score an own goal and then wondering why farmers are going bust and the supply chain is falling apart.

Chair: We have got quite a lot—

Rupert Lowe: The question we have got to ask ourselves is, is DEFRA equipped to make the long-term plan that farming needs to deliver for the country? That is the question I want to ask all of you.

Harry Metcalfe: Farming is a long-term strategy when you go into it, and food production is at the heart of farming. That is what we are here for, that is what the land is there for, and we enjoy doing it. It is the risk element that has been introduced. I have farmed for 40 years, but it has only got a bit weird in the last five or six years. It was not quite as bad; you had the occasional shock, but something has happened.

I will just drag this point out: when I started as a grain buyer, there was something called intervention. Intervention was an EU scheme that put a minimum price on grain. In 1984-85, in November I would pay the farmer £117.82 for wheat delivered. That is about £110 ex-farm, or something like that. If I put that into the Bank of England calculator, that puts the price of wheat today at £360 to £400 a tonne, depending on which calculator you use. We were around the £180 mark, but since it has become more widely known that we have had a very bad harvest in Europe, it has moved to the right side over £200. But it is a long way adrift from where inflation should take us. That is the challenge: why has that global grain price not moved up with just inflation? Normally, supply and demand sorts this out, but when you have grain traders in Chicago or somewhere setting the price in the UK, we are this little patch and we may have suffered a bad climate while there has been a good harvest in the southern hemisphere or something. I do not know what we will do, but somehow we need to protect the farmers when this situation occurs and we have a really bad harvest. Europe is wrestling with this as well, because it cannot afford the agricultural budget either.

The only answer I have relates to something that Jim said. I asked one of the chicken farmers I supply how he was getting on with grain prices going up. I said, “Does that affect you?” He said, “No, the price I get for my chicken, with the supplier, is linked to the grain price. With the grain price going up, I actually get more, because they know I am paying more for the grain.” I am not suggesting that we should get an intervention, with a min price or something, but in quite a number of places the food system is protected from these price shocks. We did not know that Putin was going to invade Ukraine and that diesel prices would go up. How you actually manage that as a Government is extremely hard, I think, but it is the reality of what is happening in farming now: the farmer is suffering huge shocks, but he has a contract to supply grain and he just has to get on with it.

To come back to the grain buyer, there is one thing that really annoys me. I can sell forward. If I wanted, today I could sell my 2026-27 harvest; I could receive a price and say, “Right, I can make a profit on that.” But if I have another terrible harvest like in 2025, where instead of having 500 tonnes I have 200 tonnes, I will have to make up the difference as a farmer when that grain trader says, “I want the other 300 tonnes.” If I have had a really bad harvest, the price will be higher and it will cost me a lot of money. It is not encouraging me to take advantage of a good price. You wait until you have harvested and you take your price then.

It just seems like that is something where the supply side could help farmers, if we have these shocks from climate, diesel or fertiliser. I actually worked out that I have paid 34% more for my fertiliser this year than last year. As Rupert Lowe was saying, I have bought it early, because the Government are introducing a carbon tax on it from 1 January, so I know it will go up after that. That kills cash flow, because I have paid everything up front.

I do not have the answer, but there are these shocks. How do we protect farmers? How do we keep them going through this rough time? We cannot say that every summer will be like the one that we have just had. You do not want to put something in place and say, “That’s it,” but I think we need something, when these major shocks are happening that do not have anything to do with farming, if we are going to get increasingly concerned about food security. I am not growing food in the high-risk environment that we have at the moment; I am going to minimise the amount of food I grow, because I just cannot afford it.

Chair: Thank you for that. It is not necessary for all three of you to answer every question, so unless either Jim or Bal has a specific comment on farming, perhaps we will move on to Rupert’s next question.

Jim Bligh: I did have a comment on the extra costs and DEFRA’s capability. DEFRA is an economic Department—Paul Kissack, the permanent secretary, has said at the EFRA Committee that it is an economic Department, and that is right—but I do not think that it always behaves as an economic Department or is viewed as such by other Departments. When you have a crisis, or even when you do not, I am not convinced that DEFRA is able to pull the levers across Government for the food system in a way that a Department such as the Treasury might be able to.

Balwinder Dhoot: There are volatilities and costs that are out of a Government’s control: wars, the climate, third countries putting tariffs on. Q13 Rachel Gilmour: I should say that as well as being the Member of Parliament for Tiverton and Minehead, which is a very rural constituency with lots of wonderful farmers, I am also co-chair of the farming APPG. How prepared do you think the farming sector is for a major disruption, whether in respect of fuel supplies, droughts, as we have seen over the summer, or flooding, as we are inevitably about to get this winter? Harry Metcalfe: I’m afraid I do not think we are prepared at all in my sector. In the arable side we are not prepared. With my friends who are not farmers, I compare farming to having a factory: you produce food, but you do so without a roof on your factory. If it suddenly rains at the wrong time, or if it does not rain or whatever, you are stuffed. You go through all the processes, and you do everything you can—we have the best equipment and we have done everything we can—but your output is determined by that happens with the weather. Singapore, the Netherlands, Japan and Israel—there are lots of countries that have active and assertive approaches to the resilience of their food systems. They have different types of concerns, and they will have different complexions to their strategies, but there is plenty to be learned from other countries. I am sure Tim will be able to say much more.

Professor Lang: I think you have given that breadth. Different countries have different strategies, and their motivations are different. If you go to the Netherlands, which I did for my report, it is a small country that had experience of famine in world war two. Welfare and health policy are really important in the Netherlands because of the 1944-45 famine, and you can still measure the life expectancy of Dutch women by the preconceptual care of their grandmothers—it is quite extraordinary. The threat of famine has had a very big impact, and a bit like how Finland decided to specialise in ceramics and glass, post second world war, the Netherlands chose to invest in those very fertile soils beneath the sea level. It built a culture within Government of taking food production very seriously, while Britain’s approach has been much more about avoidance.

I agree that Finland is also very good. In my experience, the closer to Russia you get, the more seriously they take this. I agree that Singapore is interesting as a city state, because it has decided to double its own production, even though it is a city state, not a big landed sovereignty. I was personally very impressed in France by how—not in the name of resilience, but in the name of rebuilding urban-rural connections—they had done some very interesting work to diversify food systems in the name of agri-food regionalism. Now that work has been of great interest and support to the French Government in the name of resilience.

At the sub-national level, I was particularly taken by the French-speaking part of Belgium: Wallonie or Wallonia, depending on what your language is. There is a city called Liège—it is like Sheffield, because it is a deindustrialised coal and steel town—and 15 years ago they essentially started to say, “What is the point of Liège?” They said, “Well, let’s run with food as a purpose. People in Liège eat food,” and they basically built a new culture. They have a big annual food festival, and they have started a new programme of regional horticultural enterprises around it.

What I did in my report was look at countries, which is why Neil was turning to me, and there are lessons we can learn from them. For me, the No. 1 is actually Sweden, which I was hinting at earlier. It has done the most astute national review of resilience and what it wants from its land. Norway is doing something very similar, so I think we can learn from the Baltics. Latvia was very interesting to me. It looked at, “What do we do if Russia rides all over us? Well, probably the Government will have collapsed, so we need to go down the levels and think about hyper-local community food resilience.” My answer to your question is: don’t just look at national Governments. We need to look below national Governments at the regional—or the sub-national, to be pompous—and at cities down to the very local.

To go back to the question Sir Geoffrey asked a quarter of an hour ago, one reason why I think Neil and I were both saying that you do need the Government to set new frameworks is that one weakness of DEFRA and the British Government system is that they do not apply food and environment strategies to the sub-national. It is all assumed to be done from Whitehall, until you go to Wales.

In Wales, there is a really interesting movement going on. It has created and appointed a food security adviser and a food strategy adviser, but it also has 22 local food partnerships covering the whole of Wales. County councils are doing interesting things in Wales. It is much more multi-level. Do not just look at what we can learn from other countries; we need to look at who is dealing with the multi-level-ness of food security resilience well. The short answer to that is, no one is doing it perfectly, but there are wonderful lessons we can draw for different levels from different countries.

To wrap that up and to try to be practical, one of the things my report suggested was that English mayors get powers. The Devolution Act needs to do for England what it has done rather unequally and differently for Scotland and Wales. We need to unleash imagination and change to be a more resilient food system. Giving mayors and unitary authorities responsibilities and powers—people like me have been arguing that that needs to come but for resilience. If you are just planning for bombs going off, shocks, disasters, floods and droughts, you invoke the Civil Contingencies Act. Call it a “resilience Act”; you would want food to be in there, in which case we need to be giving powers to different levels for different capacities. I think the metro mayors would be up for that in England, and certainly the devolved Administrations in Wales and Scotland would; Northern Ireland is slightly different.

To answer your question in one sentence, there is lots that we can learn from lots of other people, but no one has it perfect because we are all having to rethink what we have done in the last 70 years. We have created a more vulnerable food system that is more prone to shocks. We are now all having to think, what do we want for the next 30 years? We will need to look at that multi-level-ness very seriously.
