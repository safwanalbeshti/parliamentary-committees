Q58 Chair: Today we are holding our second oral evidence session on the draft Conversion Practices Bill. The Joint Committee has been appointed to consider and report on the Government’s draft Bill, which sets out provisions to prevent and criminalise abusive and harmful conversion practices. I am very grateful to our witnesses for attending today.

The Committee is dealing with highly sensitive matters of which people in this room and people watching or listening will have direct personal experience. We understand that some people may find today’s evidence session distressing and difficult to listen to. Several organisations that can offer support are listed on our website. I remind everybody in the room that, under the rules on sub judice, we are not to discuss any live cases before the courts.

Before I start with questions, I have apologies from Olivia Bailey, who will be a little late. She is stuck in the Chamber with Committee business, but she will be here post-haste.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: I would like to declare that Jayne and I have known each other for a long time and had a shared endeavour in fundraising for the Oxford children’s hospital.

Lord Isaac: I declare an interest in my former roles as chair of Stonewall and as chair of the Equality and Human Rights Commission. I have had dealings with, and have been lobbied by, Jayne.

Chair: Could both witnesses introduce themselves?

Ian Smith: Hi, I am Ian Smith. I am based in Cardiff—in your constituency, I believe, Chair. Essentially, I am here not in any political or campaigning stance, but literally as one of the 75% of those victims, if you like, who responded to the consultation suggesting that they needed to see a change in the law. That is one of the reasons I am here, after suffering what I would term abuse within those contexts.

Chair: I am really grateful to you for being here, Ian. Thank you so much for that.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: I am Deputy Jayne Ozanne. I am a parliamentarian in Guernsey; I am a States Deputy, which obviously is totally separate from this.

Many of you will know that I set up and chaired the Ban Conversion Therapy coalition for many years, but I passed that on when I became a parliamentarian. I also set up and chaired the Ban Conversion Practices legal forum, which I passed on to Professor Trispiotis, whom you heard from last week.

I think it is helpful to say that I led a debate in the Church of England in 2017 to ban conversion practices. That debate passed—it was a private member’s motion—and it ended with the then Archbishop of York, Dr Sentamu, calling on the Synod to call on the Government to ban it.

That was the start of my long relationship with the Government to try to ban it. I became a Government adviser, wearing a hat to try to ban these terribly abusive practices, but perhaps most importantly and relevant to today, I also set up a foundation. I have my own foundation, but I also set up the Global Interfaith Commission on LGBT+ Lives, and one of the things we sought to do was to look at how we brought senior religious leaders—I am talking about the Pope, archbishops and Chief Rabbis—to a point of recognising the harm these practices do. Many of them signed a statement to call for a ban. I sit very much in the religion-meets-LGBT space.

Q59 Chair: I am grateful, Jayne; thank you so much. If you need a moment, please just indicate that to me as we go through this. We really are grateful that you are here.

Obviously, this Committee has been established to scrutinise the draft Bill. I wonder whether either of you could outline your overarching views on the proposal as it sits currently about banning abusive conversion practices and the importance of doing so. Of course, any reflections you may have that inform those overarching views would be most helpful.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: It has been a long journey to this point, and it would probably be churlish of me not to say how pleased I am that we actually have a Bill that we are looking at and debating today. I recognise the challenges of this Bill and I will lay them out, but I do want to say up front how grateful I am, not least because the delay, which we have experienced for eight years now, has emboldened perpetrators, and I hate to think of the number of people who have suffered during that time of delay. I am grateful to see a Bill that looks at overseas provisions. I am grateful that it is looking at both LGB and T.

Before I get into the Bill in granular detail, I think it would be fair to say up front that “conversion practices” is an umbrella term. It is one that we have invented, in a sense; we often called it conversion therapy in the past. Recognising that it is not therapy, and indeed it does not convert, we moved to the phrase “conversion practices”, but that is not a term that people who are often undergoing it recognise, so when we have done research in the past, I am not sure we have actually managed to capture everybody, because people did not understand what we were talking about.

I think the best example is myself. Many of you may know that, sadly, I too am a survivor. I chose willingly to go through 20 years of what I now recognise would be called conversion practices, but it took me actually presenting at an event for the Government on this for me to recognise, “Goodness, this actually involves me too. These are things that I’ve gone through.” I have received over 20 years of prayer. I have gone through many exorcisms. I have fasted, I have prayed and I have gone through all sorts of things that many now would say was very clear abuse, and I did so willingly. I consented, because I thought it would help me.

I think we need to hold this term in all its breadth very broadly, and we will come back to that, but the definition of conversion therapy is probably the hardest thing, which every jurisdiction I have worked with has found difficult. I have to be honest—and I am sorry to be critical—I think we have got it badly wrong here, and I can unpack that either now or later, but I do not think that the route we have chosen to take is a wise one. I agree with what Professor Trispiotis said last week. I wish we had followed what other jurisdictions have done, specifically in looking at the concept of suppression. The focus on causation has all sorts of challenges in healthcare and in religious settings.

Many of you may have received the Cooper report, produced by a group of us—legal academics and quite senior legal counsel—and chaired by Baroness Helena Kennedy. I was involved in pulling it together. The report sought to advise the Government on how they could go about banning conversion therapy. The concept we came up with was that conversion therapy is any practice in which the predetermined purpose of the person practising it is to seek to change, suppress or—I would say—heal someone’s sexuality or gender identity. It started with the mindset of the person with the power trying to change. It was that predetermined nature, which said, “You can never be gay,” or, “You can never be trans,” that caused the problem. I fear that in the definition we have landed on here we have slightly moved away from that and focused on the victim, not the perpetrator, if that makes sense.

The definition is problematic; I know we have not got all day so I am going to move on. The second most difficult thing is, I think, unintentional. You have created two categories—an abusive category and a non-abusive category—by using the adjective “abusive”. I think I understand why that has happened: you are trying to establish a criminal threshold. But the problem is that in saying that your non-abusive forms do not meet that threshold, you are giving licence to people to say, “Well, actually, what we are doing doesn’t really make that much difference so we are going to carry on doing it.” That is the vast majority of people who are trying to—as I think some would say—pray the gay away. Prayer goes to the heart of it. I speak as a Christian and someone with a deep love of prayer, but not all prayer is healthy or good. In my case it caused me, sadly, after 20 years of seeking out prayer, to end up in hospital fighting for my life because my body could not take the strain of the constant messaging that who I was was unacceptable and that I would never seek to have the intimacy that I craved. A lot of people focus on sex; it is about love. It is about being able to form a family unit and to have a satisfying, fulfilled life full of dignity.

The Church of England already used the abusive/non-abusive distinction this summer to allow an event at the General Synod to go ahead. The Archbishop coined that phrase—“Well, it’s a non-abusive form”—so those terms are already being used, wisely or not, to create different camps.

I have mentioned already that I think the threshold is too high. Trying to prove “serious harm” or “serious alarm or distress” puts all the focus on the victim. Obviously suicide is serious harm, but we do not want to take someone to that point. We want to intervene before they get to that point, so I have significant trouble with where we set that threshold. On that I might disagree with my colleagues who gave evidence to the Committee last week.

The Bill does not touch on consent. Arguably, you cannot consent to abuse, so potentially it does not need to be in there, but if we want a Bill that covers everything we ought to be explicit, not implicit.

As others have mentioned, the Bill does not touch on suppression, which is by far the most normal form in religious settings, now, that I am aware of—suppression meaning, “You might be gay but just don’t act on it.” So, either you need to be single and celibate for the rest of your life, despite the fact that you might yearn to love and be loved—that is what sent me to hospital—or, arguably worse, you just have to pretend to be straight: “Go and get married to someone and it’ll all be okay”. Sadly, we now see, particularly in the religious world, a lot of broken marriages with families torn apart because one of the partners finally comes out, often in their 50s after years of anguish, and that leads to tragedy on all sides.

Suppression has its own problems. I get that some people are called to be single and celibate for the rest of their lives—that is wonderful, but not if it is forced on you and, perhaps most importantly, not if that is what you are taught when you are growing up. That is happening to many teenagers who, perhaps, confide in their youth pastors that they are suffering from same-sex attraction and are told, “Well, you just have to bear that for the rest of your life. You will never be allowed to have a relationship.” That is what sends so many people to a very challenging place with their mental health. We have a lot of studies on that, which I will leave you with. Sorry, that was a very long answer, but it probably goes to the heart of today.

Q60 Chair: That is incredibly helpful, Jayne. I know that colleagues will want to go into some of those points, and I will turn to them in a moment. Ian, is there anything that you want to say in broadbrush about the position of the Bill and the importance you attach to it—assuming you do?

Ian Smith: To agonise over the legal detail I can absolutely understand, and I also understand how much time people have put into going over the legislation. Jayne has been working on this for eight-plus years; I have not been working on it for that length of time in the least.

What would have helped me at the time I was going through what I was going through is for institutions to be held accountable, because the country that they found themselves a part of took it seriously. I consented to the practices that I went for, which included exorcism and writhing around on the floor while I was being “prayed for” by “caring” individuals, and I also consented to therapy. It was organisations, such as the Association of Christian Counsellors, Teen Challenge, Assemblies of God and Evangelical Alliance, that were overseeing that, but those institutions had no sense that there was something wrong with the practices that those individuals were carrying out.

When you look at the respondents to the original call for evidence, the institutional respondents were more opposed to this than the individuals. On the whole, individuals were saying, “Enough is enough,” and the same goes for victims. If as a country we are saying that we value individuals and that we value their liberty over the liberty of the institutions that have carried out this abuse, then that makes me happy. The institutions that were apparently overseeing the kinds of practices that I was involved in still exist today. I echo what Jayne said about suppression. Strangely enough, I designed True Freedom Trust’s second website. They still exist. From my perspective, it is suppression.

Strangely, it was only five years ago that I recognised I was a victim. Before that, I believed it was me—“Oh, I was so silly. I shouldn’t have got involved. I shouldn’t have done this.” It was only when I watched the documentary “Pray Away” on Netflix and recognised how Exodus International, an organisation in the US, had funded organisations and church institutions in the UK, that I thought, “Hang on a minute—I was a victim.” It took me a long time to recognise that I was a victim, and even to say the word “victim”.

I can hold that word now, whereas I couldn’t have done that before. Especially when you are male, you just think, “Oh, just get over it. You can get over it. It’s fine. It’s just a bit of prayer.” But it was massively damaging for me, including when it came to suicidal thoughts. I tried to take my life and ended up in a psychiatric unit in Newport, just outside Cardiff. It took a psychiatrist to turn round to me and say, “There’s nothing wrong with being gay,” which doesn’t sound like much, but having been institutionalised for so long, it was like, “Oh, somebody’s just told me something I’ve not heard before.” I spent so much time in these institutions that I didn’t know who I was. If it wasn’t for that psychiatrist with those simple words, I probably wouldn’t be here.

I hope that demonstrates why I believe that this law is important, and why I support that 75% of respondents who were victims of what I have gone through.

Chair: I am really grateful, Ian.

Q61 Sir Jeremy Wright: Thank you both for what you have said already. You have covered some of this territory, so please do not feel the need to repeat anything you have said already. You will know that one of the things we are wrestling with is the degree to which the Bill moves beyond what the criminal law already says. I am interested to know how you would feel had the Bill as currently drafted—I take Jayne’s points about how we might improve the drafting—been on the statute book at the time at which you had the experiences you have mentioned to us. What difference do you think it would have made?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: The truth about legislation—I don’t need to tell you—is that it has two effects, the first hopefully being a behavioural change. It gives a very clear statement to society about what is expected of us, and it sets a level. What the Cooper report proposed, and what I think you are proposing, in a sense, is a mix of criminal and civil law, with protection orders for the day-to-day criminal—for those potentially one-off cases where there has been an institutional course of conduct. I do not see us prosecuting small cases—I know there is fear about parents or single religious leaders, but I hope we can step in and warn against that—but when we have repeated course of conduct by large institutions, that is where the criminal Bill will come in.

To give a slightly longer answer, I often talk about conversion practices in three phases, and the Bill really only helps with the third phase, so please bear with me. The first phase is what I call the silent phase—I do not know whether you had this too, Ian. You bear a secret that you do not dare tell anyone, because the moment you do tell them that you are suffering or struggling with your sexuality is the moment you are put on what I call the naughty step, especially when you are a teenager. In my research, over half of those going through conversion practices were under 18 and three quarters were under 24.

Your whole support basis and the power structures at home all lead you to want to stay silent, but something happens—either you are outed by a jealous rival or a sibling, or you have a breakdown, so there is a crisis point. At that point, phase 2 kicks in, which is normally—it was for me—friends and family coming alongside wanting to pray for you: “This is okay, Jayne. God loves you all the same.” That is untrained people in a loving context, coming alongside you, going through every element of your past and history to try to find the reason why you have found yourself sinning like this.

You go through that once or twice, and maybe you can put up with it, but when you have done it for 20 years with different groups of people around the world—I mean, I could have a T-shirt saying “I’ve been prayed for by nearly every evangelical leader in the world,” because I have. I was so desperate. I was a member of the Archbishops’ Council at the time, so I was quite a senior figure in the evangelical Church and I did not want anyone in the UK to know. I was going all around the world trying to find people who would pray for me. These were well-meaning people, well-intentioned, full of love—but the constant message was, “Who you are is wrong and you have to change.”

When that does not work, in my case after my first breakdown, I started to look for more serious forms, which is when I went for exorcisms or I paid a lot of money to Christian psychotherapists who could help me. These are people who trained as psychotherapists but had a religious belief, and they professed to know the way out. I must have spent thousands of pounds trying to find a professional way. This is where the Bill would kick in—arguably, it should kick in at phase 2, but that is quite difficult, I think. Phase 3 is when you are going to organisations that profess to find you a way out, and that is what we are stopping. I hope we would put “the fear” into phase 2 and prevent it from happening.

I should say that this is doubly applicable to my trans and non-binary friends. I think all the research shows that they are more likely to go through this, but in exactly the same way, with friends and family, and untrained prayer ministry counsellors. Today, we have far more safeguarding concepts, but that still does not go to the heart of this.

I probably have not answered your question. There are a lot of reasons why the current law does not go far enough. We have talked about causing harm or intent, but here it was about a loving action—the intention and negligence is not there. I do not believe the abuse was physical enough, but it was mentally tortuous. I disagree with some of the comments made last week. We do not have sufficient law in place at the moment to cover this most insidious form, which is prayer.

Chair: Ian, do you want to add anything to that?

Ian Smith: Sorry—what was the question?

Q62 Sir Jeremy Wright: We all have comments and questions about the breadth or narrowness of the drafting of this law, but if what we have on the paper in front of us had been law at the time, how would it have helped?

Ian Smith: It absolutely would have, through preventive optics. There is no doubt in my mind.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: It would have set a bar. I am not sure that the Bill would have protected me in respect of prayer, to be honest. I do not think that, as it is currently drafted, religious practices would fall at that serious harm level. I do not think the predetermined purpose, as I call it—I would have a jolly good go at trying to prosecute under it, but I do not see those every day—

Ian Smith: I am not sure about the legal thresholds, because I am not an expert on that. It is literally the optics. It is just about saying, “If you are the Association of Christian Counsellors or the Evangelical Alliance, be careful.” Yes, it would have protected me to some extent. Would it have protected me fully? I’m with Jayne on those changes.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: A lot of people hear about conversion practices through a drama or a news report, which goes for the extreme—it goes for the exorcism, which we have both gone through but is quite extreme. The vast majority—there are various research reports that will back this up—is what I call the everyday prayer counselling, which is not harmful physically but is mentally. It was in my case, and in the research we have done, two thirds said they had had suicidal thoughts and a third had attempted suicide.

People sit with the level of self-hate, self-harm and shame, believing that who they are is not acceptable, for years, not just weeks or months. I spent 20 years believing that was who I was. You might think, “Goodness, you are an Oxbridge-educated woman, you should’ve pulled yourself together,” but I truly believed, because everybody else around me believed, that this was wrong.

Ian Smith: You are talking about children as well. I remember one example in a church in Newport. I was 18 or 19, and there was a group of maybe 10 people praying over me. Most these guys’ brains have not finished developing until they are 25, and you have young children praying over other individuals. In my case, the women were sent out the room, because of course the men are the strongest—

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: The real prayer warriors.

Ian Smith: They can get rid of the demons for two hours. We are talking about developing brains. I worked in the youth justice system as a counsellor. You know and understand that adverse childhood experiences really impact people’s lives to a massive extent when they are older. If that feeds into it then yes, prayer is damaging.

Q63 Naz Shah: Thank you both very much for coming in; I really appreciate it. I have a quick supplementary question to the question that Jeremy asked. You have talked about people who have prayed and their intentions. Should the people who caused you distress ought to have known that what they were doing would harm you?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Yes, they should have known. Indeed, that is why I had a private member’s motion in the Church of England, and the Methodist Church, the Sikh faith and the Hindu faith then took it on. We now have statements from senior officials. But it still happens.

The Evangelical Alliance, which is a network of evangelical churches—not all evangelical churches believe this, but thousands of them do—talks about the need for transformation, which is code for, “You must change yourself. You must deny yourself.” I had to do a BBC One programme on Sunday and I was confronted with a vicar telling me that I needed to deny myself and change myself and that Christ had died for me. I just thought, “This is live on air. This is terrible. We’ve got to cut to that.” But that belief still exists, and it takes a lot to get out. I call it the hermetically sealed hermeneutic. You cannot argue with the mindset, because people really believe that what they are doing is of God and think, “No matter what the institution says, I’m going to do it anyway.”

I have to be honest—perhaps this is a slight segue—that I think the counselling world does not always understand religion either. When I finally found some help after my first breakdown, I was with a psychiatrist who said, “Well, Jayne, it is simple: you just have to change your religion.” I remember looking at her and thinking, “Hmm. You really don’t understand this, do you? I’m not going to speak to you again.” The fact that it involves religion makes us all want to walk on eggshells, because we don’t really want to upset anybody, but we have to recognise that, sadly, there is a dark side. It is not just Christianity; the Muslim faith and the Jewish faith have their equivalents—special waters that people need to drink or, sadly, some maybe more physical abuse. I know of too many young women who have been sent abroad to be raped in order to make themselves straight. That is why we have to have overseas orders.

Ian Smith: I know it is anecdotal, but between us we have had how much prayer—10, 20 or 30 years? This omnipotent, all-powerful God did not change our sexuality. If you are going to be in a room standing over somebody, praying for them, I wonder whether that is best done not in the presence of the person who is there, because it did not change me, unfortunately.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: It just made me feel terribly sinful. The other side of it is that it is always your fault if the prayer does not work.

Ian Smith: Yes, exactly.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Is that your experience too? You are not being honest enough with the person praying for you, or you have not got enough faith—if only you had enough faith, God would answer this. It is always your fault.

Ian Smith: It is your fault, not the institution’s and not God’s.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: There is no way out.

Chair: Thank you; that is very helpful.

Q64 Danny Beales: Thank you very much for sharing your experience. I appreciate how difficult it must be, but it is very helpful for the Committee to have your expertise.

Jayne, you were touching on some of the dilemmas that the Committee is weighing up around significant harm being a trigger point and the role of the victim. To build on what you were talking about—prayer and some people’s concerns about freedom of religion—one of the arguments of those who have drafted the Bill is that the harm measure is there to balance those freedoms and draw a line. Ian, you said, “If you’re going to pray, pray with us not in the room.” I want to delve into that a bit more—

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Where do we draw the line?

Danny Beales: Where do you think the balance of legislation should lie around religious practice in that regard? At the moment, it is around a level of harm. I am spitballing here, but the first time that someone offers to pray would probably not tip into that place, but as you say, with your experience, given the significance of the impacts on you and the impacts you have talked about, it arguably would. Jayne, would it be better if that was not in the Bill, and it covered any activity that sought to change, as in the current drafting, or to change and suppress, in your view?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: I think that all forms of conversion practices are abusive, because by their nature they are telling you that who you are is less than and that there is something wrong with you. I understand that people will hold that belief very passionately and conscientiously. The holding of that belief is perfectly within article 9 of the European convention on human rights, which is a qualified right. I don’t want to teach you, but the first part is about holding a belief. No one can change what we believe or why; that is something that we all have as individuals.

How we practice that belief—the second part; the limited part—is limited to whether it causes public disorder or harms individuals. I do not have the wording in front of me, I’m afraid, but it is a limited belief. When it causes harm, which we know this does, it should be stopped. The UN special rapporteur on religion or belief came a couple of times and ran sessions here in the House of Commons to discuss just this, and explained that he was totally behind the ban, because he recognised that the practices aimed at an individual did cause harm, but that you could hold that belief.

There are parallels, dare I say, with abortion. We have had instances of people praying around abortion clinics; you may remember the challenges we have there. Where does prayer become oppressive? If people are standing there silently with their placards, where do you draw the line? I think we need to have a common line for those issues and this one, too. When you have practices aimed at an individual where words are spoken, with the practice being the speaking, as if you are in a therapy or counselling session—a lot of prayer is really therapy wrapped up as, “I’m asking God”—that is what causes the harm.

Q65 Danny Beales: That is an interesting example. My understanding is that there is a cumulative nature to that as well, which might trigger a buffer zone. It is about not banning any prayer, but creating parameters around it.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: It is creating a safer space.

Danny Beales: In your view, should even the first instance of a prayer be criminalised?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: There is a difference between a prayer that is asking God to help you come to a point of peace and one that is asking God to make sure that you are straight. It comes back to that predetermined purpose I mentioned earlier. It is the same with therapy. Any therapy can allow you to explore who you are or can challenge who you are—that may take time; I do not think many people can do that in six weeks, because they will often change—but this is channelling you down just one avenue. “I have a belief that being gay is wrong and I am therefore going to pray for you to be straight”—that is the problem in the power dynamic. Would I prosecute after one little prayer? No; I would have a word. That is why we have the law.

Q66 Danny Beales: I guess that is my question. Having that test about harm, as you said, probably would not criminalise certain initiatives, but—

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Well, you would criminalise it, but whether you prosecute—

Ian Smith: Can you qualify who the person saying the prayers is directing them towards? Is it God or the individual that is in front of them?

Danny Beales: I guess it could be either, or both. I am just trying to tease it out.

Ian Smith: So you are calling on the higher power in order to make a change—is that right? Are you suggesting that prayer is calling on the higher power to make a change on behalf of somebody else?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: It has to be directed at a person. If I am sat in a room—

Ian Smith: So you are not calling on the intermediary.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: No. It is about the impact on the individual in front of you. A lot of prayer will be directed—somebody will have their hand on you and pray. It is the fact that they are trying to pray for you to be changed to a thing that they think you ought to be as opposed to praying for you to find a point of peace. You might say that that is too subtle a difference, but they are worlds apart: helping me come to a place where I know who I ought to be is very different from telling me that I have to be straight.

Q67 Danny Beales: I am conscious of time, and other Members want to come in. You said, “You probably wouldn’t criminalise them in this case”—

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: I wouldn’t prosecute—that is very different. The criminal line stands there, but the CPS sits on many cases working out whether they have enough evidence and whether they will prosecute. On the evidence of one prayer that nobody else witnesses, I do not think that you would be able to prosecute. It is still wrong.

We have that issue in numerous cases of rape, sadly. A lot of women get raped but they cannot prove it, and the CPS will not prosecute. This, sadly, is very similar, which is why I am trying to take the threshold down: because of the pressure on the victim. I think we will talk about why more people have not come forward, but the bar here is extraordinary—especially when you have lost everything anyway. Q70 Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Do you think the Bill includes that at the moment?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: I think it ought to. I do not think it does at the moment.

Q71 Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Why do you not think it does at the moment?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Because I think you have set the threshold so high that nobody would ever get over it.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Which threshold?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: On abusive versus non-abusive and serious harm. Your definition does not get there.

Legislation has been in place around the world for arguably eight years—Malta was first. I think this question came up last week: how many cases have we had? Hardly any, because it is very difficult to prosecute, even with legislation. The clear message has been sent to people, but the cases you are likely to prosecute are those where you have evidence to prove beyond reasonable doubt that there has been a course of conduct. That is likely to be in a large institution where a number of people come forward and say, “This is what I went through at this person’s hands.”

That is why I am not so worried about parents—although I know others are. I am afraid, having met a lot of the survivors, that there are some parents who have thrown their children out of the home, organised for them to be raped, beaten them and locked them in cupboards. Those parents should be brought to justice. A parent sitting there having a conversation with their child and helping them come to a point of peace is very different. I have known too many parents in this context who are so ashamed of their children being gay or transgender that they will do anything, including sending them abroad without their consent, to try to sort them out.

It all comes down to shame. Personally, I think that is a huge driver: shame for the parent, shame for the individual, shame of not fitting into a culture or society that they think they should belong to. No Bill is going to change that; I recognise that—that is part of a progressive society. However, we have to recognise what keeps people silent and what motivates them. Certainly in my research, in a study of 460 people who had been through it, 72% believed it was sinful and 63% were ashamed of their desires. That shame is what drives so many people, and that is, I think, what we are up against with this Bill.

Chair: That point about challenges segues quite neatly to Lord Hope, and then I will bring in Baroness Browning.

Q72 Lord Hope of Craighead: Your evidence is fascinating and extremely helpful, but I would like to move on to a slightly different topic. When it comes to criminalising behaviour, the prosecutor needs to have evidence. What challenges do victims and survivors face in coming forward, knowing that they are likely to be approached by the police, who might say, “Can you give us the evidence, please? If we have the evidence, we will prosecute. If you won’t give us the evidence, we can’t”? Can you help us on that? The Bill uses the criminal law as a mechanism for the prohibition, but you have to have a mechanism that will work.

Ian Smith: On the general level, it is not necessarily legislative; it is about highly controlling religions, victims’ ability to come forward and, for example, the collection of evidence—or the lack of it. Obviously the fear of not being believed by people is a massive one, as is the distrust of the police and authorities to deal with this. I was working in the youth justice system, dealing in certain cases with people who worked as sex workers and were raped. Coming up with the evidence to support their situation was obviously really difficult. In this case, to suggest that we have evidence of the threshold for suppressive practices or prayer is not exactly easy.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: I have talked about shame, but the other issue is power differentials. When people have been subjected to this, it has normally been an abuse of power—someone in authority who they have either trusted or been forced in front of. More often than not, that is a parental figure, a religious leader or some other person who believes they can heal them. Distrust of power and authority is therefore built into you, because it has caused you so much harm. The brave souls who have come forward often are not understood or believed: “Oh, it’s just a bit of prayer, get over it.”

We have that double whammy—that is the starting point—but the real issue for most of the victims I have worked with is that it has taken them years. They have often reached adulthood, fallen in love and decided to take the plunge and have a relationship, and they have therefore been cut off by their friends and family and lost their social group and the social crucible that brought them up. If they have tried to rebuild those relationships, the very last thing they want to do is go to a prosecutor or the media and start pushing or speaking out. That is why it has been so hard to have people who are willing to speak on this—perhaps like ourselves—because the cost of speaking out, particularly for family relationships, is very high.

Q73 Lord Hope of Craighead: If I can interrupt, it is a question of how we can help you. The trouble is that you have to prove that the practice was performed, that the victim suffered that practice, that it was abusive and, finally, that it caused the victim serious harm. In New Zealand, that last point is not required, because the assumption is that in children under 18 the practice itself will be seriously harmful. Is that helpful?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Yes, very helpful. All these practices are harmful, whether or not you then have to go on and prove it. Sadly, the truth is that we find out when someone is in hospital, having taken an overdose, or when they have died. Even then the parents do not often admit that it is because they were gay. All the friends of the individual who has taken their life know, but the shame element means that it does not come forward.

What tends to happen—I do not know whether this is your experience, Ian, so I cannot speak for you—is that you are going through this with your peers, who are perhaps also suffering in this way, and you all then reach an age where you can speak up for each other. The proof is the witness—I prayed for you, you prayed for me, we went through all this together. That is why we are hearing about it a lot more: because people our age—I am a bit older than Ian, I think—are finding the courage to speak out.

You are right about taking away the harm threshold. In Scotland and Northern Ireland, instead of “harm”, I think they have talked about the likely risk—

Lord Hope of Craighead: Yes, they talk about risk.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: I think that is much more helpful. I think we can all see the risk, but if the only evidence you have is a dead child—I am sorry to put it very bluntly—that is too far. The evidence is likely to be someone witnessing someone being prayed for, but that requires both the victim and the person to come out, unless you have hidden cameras, which my dear friend Paul Brand from ITV was able to get evidence with, but that takes a lot of planning.

Lord Hope of Craighead: Thank you very much; I had better leave it there.

Chair: Thank you. We have a hard stop coming up, so I will call Baroness Browning and then Lord Isaac. I would say that we have eight minutes.

Q74 Baroness Browning: Thank you both very much for coming and sharing what you have told us this afternoon—we appreciate it very much. You have mentioned the CPS and the fact that very little gets through under existing law. Take it back a step from the CPS. Why do you think law enforcement at the more basic level has not been more active?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: They have not had the protection orders they could use, for example. I think the recommendation in the Cooper report actually came from work with social workers, Galop and the police, but there is a huge lack of understanding. Anything to do with religion in particular becomes a bit of a taboo area. I have run educational training sessions in the NHS; I know some of my colleagues have done so in the police. Here, we are looking at banning conversion therapy, but if we want to end it, that requires a package of education, particularly for our police, law enforcers, social workers, NHS and teachers, so that they can recognise it. There is a lot more understanding in society today than there was eight years ago, and that is thanks to the fact that we are touching on this a lot more, so people are more aware, but I do think an education package is required in order to help people see the signs.

Baroness Browning: Thank you. Ian, do you want to add anything to that?

Ian Smith: No; I’m good.

Q75 Lord Isaac: Thank you for being so open. I want just to reinforce the comments that my colleagues have made. Obviously, nobody wants a death, a suicide or to be in a position where abuse is being promulgated, but some stakeholders have told us that they are concerned about the chilling effect of legislation on exploratory conversations with parents, genuine pastoral support and medical counselling. Tell us what your views are on a potential chilling effect.

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Yes, I have heard those concerns. I think we are one of the few nations in the world in which such vocal concerns have been raised. There has been no evidence of that happening in other countries, but it comes back again to the definition. Having healthy, challenging conversations, particularly with young people, and pushing back, is absolutely right and proper. I come back to this phrase, “predetermined purpose”: when your mindset is made up, when the perpetrator’s mindset is the one that will not change. That is where the problem lies, because that is forcing someone down a path; it might be the path they choose at the end of the day, but it is forcing them down a path they have no option but to follow. That is why we need to come back to the definition.

Law around parents is difficult. We had a very similar debate around the smacking ban. Is it right to chastise your child? At the end of the day, the child has rights as much as the parent, and our job as legislators is to protect that child more than anything. I do think it is possible to protect children and to give parents leeway but to know where the line is, and when that parent is saying that the child can never be gay—“No son of mine will be gay” or “No daughter of mine will be trans”—that is code for many children to choose to run away and to find different ways of being, but they know that home is not a safe place.

Q76 Lord Isaac: Do you agree that exploratory conversations that are not focused on a predetermined negative outcome would be acceptable?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: Absolutely. I do think there is a misunderstanding of what affirmative care models mean in the therapeutic community. That does not mean that they just say yes to the child. They challenge completely, but it is done in a way in which the child recognises they are safe. That is the whole change in counselling. I hope you might call in for evidence the chair of the memorandum of understanding group, which brought together all the major health professionals to sign a memorandum saying that conversion practices were wrong. It has done a lot of work on this, but how you cope with someone in front of you who is trying to come to a point of peace is a journey you need to take them on, and you do not do it by closing a door in their mind beforehand.

Q77 Lord Isaac: Would you accept that, in terms of legislation and how it is drafted, it is quite difficult to capture what you are describing?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: It is, which is why we should learn from other countries that have gone before us, and I fear that we have gone right back and tried to go in a very different direction. There has been a lot of work: the UN has tried to help people learn, as has the Council of Europe and the EU. We should be following suit, not just trying to do something different. I am sorry; I do think that we have gone off down a rabbit hole that is not helpful to us.

Q78 Naz Shah: Thank you both very much—that was really helpful. Ian, you talked about young people and their brains not being fully formed until they are adults. Jayne, following on from that, you talked about children’s perspective and said that the Bill is drafted too widely. Bearing in mind what you have both said—I am genuinely trying to understand this—if an 11-year-old child wants to buy a breast-binding belt and the parent says no, that seems contradictory. On the one hand, that person is saying no and is imposing their view, saying, “I am not going to do this.” On the other hand, they might be criminalised and will still fall into that. Do you think that could then be termed as abuse?

Deputy Jayne Ozanne: I think it is so difficult to talk about in the abstract. I am sorry to say this, but it is very difficult because it is about a complete context, isn’t it? Is the child just pushing boundaries or is there a real issue? Most trans children I am aware of have known from the very get-go, and their parents deep down have known too, from the moment the child has picked up a toy.
