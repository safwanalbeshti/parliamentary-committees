Ed Lennox: Good morning, everybody. I am operations director of Clean Up Britain, which is the only organisation in the UK focused on littering and fly-tipping. While we have our roots in littering and fly-tipping there is a danger for us in scope creep. When you start looking into the issues that surround that you find the related issues of lack of maintenance, lack of care, and the appalling slide in standards that we are allowing to surround us. We are becoming immune to seeing falling-down signs, signs that you cannot read, foliage overgrowing, weeds growing out of the pavements and so on. That has really led us into quite a deep analysis of how dysfunctional the state is. I do not want to be pejorative about the way in which tax money is spent creates structures, layers and layers of bureaucracy and legislative opacity between who is responsible for what. But, in the end, if you wanted to design a system that was not going to deliver a well-maintained public realm, we have it.

I want to make it clear that when you actually start to realise what the problem is there is a danger that people perceive that you have some appalling political agenda, and we have none. We just want to see the job done. In too many cases, the job is not being done, and in too many cases the money that is spent to do the job is being spent and allocated by people who simply have no understanding, experience or ability to rationally determine whether that is good value for money or not.

Clean Up Britain specialises in shining a light on what the problems are, and we have some very high-level recommendations. In the end, there is no connection, as there was certainly 50 years ago, between a local problem and a named local individual who had pride in their work delivering good value for money to get that problem fixed. We aim to go some way to getting back to that level where accountability and responsibility are vested in local people, with fewer layers of bureaucracy. At the governmental scale, there are also too many agencies involved in this. That is the problem.

Patricia Brown: I wanted to ask you about that. I was thinking about the sense of where the whole of the realm is not in a private space; whether that is our home, a school or inside a commercial building. I think of where we congregate, interact, live our lives and walk to places as our public realm and public space. That also goes to things like playgrounds. It is wherever we can spend time and need to be in order to go about our daily business, and that is roads as well as what lots of people think as actual public space, which is a little pocket park or a little pavement area that has been designed and thought about. I think deeply about this and I have created an initiative called London 3.0. I witnessed and actually worked on the improvement of public space and getting that concerted management, whether that is through business improvement districts or other measures, to have that ownership of the public realm, to think and to worry about the details, and to enforce the things that need enforcing.

Several things happened: it was going up Maslow’s hierarchy of needs and then place actualisation. We really did incredibly well. Then along came austerity and budgets were cut dramatically. Attention turned away from those small things that Ed has especially talked about, and a whole range of things such as the way we run waste services, which I am sure Ed is expert in. It is bonkers that we have a system where people take their rubbish and put it on the streets. That rubbish could be full of coffee grinds and grease. We expect people to negotiate their daily lives around binbags that are leaking rubbish and, in some cases, toxic fumes on to our public realm. Then we expect the highways authority or the local authority to pay to clean them.

We need a fundamental rethink and a fresh look. The reason I call this London 3.0 is because we made all these changes and we went up, but now we are going down. In the face of many changes, including urban innovation such as hire bikes, dockless bikes and delivery drone things that are delivering to people in some parts—a whole series of things that are taking space—the private sector is actually monetising our public space. Local authorities and business improvement districts are trying to keep up with that.

Then there is a cultural aspect, which is that you need to want to care about these places and you need to have ownership and a sense of care. Much of this comes down to having people who do not sweat the small stuff in our built environment. It is really important to focus on the details and to actually have a management structure and a funding structure that allows people to be on the streets to enforce these things. We need that and a cultural change in the public. Much of that has gone wrong since the 2000s, from what I call the takeaway/leave-behind culture. If you go to Venice, where there is only one McDonald’s, you will not see a Caffè Nero cup and somebody’s cigarette packet sat on somebody else’s window ledge. Why have we created a culture that enables the public to think that it is okay to take something away and leave it behind? I have more to say, but I am going to leave it there.

Ed Lennox: The built environment in the public realm has deteriorated in the opinion of 77% of people. I am not going to start referring to notes here as I can furnish them later. All the research shows that the perception is that the public realm is disintegrating in front of our very eyes. In fact, there is evidence even across Lambeth Bridge, if any of you want to go and look at it. I dare say there are different agencies responsible for different parts of that bridge. If you walk along the roadside of it, rather than swim underneath it, the roadside is quite well painted. But, if you look at the side of it and underneath it, I can almost guarantee, just by looking at it, it has not had any maintenance done on it from the riverside and from the underside for at least 15 years. It has faded and there is rust showing. We have completely lost our ability to live by the old adage of “a stitch in time saves nine”, which makes me sound incredibly old, but it is true. Preservation is better than cure. We have lost that.

As Patricia said just now—I cannot remember the exact words, forgive me, but somebody will have a record—

Patricia Brown: The takeaway/leave-behind culture?

Ed Lennox: We have that, but we are not investing in trying to create the culture that we want through our children. We do not have public service broadcasting anymore. We do not have message reinforcement that littering is disgusting and unacceptable; we have therefore become tolerant of litter piling up everywhere and it has become normalised. Until we realise that we have to invest in society, we will not have the public realm that we want to see. We have a laissez-faire attitude of, “Oh, we think everybody will behave right”. They do not.

It is a criminal offence to drop litter. It is a criminal offence to fly-tip. There were 1.3 million fly-tips in the last full year for which there is reporting available, and fewer than 1,500 prosecutions, most of which were not successful. The average fine was £600, which does not even go close to addressing the issue. The point is that you have almost no chance of being caught doing the wrong thing, none at all. The penalties are tiny, and we do not invest in doing the right thing.

Until we actually grasp that nettle and are honest with ourselves, we are going to live in an increasingly decrepit, ugly, dirty, filthy, embarrassing country. That is not to say that there are no areas which are beautiful. Around here is a lot better than most, let me tell you. The thing that really bothers me most about the built environment is that we have inherited in this country some incredibly beautiful structures from our forebears, who built things to last a very long time. Stone bridges are a particular passion of mine. I do not accept this austerity thing, because most councils are terrible at spending money and I can give you examples of it. They have no clue how to negotiate a contract properly and get good value for money. There are endless examples of that in Clean Up Britain, but stone bridges are one that really bothers me, being a particular passion of mine.

If the roads are not swept, the debris, detritus, leaf mulch and what have you accumulates on the side of the road. It grows deeper and deeper. When it gets to a certain depth, trees can grow in it and they do. I could take you to at least 50 bridges around the country where there are trees growing out of stone bridges with roots going into the beds of those bridges. These are things that we have inherited, and we are absolutely failing to look after them on a grand scale. We know the cost of everything and the value of absolutely nothing. If you buy cheap, you pay twice, and we are probably paying four or five times, because we will have to rebuild a lot of the environment.

David Milner: It is not that that is not important, but we have experts on that here. To your first question, yes—there is massive regional differentiation. When you look at highways authorities, for example, they see a lot of these nice things we are talking about—the bench—as a liability. An example where I have seen that improved would be Lincolnshire.
