Tracy Gilbert: Welcome. Lindy, what do you see as the biggest challenges facing the FCDO’s development agenda?

Lindy Cameron: The biggest challenge is simply that the world is more contested, more complicated and more conflict-ridden than it was when I was last in the Department. I should say I joined the civil service into DFID as a junior governance adviser back in 1998. I intended to stay for two years and have stayed for 26, so it is an absolute joy to be back, not just here in front of the IDC, but in the Department responsible for development, but I think the world is more complex now. Also, to be frank, what has changed is that what countries want from us has changed. In a funny way it takes me back to an earlier period in my development career, thinking about the time of budget support, the time of the “voices of the poor” work that the World Bank led around the year 2000, and thinking about how countries really expressed what they wanted from us. The context has changed, development has changed and resources have changed. You cannot not notice or mention that, and reduced aid obviously affects what we do. In some ways, what I want is consolidation, where development professionals within the organisation feel valued for their skills and expertise, can see their career paths as part of a future in the Department and feel equally valued with other colleagues. Lindy Cameron: It is not really for me to comment on the last few years as I was not in the Department. Nick Dyer: We have gone through so much change over the last few years, and it is probably fair to say that our voice and influence has perhaps not been as strong as it was in the last 10 to 15 years. Lindy Cameron: The vision I laid out for FCDO is a high-performance organisation with high trust in its staff. It is one that is high tech and equipped with the technology to do our jobs, and to do the most exciting and interesting version of our jobs. It is one with high talent, where we have the best talent for the jobs that we need for today, and for the careers that we need for the future in the longer term. It is one right at the heart of Whitehall.

I think the transformation outcome that I am looking for is not dissimilar, and I would not disagree with the analysis that was laid out. When I look back, the organisation a couple of years ago was bigger than DFID and FCO were on the day of the merger. It had become quite top-heavy, so we had a lot of SCS and grade 6 staff. There were many brilliant experts, but it was nevertheless crowding out some of our brilliant junior staff.

One of the things I have done with the Foreign Secretary is really encourage our staff to think about how they can be bolder, faster and more creative, and how they can take more risk. It is about how we can shift the culture of the organisation, which is one of the things that I want. It feels like the most junior person who is the brilliant expert in something should be the person who can brief the Foreign Secretary, and who can generate the ideas quickly to deal with the issues that we have today.

The intent to transform the organisation is similar, and I do not disagree with the analysis of some of the challenges that we have faced. It is really important that we take our staff with us on the journey, and the trust point really matters to me. A lot of what I have done in the last few weeks has been really listening to staff to understand what it has been like for them. Obviously, those of us who were overseas were at some distance from that, so I have been really empathising with UK-based staff, in particular.

We think that we have 17% less UK-based staff in the UK today than in March 2025, which is about 18 months ago. You are right that there have been some changes in country-based staff and UK-based staff overseas, but the most significant change has intentionally been in the UK.

I also have to say that, having personally spent much of my life in the network—I was DG country programmes, so I ran the DFID network overseas for four years—I very firmly believe in what our brilliant staff do overseas, and in being really close to the partners and customers who they work with. I have just come from an India network, and it had the most extraordinary range of country-based staff, many of whom have been with us for decades in different roles. I believe in our staff, whether they are country-based staff, or whether they are experts in everything from finance and HR to education, health and lots of other things. I hope that we can carry on the journey with higher levels of trust.

I also want us to accelerate the journey on technology, which I think is really important. I am acutely conscious, having been the CEO of the National Cyber Security Centre, how fast technology is moving. Nick has probably heard me say this about a million times, but what I say to our teams and staff is, “This will change in the course of your current job. This is not a next-job issue or future career issue. This is something that you should be doing.”

I cannot remember the numbers, but we are lucky that more than 80% of our current staff are using AI today in their jobs, and more than 70% think it is helpful in their jobs, and that it is helping them to do the most interesting end of their jobs. We are really lucky to have an increasingly good set of in-house tech staff, which is again one of the changes, to help equip us to do that really well.

That way, we can make sure that, frankly, the staff we have are doing the most exciting and interesting end of their jobs. The other bit I want is that they then feel that they have exciting careers in the organisation. I think this is consistent with Antonia Romeo’s wider vision for the civil service. I am not sure that people will necessarily have a 60-year career in the civil service, but I want to see staff who have everything from a two or three-year period of time to a 30 or 40-year period of time. People will make different choices about whether that is expert-led, or led by an aspiration to be managers and leaders—a bit like me—but I want them to feel like they have those choices. I want them to feel not only like they have a level of agency but that we are clear as an organisation about what we need.

To your point about expertise, I have already asked us to review quite carefully what staff we still have in the redeployment pool, because we are coming towards the end of this period of significant change in the UK. I have asked for a review of who we have in the redeployment pool, to make sure we understand what key skills we could lose in that last phase because I think that really does matter. I am conscious there are critical areas of expertise which if we want to shift, we need to shift consciously. With the combination of a change in the structure in the UK—we completely restructured the directorates; I think that was a well thought through exercise—and the simultaneous shifting of allocations to country offices, and therefore structures of teams overseas, it is important we do that consciously together, and make sure the cumulative expertise we have across the whole organisation adds up to the right expertise for now and the right building blocks for the long term. This is something I am quite passionate about, as you can imagine, so there is a lot there.

Nick Dyer: I have been on record in this Committee saying two things: first, how important expertise is for the organisation and our development work and secondly, that the skills, the shape and the number of our development expertise is going to change over the next three years because the nature of work is changing. Lindy Cameron: We have done some re-skilling on the digital side. We have opened one of the digital schemes to people who want to move across the organisation, because that is a potential growth area. That is an area where people can re-skill and re-train.

Lindy Cameron: Highly indebted poor countries.

Two things are different now. One is that the nature of the debt is different. Rather than official creditors, as it was in 2000, you now have a lot more private creditors and you have a lot of Chinese debt, which did not happen before.

Secondly, you need to distinguish between two things. Back in 2000, it was a solvency problem. Basically, countries had too much debt and it wasn’t sustainable; they needed debt restructuring. Basically, they needed debt forgiveness. By contrast, now it is more a liquidity problem. The size of the debt is not a problem, but it is the payment they have got to make on it—the interest rates they are being charged on the debt—and they have a liquidity problem. They are basically spending more on debt service than they are on health and education in many cases, and in some cases they are spending about 40% or 50% of their public revenue on debt service.

The question there is: how can you refinance debt that is high-cost and short-maturity into longer-maturity and lower-cost debt? There are ways you can do that, but to do it you have to understand the creditor mix and you really have to understand who is offering the debt and at what terms. You really have to understand that creditor data set.

Then there is the question of who is going to finance it. There are options from the IMF and the World Bank, which could fund it. Then there is the question of what the expectations of countries are in return. Basically, there is a moral hazard problem here. We did debt relief in 2000, so why are we doing it again?
