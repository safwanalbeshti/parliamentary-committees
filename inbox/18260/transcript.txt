Chair: Welcome to today’s meeting of the Science and Technology Committee. This is the final evidence session in our neuroscience and digital childhoods inquiry. Paul Waugh: I am Minister for Early Education. Emily, you asked the right question, on the different impacts on brain development and physical development. On brain development, it is worth saying—as others have already, and as you have heard in your evidence—that the evidence base is quite thin in terms of a causal link between screen use and cognitive development, and brain development more generally. That does not mean we accept that; it is precisely why we are doing so much research, certainly within DfE and other Departments, to get that evidence. We ordered a systematic review of all the causal evidence out there. There is not much of it, but we are doing a systematic review of what exists at the moment.

As already mentioned, on the nought to fives we have taken a precautionary principle anyway. That was informed by some of the evidence we have so far about the impact on attention span, sociability and emotional development. Particularly at that vulnerable age of nought to five, we thought it best to be precautionary. That advice was worked up with parents and teachers—crucially, with people in early years settings. It proved very popular, as Ian said, because parents now finally have some clear guidance on nought to fives. For example, do not use a screen at all for an under two; if you do, it must be alongside a parent, looking at a video album, e-book or something like that. From two to five, it is one hour a day maximum, again alongside an adult. “One’s your lot if you’re a tot,” is the phrase to remember if you are a parent of a two to five-year-old. That is quite helpful advice for parents. Again, the evidence base is thin for that, but we were being extra-precautionary about it. That is in terms of brain and cognitive development.

On physical development, we can go on about this further later but the key point is about displacement. In other words, time is not infinite. Time spent on a screen means time not doing other things—for example, being outdoors or doing physical exercise. We do not need a huge amount of causal, detailed evidence to prove that. It is a fact. In terms of displacement, we know all the advantages for children’s development of physical education and social interaction—face-to-face with parents, carers and peers. As a Department, we really want to stress those developmental benefits and promote them as much as we can. We can talk about that in greater detail, but we are completely across those two bits of brain development and physical development.

Michael Thomas: I am chief scientific adviser at the Department for Education. I will just stress what my colleagues have said: the evidence base at the moment is weak in terms of causal evidence on actual effects. We have lots of observational association evidence. We are very keen to collect new causal evidence. At DfE, we have a suite of research and development studies to collect that evidence. That includes randomised controlled trials. For example, we have a new trial starting up that delays when smartphones are given to primary-age children, to see the effect of what happens when a child receives a smartphone.

Looking specifically at causal evidence, we have systematic reviews of the effects on children of social media and screen use, and of AI use on cognition and learning. We have longitudinal studies following up cohorts of different-aged children, tracking their technology use and seeing what the associations are downstream. We have developmental research looking at brain mechanisms about when children use AI and how it changes their engagement. We have real-world evaluation of educational technologies. So, there is a suite of research and development. I stress that we have a challenge in that sometimes the technology is changing faster than we are able to do the research. We are very active in keeping up to date on the latest emerging findings while we still carry out this longer-run research on the causal evidence.
