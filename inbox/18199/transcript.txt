Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Anthony Breach, Bernard Donoghue and Alistair Handyside.

Q87 Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Committee. Today, we are holding our second evidence session on tourism. For our first panel, I welcome Anthony Breach, director of research and policy at the Centre for Cities, Bernard Donoghue OBE, director of the Association of Leading Visitor Attractions, and Alistair Handyside MBE, executive chair of the Professional Association of Self-Caterers UK. Gentlemen, thank you very much for joining us this morning. It is really good to have you all with us. Before we begin, I need to remind Members to declare any interests at the point they ask a question.

I will kick off. To start with you, Bernard, we hear lots of discussion about the Government’s proposed overnight visitor levy. There are very strong feelings both for and against it, including on this panel. What problem do you think it is attempting to solve? Will it be the most effective way of addressing that?

Bernard Donoghue: I think it is attempting to solve three distinct problems. The first is the paucity of local government finances over several decades. Local authorities have seen a failure to fund not only their statutory services properly but also the voluntary services—those non-statutory functions such as tourism and culture. This presents an opportunity for new cash to local authorities and to regional mayors.

The second is, I suppose, precedent, in that many countries overseas, and indeed many cities, have this model to get more money back into their own visitor economy. Some are more successful at this than others. For example, when it was proposed that Amsterdam would have a city tax scheme, all the money was to be hypothecated for the visitor economy, yet Visit Amsterdam, the tourist board, has never seen a penny. There is a difference between intention and delivery.

I suppose the third is that all of us in the visitor economy from whom you are going to hear today are nothing if not realists. We realise, particularly in light of tomorrow’s Budget, that the overnight visitor levy is likely to be enacted, with a push to devolution across England in particular. Particularly from our perspective at ALVA, we are keen to see any new proposal made as functional, efficient and effective as possible, for not only the broader economy but the visitor economy as well.

Q88 Chair: Thank you. Alistair, do you agree that those are the problems that the Government are setting out to solve through the overnight visitor levy? Do you think it will achieve that?

Alistair Handyside: I broadly agree with Bernard, although I hope that questions on the method of delivery, how the money is spent and how it is collected will be asked during this session. That is where the real challenges lie.

Q89 Chair: Thank you. Anthony, do you have anything to add?

Anthony Breach: Thank you, Chair, for the question. I recognise a lot of the issues that other members of the panel have suggested. They are clearly part of the wider context for this reform. I think the core of the argument that the Government are making for this is that it is a step in their programme of devolution to improve both local and national economic growth. If you look internationally, we are an exceptionally centralised rich country for our size. We are by far the most centralised country in the G7, and only about 5% of our taxes are collected at local level compared to about 14% in France and 24% in Japan—both places that have a visitor levy. The implication is that that then means that local government has few incentives to grow its local economy. If it increases the size of its tax base, creates more jobs and increases wages, ultimately that all flows up to Whitehall and does not increase resource for local government in particular. That adds up to quite a big national problem, as well as being an individual local problem.

Our expectation and hope is that the visitor levy is an important first step in moving towards a more typical local finance system similar to those we see in other countries. I think for the purposes of this debate and Committee, one important implication is that we need the visitor levy to be as pro-growth as possible and to provide strong incentives for mayors to support the visitor economy and increase the number of visitors in their cities.

Q90 Chair: That is really helpful. Bernard, I go back to you. When you set out the objectives and certain problems that you thought this levy was attempting to solve, there was not a positive economic case among them. It was all a degree of political inevitability about giving strategic local authorities this power. Apart from political support, do you see there being a positive economic case in the same way that Anthony set out, and that that could be a good reason for introducing this levy?

Bernard Donoghue: Yes, potentially. Tourism is the UK’s fifth biggest industry and third largest employer. It creates more jobs at all skill levels more quickly than any other sector in this country and is the third largest employer in every UK constituency. If you were planning to organise a growth strategy for the fifth biggest industry, you would not start where we are now. We do not have a Government tourism strategy. Despite one having been written for the last two and a half years, it has not been published. There have been three examples in the last 10 months of policies emanating from No. 10 and the Treasury affecting tourism, but they are all completely unlinked to each other and they have been created in isolation from the tourism strategy.

Q91 Chair: Which are?

Bernard Donoghue: First, the Treasury proposal to charge overseas visitors to enter our free national museums and collections. That has now been vetoed, wisely. The second is the overnight visitor levy. The third was the decision to cut VAT for visitor attractions over the course of the summer. Now, if you had a normal tourism strategy, they would all be elements of that, but we do not, so they were all determined in isolation from each other. That is no way to grow Britain’s fifth biggest industry.

Coming back to your particular points, yes, if an overnight visitor levy is introduced with care, consultation and caution, the money emanating from it, if hypothecated and reinvested back into the visitor economy, could be transformational.

Q92 Chair: Alistair, we heard a number of reasons why your fellow witnesses think that a levy could be a good idea. PASC UK opposes the levy in principle. What evidence would you need to hear to change your opinion on that?

Alistair Handyside: I will again pick up a couple of things that Bernard said. We do not have a visitor economy growth strategy. We have a situation where domestic rural and coastal tourism are in significant decline, with an almost 30% reduction in overnight stays since 2022. Introducing a levy into those areas will have a disproportionately different impact than if you introduce it into cities and metro areas, where visitor economies are growing. This is why we are absolutely adamant that robust economic impact assessments must be made. If you ask the same questions of different areas, you will get different answers. For us, the No. 1 ask is that we do not skirt this and go through too quickly, as we did in Scotland and Wales. We must have a proper, robust economic impact assessment and political analysis of this before implementation.

Q93 Nigel Huddleston: I refer honourable Members to my declaration in the Register of Members’ Interests. I am also a former tourism Minister, I used to work in the sector and I go back quite a long way with pretty much all the panellists we have here today in terms of interactions. I should also declare that Alistair organised a trip with PASC for me down to the south-west in my shadow Secretary of State role earlier in the year. I am also an active member of multiple APPGs. I hope that that means I know what I am talking about here. Let me start off with my view and then come to you.

I think this is a ridiculous idea. I have yet to see any evidence whatever that you can support an industry by taxing it more. That is my point of view, but if it is going to go ahead—Bernard, I think you are right that it will be in the Budget tomorrow—we all want to make sure it is done in the most helpful or least destructive way. To that extent, there has been a debate, a consultation and lots of views and opinions about how the levy should come in, whether it should be x pounds per person per night, or a percentage levy. First, what would you like to see? What would your preferred option be?

Bernard Donoghue: I suppose that there are two answers to that. In our submission, we put that it should be a percentage rate so that it would be a progressive taxation, if you like. However, the easiest to administer would be a flat rate of x pounds—so, something like £4 or £5—but that would be disproportionate, according to whether you stay at Claridge’s or a Travel Inn. Our preference would be a progressive rate.

Q94 Nigel Huddleston: Alistair, what is your view?

Alistair Handyside: Absolutely. I keep deferring to Bernard here. It is so regressive to have a system of per person per room per place per night. Our pricing varies hugely from summer to winter. We are trying to build winter. A percentage goes down as an amount over the cost, whereas a flat rate stays up and can become enormous. For example, in Wales, it went from a 3% or 4% tax to a 20% tax. The regressive nature of a per rate is less favourable for us.

Q95 Nigel Huddleston: The other element is that we are talking about and focused a lot on tourism here, but the reality is that this will be imposed on all commercial accommodation types—absolutely everything. It will therefore impact not just tourism but business travel, visiting friends and relatives, carers staying overnight to look after relatives, and absolutely everything. Do any of you have a comment on this? Do you think it is right? Will it help the economy?

Bernard Donoghue: I do not think that you can distinguish between different constituencies or demographic types. To come back to your first point, again if you were organising the growth strategy of Britain’s fifth-biggest industry, you would acknowledge the fact that UK tourism plc at the moment is internationally uncompetitive. We are one of the most-taxed destinations in the world. This just adds to that tax base. It makes us uncompetitive. Also we have seen that, when successive Governments have added tax burdens, customers have responded. For example, we no longer have tax-free shopping. That is an act of economic self-harm. The quicker that this Government reintroduces that, the better. We have VAT at 20%. It is often argued that we should have an overnight visitor levy because so many other European countries in particular do as well, but they do not have 20% on their tourism economy. The vast majority have either 10% on their tourism economy, including accommodation, or less than that.

Q96 Nigel Huddleston: Or, indeed, it is a deliberate strategy to reduce over-tourism, which is exactly the point. I know some colleagues will come in on affordability and the details of the mechanics later on, so we will park some of that for the moment. Alistair, looking not so much at who will pay and the arrangements over the imposition of the admin—my colleagues will come to that—how will it really be covered? Is it possible in this day and age where we often have packaging, all-inclusive or B&B, to say which bit is the accommodation versus which is not? Will this not just cause confusion and difficulty for those administering it?

Alistair Handyside: With any of these tax systems, I have great sympathy when they impact one sector more than others. If you have food and beverages, how do you separate those? I understand all those challenges. The way I would look at it is that, at the end of the day, the consumer is less worried about how something is componentised—I made up that word. They are looking for what the price is. With the CMA rulings, we have to show a final price, so it will become somewhat invisible.

We have analysed our data very closely for the last three years, and prices have not gone up by inflation. In 2028, if you in Government say to us that we must add 5% to our business costs, because that is what it will be, and we want to cover inflation, we will be trying to put up our prices by almost 10%. I would defy any of you in this room to find a business that can put up its prices by 10% in a year. This will end up being paid and absorbed by the businesses. This is a business tax, make no mistake. Before profit, before anybody takes any money out of it, it is a business tax. Business taxes will hit investment, jobs and local economies.

Q97 Nigel Huddleston: Of course, there are many arguments as well, such as in the submissions, for exemptions for very small pitches where you have tents, and those kinds of things. That creates real complexity in the implementation of a tax system there, but I know that that will all be considered. Bernard, you mentioned as well that we are already one of the highest taxed countries in the world for tourism, which I think is beyond question.

I have a question for all of you, and I will make sure Anthony has an opportunity to answer here. Bernard, you articulated that there is no clear strategy. There are different Government Departments, responsible for different things. The taxation element has the business Department involved. There is DCMS involvement, the Cabinet Office coming in and No. 10’s views and opinions all the time. Do you think this speaks, across Governments—let us be fair—to not understanding and not prioritising tourism as the important industry that you have said it is?

Bernard Donoghue: Yes, absolutely. I will add to that. It has just been clarified for us that the overnight visitor levy would also be subject to VAT. Ultimately, you would have a tax on a tax on a tax on a payment. That makes no sense whatever.

To go a little further, I first appeared before this Select Committee 29 years ago, when DCMS and indeed the Select Committee were first created. In those 29 years that this Committee and its predecessor Committees have been standing, we have had 26 tourism Ministers. It is not just a case of not having joined-up government; there is also a really strong case here for no corporate memory about the importance of tourism across all Government Departments.

Anthony Breach: I recognise a lot of the frustrations in the comments made by other members of the panel. You see this kind of churn not just in tourism but other policy areas as well. One thing to reflect on in the context of devolution is that this is being devolved to mayors, who will be responsible for their place, and will take a place-first approach to the implementation of this levy. I think that should help resolve some of the problems presented by members of this panel. The mayors are all very conscious of the impact of policies that they make on jobs, investment and the economy in their local area. If this becomes the single most important revenue stream to them, that will focus minds even more.

Alistair Handyside: I just think this is the wrong tax at the wrong time. I appreciate that you can see support for it in metro areas, where the scale would be dynamic, but Manchesterism does not work very well in east Devon. It really does not translate in the scale we would raise. My final worry is that I worked with Nigel as tourism Minister before, during and after covid when we were trying to move business out of London. That has been Government policy for 20 years that I have known about. We are about to give London the biggest marketing budget over all the small counties around the country. Who wins—Visit London or Visit East Devon? That is just to give you an example.

Q98 Jo Stevens: I just want to follow up with one question. What you have all said came across very clearly in terms of there being a big difference whether you are a city or outside cities. Let us take for the moment that this is going to happen and it works in cities. Bernard, you said it needs functionality, efficiency and effectiveness. Is there a stand-out city anywhere, not necessarily just in the UK but elsewhere, that you think does this really well? Where would you point to?

Bernard Donoghue: I would point to San Francisco. There, the money is well administered. San Francisco also has a statutory registration scheme for accommodation, which is crucial. That is a first step for the implementation of something like this. If we do not know who is providing paid accommodation, we do not know who to tax. They have a statutory registration scheme, and they also go one step further: they hypothecate the moneys. It is 100% ringfenced to be reinvested back into San Francisco’s culture, heritage, nature and transport. I know that the Treasury hates hypothecation and I understand all the reasons for that, but this is one of those opportunities to reinvest back into the reason that most people are in accommodation in the first place.

Q99 Mr Alaba: Good morning to you all. I declare an interest. Prior to coming to this place, I worked in hospitality. I ran, owned and consulted in businesses across the country, and in London as well specifically in terms of my operations. I now represent a coastal community, which I will come to in a second.

First, what effect would the overnight levy have on the affordability of domestic holidays, the UK’s competitiveness and visitor demand?

Anthony Breach: Thank you for that question. Our starting point on this is that it makes sense for mayors individually to choose what is right for their area. As Jo mentioned just now, it might work for a large urban area and a mayor may come to that conclusion, but it may on the other hand not work for a coastal community. The mayor will take that decision, and it is entirely appropriate that we have different responses across the country because we have these very different tourism markets. I would expect any local economic assessment to identify both the pros and cons of introducing this levy within that context.

When you look into the evidence on the elasticity of visitor trips between business trips and tourist trips, you see that tourism trips are considerably more elastic than business ones. People are more price-sensitive. It also seems to be the case that elasticities for domestic travel are higher than for international travel. Once people have chosen a destination abroad that they want to go to, they are pretty likely to continue to want to go there, even if there is a small increase in price. Domestic visitors again seem to be more price-sensitive. Therefore, I would expect that, especially coastal communities where cost- competitiveness is a key part of their offer, the visitor levy might not make sense in a way that it might in a larger urban area.

Bernard Donoghue: I entirely agree with Anthony. Anything that puts the price up, particularly in the cost of living crisis, is something that UK citizens will think sensitively about. We are now in year 5 of the cost of living crisis. Only earlier this month, we saw the lowest consumer confidence index since the end of covid. People are making really tactical decisions about how they spend their leisure pounds and leisure hours. Nowadays, particularly for visitor attractions, the competition is not between one visitor attraction and another, but the average family in the UK has a disposable leisure budget of £100 per month. That is a family of four going to a cinema once—or out to dinner, on a day trip, or having a subscription to Netflix, Amazon Prime or Disney+. Competition for the leisure pound and leisure hour has become more competitive, with people making really tactical decisions. If anything looks like it will make the price of a staycation or domestic holiday more expensive, and the ease to book abroad more engaging and enticing, people will do that. If your question is, “Do you think this will have a detrimental effect on domestic tourism?”, from a price point of view it undoubtedly will.

Alistair Handyside: Thank you for your comments earlier about the differences in markets. I will not repeat that it will have a greater impact on rural and coastal areas, primarily domestic visitors and not business stays, which are much more sensitive.

I will go a bit into the technology on pricing elasticity of demand, or PED. This happens in every business, regardless of whether we are talking about hospitality. You work out how much you can put up your prices by how much it will push down demand. In a tiny business like ours, that is an argument between my wife and me. She wants prices up; I want them down, otherwise we will not fill up. Airbnb has algorithms and AI, and every bit of it is based on that argument. Up puts prices up and demand down. UK Hospitality Haven in Lichfield has done the work to show that for every 1% increase in price in our sort of area, rural and coastal, you get a 1.25% decrease in demand. So a 5% increase in price, which I genuinely do not think we can pass on, would result in a 7.5% reduction in bookings. That is why I say we cannot pass this on. We will have to absorb it.

Q100 Mr Alaba: Anthony, you talked about the fact that things are very centralised in the UK economy, so how do you incentivise local government to invest, which then obviously improves the experience for visitors? What do we do? Alistair, you talked about feasibility. I link into that SIC codes and that kind of stuff, so that a case can be made to the Treasury. You also mentioned the need for a proper impact assessment. In a roundabout way, how do local economies and councils support an industry that is important to them, where we know there are challenges of investment and finances at local government level—touching on Bernard’s point right at the top? What do they do? How do they try to make the case?

Anthony Breach: I think this has to be the first step in a longer journey towards fiscal devolution. We are going to hear what is in the fiscal devolution road map tomorrow with the Budget. I hope that will set out not just the visitor levy and the proposals already announced, but a longer period beyond the spending review of increased fiscal autonomy for local government. That will have to entail a closer connection between the economic base of a place and its local government across far more than just tourism. That will be across things like the industrial space, domestic property, income tax sharing and so on. That will all be really important if we want to provide a broad set of incentives for places to help grow their local economies. We know other countries around the world have these sorts of levies and taxes. We know in particular that the big cities can absorb this kind of levy, and it can both provide the funding to help finance larger schemes such as the new Beatles museum being developed in Liverpool, and change incentives in the larger scheme to help the mayor and local government support more hotels being built, street cleaning and improvements to the visitor and residential experience in places visitors are likely to visit.

Q101 Mr Alaba: Alistair, can I come to you quickly with a slight tweak on that question? I understand your journey. I ran businesses. It is important with regard to taxes how that can affect profitability, and you want to survive and thrive. If the local authority, for example, could ensure it, what is needed to earn your trust in how a visitor levy could be spent wisely so it will support and sustain your industry? Is there a case to be made for that?

Alistair Handyside: Regrettably, I am not sure that there is because, like many in this room, I do not believe that we would get hypothecation or ringfencing. Without a guarantee that the money would be spent collectively and wisely, I do not really want to go forward with that. It will cost my business somewhere in the region of £20,000 a year. I think I could invest that £20,000 on extending the visitor experience better than the local council could—with respect to the local council, which is very supportive of tourism.

Mr Alaba: Thank you, I think I have veered into one of my colleague’s questions so I will end it there.

Q102 Jeff Smith: Anthony reminded us by implication that this is a permissive measure. It is a voluntary thing for areas to come into. If we want to give cities like Manchester—my city, where a visitor levy has worked well—the opportunity to raise a levy, is it not the right approach to have this kind of permissive measure? If rural and coastal areas do not want to take it up, that is fine. If the big cities do, that is up to them. Is that not the right approach?

Anthony Breach: I completely agree with that, personally. We are already seeing some mayors saying, “Thanks, but no thanks; this is not right for my area.”

Alistair Handyside: I think it comes down to there having to be, as part of the process, robust economic impact assessments and the local council consulting with citizens in their area, rather than what has been suggested that there is a fast track to this. You need proper economic impact assessments and local consultations because you need local solutions to local challenges.

Jeff Smith: Following from that, local authorities will have different spending priorities. In addition to my day job, I chair the People’s History Museum in Manchester. We are very excited about the prospect of hypothecated funding for the culture and arts scene in Greater Manchester. Q103 Tom Rutland: Good morning. I want to focus a little now on the potential impact on tourism business, which we touched on already with one of my colleague’s questions. Primarily for Bernard and Alistair, how should the Government assess the impact of the levy alongside the other tax, regulatory and employment cost pressures currently facing tourism businesses?

Bernard Donoghue: The first thing is to be transparent about how the current UK Government tourism strategy is being created. We have heard no news about it for several months. When I was at VisitBritain, I managed to write a UK tourism strategy in three months. This Government have had two and a half years and, apart from isolated policy proposals, we have yet to see anything comprehensive or useful.

The second thing is that the taxation burden needs to be at the heart of that, being very critically honest about our international competitiveness against countries such as Spain, Italy, France, Germany and the USA, and what the tax burden actually is and if it could be relieved at all before adding a further tax that consumers are likely to be affected by in terms of their preferences; businesses will certainly be affected by it because they may not be able to transfer it on. Having a critically honest appreciation of our international competitiveness, particularly in terms of taxation, is the first step to understanding whether this is a useful further tax step.

Alistair Handyside: I think we have to look at it in the round. Most of the MPs here are very familiar with our cumulative impacts and DNA. That is what has been thrown at us since covid. This is not looked at in the round but in silos individually. It has to be looked at as part of a cumulative impact.

We do not have a visitor economy growth strategy, as Bernard has said several times. We were hoping for a domestic target. We have an international target, but not a domestic one. Domestic tourism, particularly rural and coastal, is in freefall. So, Nigel, would you want to be the tourism Minister writing a visitor economy growth strategy with no tax changes, no regulatory changes, no budget and bringing in a visitor levy on top? That is not a gig I would want to write. As Bernard has alluded to, we should not be starting from where we are today. We should have a strategy and then, if it was part of a strategy, we might be looking at it very differently.

Q104 Tom Rutland: Going from the strategy to the ground-level impact on much smaller businesses, some of which you represent, what would collecting and reporting the levy involve in practice, particularly for micro-businesses and operators that are managing only one or two properties?

Alistair Handyside: There is no detail yet, but we have the examples of Scotland and Wales, which I suggest the Committee should look at very closely, perhaps if only as a case study in learning how not to do it. We think it is likely to be quarterly. Making Tax Digital is quarterly. That would be another burden when we are told by the Government that they want to cut the regulatory burden by 25%. They would have to report quarterly. You could be reporting quite small sums of money as a small business. Someone has to organise the time to collect it and remit it, and someone has to receive it and check that it is correct. You have to look at the administration costs of an extra tax. It is a whole new tax; it is not done by any currently existing system. It will have to be built, managed and checked, all of those things. We are being asked by Treasury to report on how many hours that might take and we are in the process of doing that, but it is another burden on very small businesses that already have to deal with all of that. I think we have to start treating small businesses with a bit more respect. They do not ask for a great deal but a great deal is demanded of them. It will be another administrative burden on top of the costs to which I have already alluded.

Q105 Tom Rutland: On collection and reporting, is there an international example that works well or better than others?

Alistair Handyside: No. We have looked to try to find good examples. None of them is perfect. It is a very complex tax to do. Colleagues sitting behind me will be giving evidence different from mine because it will affect their sector differently. If I was representing them I would say that; if they were representing me they would say what I have said. It is a very complex tax. It sounds dead easy: just charge the price of a cup of a coffee on a stay and we will all have lots of free money. It is much more complicated than that. You must do robust economic impact assessments, not take shortcuts or race this through legislation. It needs serious contemplation.

Q106 Tom Rutland: Would anybody like to come in on this question, maybe Bernard first? Who should be legally responsible for collecting and remitting the levy where a booking is made through an online platform: the accommodation provider or the platform?

Bernard Donoghue: That is a very good question. I do not think there is a lot of clarity because that happens in different ways in different countries and different municipalities. That is the kind of clarification we would love from Treasury. Rather than an overnight visitor levy being presented—this may be a crude summary—as a kind of smash-and-grab raid on the visitor economy to give local authorities free cash, a more considered approach about legal entitlement, legal insurance and who has technical responsibility is absolutely required quickly. If the legislation is going to come through, as we suspect it might, in the next couple of days, a good bulk of accommodation has already been sold for next year, so that will not be taken into account in new legislation. Anything we are talking about has to be at least two or three years in advance of that—this is not stonewalling—in order to have exactly the kind of measures and functionality in place to deliver this for the benefit of everybody.

Alistair Handyside: And everybody has to build the systems to do this. If you have a complicated system by which every mayor is choosing a different rate, different exemptions and different classifications, anybody who operates on a national basis, or across two regions, has to build that into their booking systems. The administration on platforms can be absolutely enormous the more complex the system is. I am not stonewalling, but you have to give people time to build this in.

Anthony Breach: I do not have anything to add.

Q107 Tom Rutland: Do you envisage that most people would pay on arrival, which seems to be the case internationally in a lot of respects?

Alistair Handyside: Pay on arrival is something we would not welcome. We do not want to get into disputes with our guests about whether they need to pay a tax. You need to put it into the whole booking process, because the CMA says it has to be that way. Therefore, if you are booking in our sectors, or in hotels where you pay, or put your credit card in at the point of booking, it would have to be included in that price at that point, but the remittance date would have to be post the date of the booking completing because, if the booking does not complete, there is no stay tax.

Q108 Chair: Presumably, there are other countries where this is applied regionally or state by state, and you can book through digital online platforms. How do they manage it?

Alistair Handyside: It is very difficult. The APPG for tourism and hospitality brought over the German hotel association manager. I think he has to manage 38 or 42 different systems in different cities. Some states in Germany have said, “No more tourist taxes until you sort it out.” It can get to be so disparate with all the issues that follow that. Guest confusion is one of the biggest ones you need to worry about with everybody setting a different tax. Everybody setting a different tax will cause displacement issues. If Cornwall were to set 6%, you can bet your bottom dollar Devon might set 2%. You are setting people against each other as well to drive it because everybody knows displacement will drive bookings. That is what will happen.

Q109 Mr Alaba: Bernard, you were talking about the fact that there have been 26 Ministers and there is no co-ordinated approach. I totally get it. Up until a few years ago I was in that world as well, so I understand that frustration, but this is not particularly a party political issue; this is just how we have looked at the industry over a generation. That would be my perspective. You mentioned earlier corporate memory, which is a really good point. I agree with you. I do not want to be overly sensitive about this. I totally understand corporate memory. That is obviously linked to taxation approaches. What would you do differently?

Bernard Donoghue: Thank you for giving me the role of tourism Minister. I appreciate it enormously. It is not a flippant point that in the past 29 years we have had 26 tourism Ministers. The average tenure of a tourism Minister in DCMS is less than 14 months. Anthony Breach: Our position is that we should not have hypothecation of the visitor levy, and there are a few reasons for that.
