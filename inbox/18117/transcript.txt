Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Thomas Woldbye and Helen Elsby.

Q550 Chair: Welcome to our second panel this morning. Please could you both introduce yourselves?

Thomas Woldbye: My name is Thomas Woldbye. I have been the CEO of Heathrow airport for three years.

Helen Elsby: Good morning, everyone. My name is Helen Elsby. I am the chief development officer at Heathrow, and I have been delivering construction projects at Heathrow for over 20 years.

Thomas Woldbye: I should highlight that you are also a member of the executive committee at Heathrow, so part of the leadership.

Q551 Chair: Thank you. Why do you think that Heathrow expansion is critical for national growth? Do you feel that that is adequately demonstrated in the Heathrow expansion NPS?

Thomas Woldbye: First, thank you very much for inviting us. It is an honour to be here. We look forward to discussing what we think is an absolutely critical infrastructure project for the UK. We are doing this in the first place because it brings huge private investment into the UK. It provides strong economic growth for society right across the country. It will drive jobs, education, apprenticeships, innovation and trade way beyond what is possible today and what would be possible with a two-runway Heathrow. There are no alternatives to that development. We work with other airports in a very complementary way, but they cannot replace the hub function that Heathrow has. We think the NPS—the national policy statement—goes a very long way to describing that in the right way. We have some areas that we might like to see improvements to, but other than that, we support moving forward with the national policy statement.

Q552 Chair: Promoters are required to provide an assessment of the project’s economic impacts across the UK. Is it clear what you will need to provide to satisfy that economic growth test?

Thomas Woldbye: We are already doing that. We have commissioned independent consultants and universities to look at the impact that a third runway would have across the country. We can already say that we are looking at billions of pounds annually in additional GDP—that could be £17 billion. We are looking at 108,000 jobs that will be there in 20 years’ time that would not be there if you did not have a third runway. We are looking at 5,000 apprenticeships. We are looking at additional trade.

Currently, Heathrow represents £300 billion of trade annually, which makes us by far the biggest port by value in the country. Without Heathrow expanding, that will pretty much stand still because we do not have additional cargo slots as we speak. If we expand Heathrow, that could grow to, for example, £450 billion, so lots of trade, which is important for everyone in the country. It is Cornish tea, steel from Wales—sorry; that does not get transported by air, but it is important in the project—Scottish salmon and Scottish whisky. All of that will see additional opportunities to trade and connect with the rest of the world.

Q553 Chair: On the benefit to the UK more broadly, not just the immediate area of west London, many of those who support runway 3 in evidence to us, and I assume in the evidence to the DFT, justify their support on the basis of connectivity between UK airports and Heathrow. Yet with the inevitable increasing cost per slot, which will be passed to passengers, nobody has yet provided the evidence that those regional flights will be viable in the open market that exists outside the very few tax-funded routes like Belfast. More connectivity between regional airports and Heathrow is part of your case as well. How does that stack up?

Thomas Woldbye: That is a very good question. It is a central part of being a hub. Our prices to the airlines are structured so that long haul is more expensive than short haul, and international short haul is more expensive than domestic. In that way, we incentivise domestic transport. That has not been designated yet, but I am sure that, as we move forward, we will find a way of continuing and growing that. As a hub, we are depending on feed from not only international airports, but local airports. It is in the interest of both the airlines and the airport to have those connections from domestic airports.

Q554 Chair: The airlines will effectively end up having to pay whatever the costs are for runway 3. They will have to pay even more for long haul flights under that modelling and there will be slightly less of an increase, but still a big increase, for the regional flights—a sort of cross-subsidy among the airlines. That is among airlines that, like BA, fly both from regional airports and long-haul. A lot of the regional airports depend on smaller local airlines, as you and others have said, and they will be able to get into Heathrow, but does that work in an airline market?

Thomas Woldbye: In my experience, having also worked in Copenhagen, which is a hub airport in exactly the same situation, that worked really well. Again, these are decisions that are to be taken, but no one is saying that there would necessarily be an increase on the domestic part. That is all about the market and how that would work out. What is more critical is that today, that market is suffering from simply not having enough slots at Heathrow, meaning that you can fly to more destinations in the UK from Amsterdam than from Heathrow. We have outsourced our hub. That would be the first thing we would look to insource. For me, there is no difference whether that is served by BA or a dedicated domestic carrier. There should be the same opportunity.

Q555 Dr Arthur: A lot of freight by value is gold, which is just going back and forward for reasons I cannot quite fathom. Where is the increase in freight going through the expanded airport coming from? You said there was going to be a significant increase in freight going through the airport. Is that new things that are being manufactured just because the airport exists, or is that freight that is quite happily going by sea now, which is lower carbon, more efficient and so on? Where is that freight coming from? I am sure it is not Cornish tea.

Thomas Woldbye: I would think that comes from the exporters of the commodities that are currently going by air wishing to export more. I would assume that the salmon growers want to export more salmon and that the tea exporters want to export more tea. There is a national industrial strategy that encourages light manufacturing. Lots of light manufacturing products and lots of medical products, which are also part of the national industrial policy, are all supposed to be growing. Their main artery of export is air transport.

Q556 Dr Arthur: You have modelled that, then? You talked about assumptions. Has this been modelled in some way?

Thomas Woldbye: That has been modelled in the economic growth assumptions I spoke about earlier. It was not done by ourselves, but by consultants and universities.

Q557 Chair: Some of the economic modelling by the DFT showed not net economic growth to the UK from runway 3, but displacement from other regions to west London, which has a much higher GVA per head and so on. Does that slightly weaken the case that a third runway brings net economic growth to the whole of the UK?

Thomas Woldbye: It is important to note that the DFT modelling is based on a certain model that it has to use, so to speak. That model omits a number of elements that are really important to get the full picture of economic impact, one being the fact that this is private investment; it is not taxpayer money. The model does not really account for that. We are already almost £66 billion in adverse when we look at that model, because it is taken as taxpayer money, as a negative in the model. That is not there. It is actually £33 billion private money that otherwise would not be invested in the UK. That is not accounted for in the model.

The other thing not accounted for in the model is trade, which is clearly a huge part of the reason for expanding Heathrow in the first place. The last one is the assumption that Heathrow competes with regional airports and will take business away. That is not the case. Heathrow is complementary to regional airports. That is why Birmingham, Edinburgh and Glasgow airports support a third runway, because they are critically dependent on that huge artery of both trade and passenger transport to connect to many markets, as Heathrow is connected to 230 airports around the world. No regional airport will ever be able to get to that point. We work with the regional airports and are complementary to them. Additional capacity at Heathrow will mean additional opportunity for the regional airports.

Q558 Chair: Can I pick up one point in your statement that was released in time for today. I think it was in the FT. You said that if runway 3 does not go ahead, the investment of your investors will go elsewhere—I suppose outside the UK. Given that the new Government are looking at growth across the UK, particularly in the north and regions that are more disadvantaged than London, would it not be true that if investment were not able to be put into runway 3, those investors would have the opportunity to invest in significant national infrastructure projects in other parts of the UK?

Thomas Woldbye: That would naturally be an opportunity for them. I have not heard of them having any plans for that.

Q559 Chair: There are plenty of plans: Northern Powerhouse Rail, new rolling stock, the lower Thames crossing, Gatwick, Luton and so on. Some airports—I cannot remember which—are looking for investors. Would it not be true that your capital is mobile?

Thomas Woldbye: They could do that. Based on discussions with them, they have no current plans to do that.

Chair: Fine. I am just trying to pin down your statement.

Thomas Woldbye: The statement is quite clear that there is no other project like Heathrow. There is nothing of the size of Heathrow, and there is nothing of the size of Heathrow that has the UK-wide positive consequences. Sixty per cent of the economic benefit of a runway at Heathrow will be felt throughout the entire country. If you compare that with other projects, I do not think that is the case.

Q560 Chair: We have heard the view expressed by several people that the affordability of expansion should be made more prominent as a requirement—perhaps as a fifth test—in the HENPS. What is your view on affordability being a condition?

Thomas Woldbye: First of all, we have a close dialogue with our airlines and we entirely agree that a third runway and the expansion of Heathrow—it is important to note that the expansion of Heathrow is not just about a runway; it is about renewing the entire airport and getting an airport that this country deserves. That aside, this should of course be done as economically and efficiently as it possibly can be. We have no interest in spending more money. We get a regulated return on what we spend, so—

Q561 Chair: Well, you do, don’t you? It is a pretty safe bet for your investors.

Thomas Woldbye: I think that is a different discussion, but our interest is to be as attractive as we possibly can be to our airlines, and we have very detailed discussions with them. We fully agree with them that affordability is important. Helen, maybe you want to talk to the way that is implemented.

Helen Elsby: Absolutely. To reiterate Thomas’s point, we are in absolute agreement with the airlines on affordability. The business case is great for the country, it is great for airlines, it is great for consumers and passengers, and it is good for Heathrow. If we do not get affordability right, then we all lose, so it is a very important component. We talk to the airlines on a regular basis about the cost of the project, and we are continually working with them to find opportunities to bring down the cost of the project, which is just one factor of the affordability equation.

To your point about the place in the HENPS, as I said, we are absolutely aligned on the criticality of affordability within the project, but we must remember that this is a project that spans 20 years of a full rework of our masterplan. Therefore, we actually believe that affordability should be checked at regular touchpoints throughout the life of the project, as is the case today in all the construction projects that we deliver; it is regularly checked through the project life cycle to ensure that the project is delivering value for money and benefit.

We are concerned that looking at an affordability test within a policy that drives a planning statement—a one-time test through the planning process—would compromise the role of the CAA in checking affordability and cost throughout the life cycle of the project, on an ongoing basis. From our perspective, it should be looked at continually, throughout the process, via the routes that we have with the CAA, supplemented with independent assurance all the way through.

Q562 Dr Arthur: From the public’s perspective, this is an incredibly important issue. The really easy way to demonstrate this to the public is by saying that none of the airlines support your proposals because of the affordability question. You say that you agree with the airlines on affordability. Do they agree with you on what affordability actually means? You have heard what they have said.

Helen Elsby: As I have said, we are in alignment on the need for the whole scheme to be affordable so that everybody gets the benefits they need, and we have jointly written to say that. We are in alignment on that point. What we are doing on an ongoing basis is working on the cost of the project, which, as I have said, is just one lever of affordability, to make sure that we are aligned on that.

Q563 Dr Arthur: Is there any other airport in the world where it has been approved despite all the airlines opposing it? Why would the Government approve what you are proposing if none of the airlines support it?

Thomas Woldbye: In many places in the world—Amsterdam, for example, but also Munich—airport expansion is approved by the state unilaterally.

Q564 Dr Arthur: But do the airlines unanimously say it is not affordable?

Chair: Are they private sector projects in which the users—the airlines, and therefore the passengers and the freight handlers—have to pay the costs?

Thomas Woldbye: The regulatory model in those places is very akin to the one that we have here, so the answer to that is fundamentally yes, with variations around that point. Some airports are privatised; some airports are state-owned. The regulatory process, like in Amsterdam, like in Munich, is fundamentally the same.

Q565 Chair: Okay. I think the cost of your capex just for runway 3 is £33 billion to £35 billion at current costs. Does that include noise mitigation at similar levels to Gatwick’s DCO? Does it include the passenger and staff element of surface access by rail, which we discussed in the previous session? Does it include the carbon costs that the airlines will have to pay, plus the cost of SAF and so on, which we discussed in our session yesterday with the Climate Change Committee? That will surely bring up the per passenger level, whether or not you agree with the airlines saying, I think, £65 per head last time. Whether or not you agree that that is a reasonable calculation per passenger for the capex, you have those other costs as well. With all those added on, what reassurance can you provide at this stage that your project is deliverable, without even further increases in service charges, which would undermine the whole rationale for demand at Heathrow?

Thomas Woldbye: First, it is important to note that passengers do not pay a carbon charge or an airport charge.

Chair: No, the airlines do, and then they have to recoup that somehow.

Thomas Woldbye: They pay a ticket price, and ticket prices are not necessarily related to airport charges. I can give you an example. Over the past 10 years, the airport charge at Heathrow has come down 14% in real terms, whereas the ticket prices have gone up by 30%. There is no scientifically proven link between the two; there have been various studies on that.

The amount of money we are talking about, compared with the ticket price at Heathrow, is relatively small in either of the cases you bring up. I have heard the £65 number, but I do not know where it comes from; it is not part of any calculation I have seen that we do. We have said that, as the calculation looks now—and we are trying to reduce that—it is an additional £15 per passenger.

Q566 Chair: That is for the capital expenditure.

Thomas Woldbye: Yes.

Q567 Chair: What about those other elements I mentioned?

Helen Elsby: To pick up on that, the £33 billion is the privately financed cost that we attribute, as you say, to the expansion elements of the airport, not the rejuvenation of the current airport. You asked about mitigation costs. That includes all the mitigation costs that we believe are necessary to comply with the policy statement as set out at the moment.

You referenced Gatwick. Heathrow operates in a different environment from Gatwick, so the noise policy would be appropriate for Heathrow. For example, we do not operate through the night, whereas Gatwick does. We address noise mitigations in different ways. We have set our budgets to be in line with the noise policy appropriate for Heathrow.

You talked about surface access. All the surface access elements that we need in order to expand the airport are also included in that cost.

Q568 Chair: Obviously, there is the box, or boxes, within the perimeter. What about the infrastructure cost of the rail links beyond the perimeter?

Helen Elsby: I will come to that. I would just remind people that surface access covers a wide range of things.

Chair: The major one being rail.

Helen Elsby: Cycle routes, walking routes, road improvements, new bus interchanges and the like.

On rail specifically, we have a sum of money. What the CAA policy directs us to do, and is included in our plans, is to look at funding the cost that is proportionate to the benefits that any rail scheme would deliver to Heathrow passengers. We would ask our airlines and passengers to pay for only those elements that they gain benefit from. Obviously, a rail scheme of that size would deliver much wider benefits to the country, beyond what would be delivered at Heathrow, so those are the elements that are not there.

Q569 Chair: Have you put figures to those elements? No. It would be useful to see them.

Thomas Woldbye: To put the numbers in perspective, airport users at Heathrow already pay £30 of airport passenger duty. We are talking about £15 for a new runway. That amount, compared with what is already being paid in APD, which was supposed to offset some of the climate impacts, is much smaller.

Q570 Chair: All right. You are in a new situation from 2018. Competition has been introduced, with a second promoter being brought into the mix. What are the pros and cons of doing that in expanding Heathrow’s infrastructure? Do you think it has the potential to increase efficiency and control the costs?

Thomas Woldbye: I think Heathrow’s competition is in Amsterdam, Munich, Frankfurt and, not least, Istanbul, which is expanding very fast. That is where our competition is as a country, and that is where our focus should be. The model for every airport that I know in the world is to have one operator, which ensures safety, security and the wellbeing of passengers, as well as a good passenger experience and airline efficiency. It is run as an ecosystem, but with one entity running it, and I think that is how we should move forward.

Q571 Chair: You have argued that the role of the CAA, as an economic regulator, should be kept separate from the planning process requirements. What are your views on the CAA’s role in ensuring the affordability of expansion and on what appears to be its very tight hold on what you can and cannot spend money on, even now? What is your preferred future regulatory model?

Thomas Woldbye: Fundamentally, our project is based on a regulatory model that is the same as the one we have today. You have what is called a regulatory asset base, which you put your investments on and get a return on. It is important to note that we have a system today whereby every single pound that we spend on investments is pre-approved in the regulatory process by the CAA. Each individual project, as it comes along, is approved at both gate 1 and gate 3, out of 10 gates, by the airlines. It is literally every single pound. We cannot spend £1 without it being approved by the airlines.

Do we think that, going forward, there should be very clear transparency and cost control, including on the third runway? Absolutely, and to that end we have suggested that we set up a joint assurance board, which would include independent experts, airlines and us, to make sure the project is delivered as efficiently as possible. We are fully open to that. If you look at it, that is what is already happening today. We can’t invest anything.

Personally, I would like to see an efficiency improvement of that process, because it is not necessarily efficient, but that is a different discussion. It is not really a third runway discussion; it is a discussion about how we make sure investments into the airport are as efficient as they can possibly be, both for the airlines and for us.

Chair: As you may know, I have experience of the restrictions in your spend over the current noise insulation scheme, which does not even comply with the actual requirements under previous permissions.

Thomas Woldbye: We would agree on that.

Q572 Chair: I cannot believe that there is no flexibility at all, but that is for another day.

You have argued that the draft NPS should clarify that mitigation measures should be proportionate and deliverable and should maintain the overall viability and affordability of the scheme. How should that be assessed? Is there a risk that measures to protect the community and the environment could be set aside as a result?

Helen Elsby: I do not believe that to be the case. The HENPS, as set out today, has very clear criteria in the tests. We welcome that, so that as we are preparing our planning application, we are very clear on what the tests are and can demonstrate our mitigations and proposals to satisfy all those tests, enabling a route through to the scheme being consented and us going on to deliver the benefits.

As you rightly say, we talk about ensuring that they are proportionate and deliverable. That enables us to ensure that we are able to satisfy those tests through the examination process, and that there is the balance between the benefits that a scheme of this size will enable throughout the whole country and the mitigation that we will need to put in place. There are some difficult elements of this for the local community, and we want to make sure we are providing the right mitigation measures for them.

We think the HENPS does that today. We would just ask for more clarity through all of those tests to make sure we are submitting the right evidence through the examination process.

Thomas Woldbye: It is important that we talk about the local community. As you know, we work a lot with the local community. There are two aspects of that. When I meet with people in the local community, what they speak about most is the 20 years of uncertainty on this and the need for them to move on. We are now talking about mental health support because people have been in uncertainty for so long. I think we owe it to the people around the airport to take a decision, so that we can say, “We are doing this, and these are the conditions on which you can move on with your life.” That has been an underemphasised aspect of the whole project. It has been really, really hard for the people in the community, and I can understand them.

The other point I want to make is that one of the reasons for us proposing this scheme was that the Airports Commission found that the north-west scheme—the runway that we wish to build—is the one that, in the round, compromises on or at least satisfies best both the needs of the airport and the needs of the local community. The minimisation of noise over London, the least houses having to be taken down, and so on—the whole scheme had all of those parameters, and that is why the scheme is there. It is not just because it is a good runway for the airport; it is because it at the same time minimises the impact on the local environment.

Q573 Chair: Helen, you said that you want more clarity on conditions and so on. Could you give us a couple of examples of where you would like to see more clarity in the NPS?

Helen Elsby: For example, if we talk about some of the noise conditions, we need to be very clear about what we mean. We are proposing a night-flight ban. We would like those to be aligned to—

Chair: Sorry, but would that apply to the whole airport or just runway three?

Helen Elsby: We would look at making sure that all local communities have a period of time when they get predictable respite. We would alternate that across all three runways to ensure that every part of the community has predictable respite.

Chair: That is 11 pm to 7 am.

Helen Elsby: This is where we would like clarity, to ensure that the proposals we have in place are deliverable at the airport, protect the economic benefit of the flights around that period and are also aligned with the International Civil Aviation Organisation standard definitions of scheduled departure times. Sorry for the detail there, but the clearer we are on what those requirements are, the more we can ensure we are compliant with them.

Q574 Dr Arthur: You introduced that as a night-time ban, but as you went into the detail, it did not sound like a night-time ban; it sounded like a night-time ban some of the time.

Chair: I think it is a ban on specific runways. On one or two of the runways there will be no arrivals before 7 am, and the existing arrivals will alternate, which is sort of what happens at the moment.

Helen Elsby: It is about the way you alternate the runways effectively to ensure that every community has a fixed length of time when they have respite from the noise. That would be—

Dr Arthur: That is not every night of the week for individuals though, or is it?

Helen Elsby: It would be overnight all the time, but the start and end times might be slightly staggered depending on the flight schedules we have available.

Chair: At the moment there are 16 flights from 4.15 am to 6 am. It is a free-for-all from 6 am till 7 am, although you try to alternate that, and then you have the daytime alternation from 7 am until 3 pm and 3 pm till 11 pm. Sorry, it is technical stuff.

Helen Elsby: Thank you.

Chair: All part of the day job.

Thomas Woldbye: You know more about this than I do!

Chair: I’m afraid so.

Q575 Alex Mayer: Last month, it was reported that you had told the Prime Minister that the original 2035 target operating date for the new runway is unfeasible and that you now expect it to be 2039. What has changed?

Helen Elsby: There are two factors that have been driving this. If we revert back to when we put in our submission to the process last year, we always outlined a range of scenarios by which the runway and the associated infrastructure would be delivered, 2035 being one of them and 2039 being another. We always indicated that range, and at that point in time we always said that 2035 was a great ambition to have, and one that we would really like to achieve. I want to leave the Committee in no doubt that we want to deliver a runway and all its associated infrastructure as soon as we possibly can, so that was an ambition that we wanted to achieve.

Probably two things have materialised over the course of the last couple of years. One is that throughout that process we always outlined that there were things that needed to be achieved in order to achieve that earliest runway date. Some of those are around planning permission reform. Some are around ensuring that we have a regulated model that allows our investors to commit significant investment, alongside a few other things. We absolutely recognise that the Government have worked at lightning speed on a number of things on this programme to accelerate its delivery, but as so often in projects, not all opportunities that you identify come to fruition, and therefore some of those things have not been able to be achieved, and that has affected the end date.

The second fact is that we have, as we have already discussed, been talking to the airline community—our customers—around the affordability of the scheme. In order to deliver that date you would have to commit to significant sums of money before you actually get consent, because we would need to start purchasing a lot of the land before we have consent for the scheme. In discussing that with them, that is a concern to them. It is also something we have been talking to the CAA about.

We believe we would be better investing that money after the consent, when we know we have permission to deliver the project, which means some of those activities have moved pre-DCO consent to post-DCO consent, and that has affected the end date as well. That has improved the affordability of the scheme, I should add, because it smooths out the cost. But I want to leave the Committee in no doubt that we absolutely want to deliver as soon as possible. We will always continue to look for all opportunities to bring the date back in if we can.

Q576 Alex Mayer: So you are saying this is not slippage; it is something you pre-planned for.

Helen Elsby: I would say that we always worked off a range of scenarios, because at this stage in a project of this size there are a huge number of unknowns, and we would always be looking at a range of dates. This is where our current assumptions lie.

Q577 Alex Mayer: Assuming it is not happening at the beginning of that range, do you now have a new range that extends further into the future?

Helen Elsby: As I said, at the moment our plans are for 2039. That is the date on which we have done all our assessments and all our modelling to submit our DCO application. It is really important that throughout that examination process we can demonstrate the deliverability of the scheme and demonstrate how we plan to achieve that. All our mitigations that we are proposing are based on that date, plus all the other dates for the rest of the delivery of the infrastructure. That is the timeframe we are working to at the moment.

Thomas Woldbye: For 2039 we have a robust plan. Given the current planning circumstances, JR and everything else included, that would bring us to 2039.

Q578 Alex Mayer: I just find it a little odd that you have not extended to 2042 or something to give you a bit more leeway.

Helen Elsby: Just to be clear, with all projects you would have a range of dates and a range of costs that sit around it. What I am trying to articulate is that for the DCO process to be robust we have to formulate our plans around one deliverable schedule. We would of course have scenarios should we be able to come earlier or later, but that is our core case that we are now working to.

Q579 Alex Mayer: Has the change of date, if we assume it is a change, affected the timetable for terminal upgrades and reaching full capacity, which was previously for 2054?

Helen Elsby: On the masterplan phasing, as it comes through, to some extent yes it has, because terminal 5X, which is the first new terminal to be built after the runway, would also move by the same amount of time as in the original plan, simply because, as I mentioned, you would need to buy all the land to deliver both the runway and the terminal. If we are not doing that now, both dates would move out. The later phases—

Alex Mayer: Is that by the same amount of time? It is four years, basically.

Helen Elsby: Yes, but as I said, as we work through the next phases of the scheme and get into the detailed design, we might be able to pull some of those dates forward.

Thomas Woldbye: But there will be better alignment between when the runway is ready and when the terminals are ready.

Helen Elsby: That is sort of built into the plan—how those capacity elements will be delivered. As I have said, as we go through the examination process, we have to take time slices of when those increases in capacity would come and be able to demonstrate our mitigations accordingly. Those would all flow through over the next 20 years or so.

Q580 Alex Mayer: Has there been any concern from potential investors that you are now at the later end of your original group of dates?

Thomas Woldbye: Not in a sense; what is driving investors is not necessarily a particular date. We are prepared to deliver a third runway. The ambitious date was set by the previous Government; we had a credible path to deliver that but, as Helen said, there were consequences to it. I think the ’39 deadline is more robust given the conditions we are working under, and investors are prepared for that.

What investors are looking for—before we get to a DCO, in fact—is what the regulation is around this and the actual business case that we can present to investors. Currently, we are lacking that because we do not know the outcome of the HENPS. That is fine; it is the process we are in. But we also do not know the outcome of the CAA deliberations on the long-term regulatory model, and that is probably more important. That is what we are waiting for.

Q581 Alex Mayer: Do you think investors have probably already built into their thinking the idea that, “There’ll be some slippage in this because everything to do with Heathrow takes forever”?

Chair: We have had several sessions on HS2.

Thomas Woldbye: That is not how they look at it, and I would say that this is not HS2. We plan in a different way; we have delivered terminals and other things on time and on budget.

Q582 Chair: Helen, could you explain something? You have been working for some years on the H8 funding to do the remodelling and upgrade on the existing airport. My understanding is that whether runway 3 gets approval impacts on your plans for the central terminal area and the timing of that work.
