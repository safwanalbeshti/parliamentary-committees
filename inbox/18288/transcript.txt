Examination of witness

Dame Rachel de Souza.

The Chair: I would like to welcome Dame Rachel de Souza, who is the Children’s Commissioner for England. We are very pleased that you are here and I hope we can gain a lot of your knowledge and expertise in this area over the next hour. I have no interests to declare. I would like to start the questioning by asking you: to what extent is the children’s social care system meeting the demands and delivering good outcomes for all children, particularly those with complex needs, including those who have been deprived of their liberty?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I have four points to make, but first I just want to thank the committee. I have been Children’s Commissioner since March 2021 and you have been shining a relentless light on children in social care—particularly the most vulnerable—as I have tried to. We have been thinking of fallen soldiers, and I am thinking of Lord Laming who was a great support to me.

First—as you know—my role was born out of the tragic abuse and death of Victoria Climbié. Not a day goes by when I do not think about her, but since I have become Children’s Commissioner we have also had the dreadful deaths of Arthur Labinjo-Hughes, Star Hobson, Sara Sharif and Preston Davey, all potentially preventable. These are the highest-profile failings.

Secondly, children's social care and services are letting down children in quieter ways too. Some 669 children are living in illegal, unregistered children’s homes, and a third of those are deprived of their liberty. My data uncovered this; do you realise that cost us £350 million last year in fees for illegal placements? Just think what you could do with that. The year before that, we spent about the same amount. Some 36 of those children cost £1 million each. That £1 million was not spent on Eton-style homes; it was on caravans and B&Bs; children put in emergency placements and other things.

There are disgustingly lower rates of educational attainment for children in care. I am about to publish on prison education—hopefully, showing what reform could look like—on 23 November, which I have been working closely with Jake Richards on. So many boys in prison are care experienced. Again, looking at the contracts of the boys in YOIs, we are talking about £28,000 or so for a boy getting two hours of education a week. How much is their education costing per hour? Care leavers are more likely to be homeless or, frankly, dead.

Thirdly, I recently published “Childhood Lottery”, consisting of 162 metrics on every part of a child’s life collected from my data powers and publicly available information. It was published in the press recently; you can see it and look at every single area. What I can tell you is the social care data showed us that children are getting a choice of bad or terrible. The percentage of children placed out of area is 89% in Westminster and 17% in North Lincs; it is still too high. We see similar variants in social worker turnover, care leaver accommodation and access to support.

Fourthly, before we go into questions, children’s social care is not adequately wrapping its arms around all the children it should. I just gave two hours of evidence on Prevent to the Southport inquiry. Over 1,000 children were referred to Prevent three times, as the killer was, and bounced back to schools. There was no social care engagement with most of them or they were signed off social care.

There are children stuck in hospitals. I talk to parents whose children could be at home if social care and health managed to work together. I talked to a mum the other day whose son was dying of cancer; he has since died. There were no funds to sort her staircase out. He had an oxygen tank on his back so they would take it off at the bottom of the stairs, run him up the stairs and put it back on at the top of the stairs.

That is the reality of us not working together properly and wrapping our arms around all children in social care. There are children seeking asylum and children with severe disabilities. There is a lack of health and social care working together to make safe, proper, specialist provision. Finally, there are the children in the criminal justice system.

To finish this point, when I came in as Children’s Commissioner in 2021 it was after 20 years of education reform. I was an educationalist and Tony Blair’s 69th academy principal back in 2005; I had a sense of what reform was. I was shocked and horrified by what I saw in children’s social care in 2021, and I remain shocked and horrified. Having seen part of the department do so well with and understand reform, how we use data and accountability, and that sometimes it takes money and needs a light shining on it, what I saw in children’s social care was just desperate, frankly. It is difficult; it is a system that fails too many children.

I am furious to hear children’s social care speak about its reforms; they are not reforms. This country knows how to do reform. We do not have reform; we have a set of good, nice ideas, many of which have not been delivered. I love unique ID; I fought for it. Regional care co-operatives are a great idea. Actually, since that idea came about, we have seen one children’s home open in Manchester last week.

What has been done about eradicating the use of illegal homes? Nothing. Ofsted is continually grading areas as great, which goes completely against my and children’s experience. I am here today to tell you what I really think from the perspective of the Children’s Commissioner who looks right across the system and speaks to children.

The Chair: Thank you very much for that introduction. It is a bleak picture you paint and we will look more closely at each area as we go through questions. There is just one for me to pick up on. You mentioned the youth justice system. Do you have a close working relationship with the CQC on children in mental health provision? What is your view of that provision?

Dame Rachel de Souza: My view is similar to the one I just gave. Again, there are wonderful people working in all these areas and in the inspectorate, so this is not about personal criticism. I visit children in mental health wards and any child who is away from home all the time. What we need is closer working between CQC and social care, which is a whole other question of looking at solutions and how to do that. There are similar issues with CQC, but we need Ofsted and CQC working far more closely over specialist provision and we need some provision built first.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: Thank you for all the work you have done over six years. You have really put some issues on the agenda in a way that none of your predecessors managed. Congratulations on shining a light on some terrible states of affairs.

I want to ask about unregistered children’s homes. You have mentioned 669 children and £350 million. Why do you think the system is using illegal, unregistered homes on such a regular basis? What would you like government and the DfE to do to end that problem?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I want to merge my answers to those two questions, if that is okay. There are about 650 MPs in the Commons; that is about the same number of children who have been in illegal children’s homes each year over the past two years. If each just took one, we could sort it; it is actually a small number. I am going to declare my colours: illegal is illegal. No area should pass its Ofsted inspection if it is putting children in illegal homes. No Government should allow children to go into illegal homes.

There are some very practical things. Ofsted will tell us that there is a delay in registering some homes; that is simple to sort out—get it done. When I sit with the President of the Family Division, he will say, “Health and social care are fighting over these children. They come to my judges on a Friday night for a DoL and are put in temporary accommodation.” That is true. I tracked every single child on a DoL and a number were in both the 2024 and 2025 data. That means children in illegal homes have been there for more than 12 months. I have the best data on this; we must not have the wool pulled over our eyes.

I am sure you have had directors of children’s services and others here who talk about not having enough capacity and that we need to do something to use what we have; we have to put them somewhere. I will remind you that it cost us £350 million last year. My view would be that local councils, care co-operatives or whoever it is need to strategically plan for children and build proper provision both in terms of building secure children’s homes and specialist foster care.

Before I get down to it, the sufficiency question is so frustrating because it is impacting right across all the sectors. Some 45% of children in prison are on remand—many for eight to 12 months—which means they have not been in front of a court and judged as guilty yet. When I talk to those boys, their education has been interrupted and they are bitter and upset. I am sure some will prove to be guilty, but many will not.

When I trace back why they are there, it is because when the judge is deciding where to put them, there is no sufficiency of beds, places or children’s homes, so they go to prison with killers and murderers instead. What do you think that does to them? Capacity is clearly an issue, but we cannot just sit here and bleat capacity if we are spending £350 million a year on DoLs.

Councils—or whoever the strategic authority is—need to make five-year plans and build the capacity needed. The problem is that many of these children are complex and have special needs, behavioural issues, a range of things, which is why we need all the services working together—as you say CQC, registration, health—to build the right capacity.

Let me just give you one case study before I come into my big, high-level view of what we should do. One girl, Summer, moved between four different homes in six months, and was then placed in an illegal children’s home on a caravan park, no doubt costing £10,000 a week. Different staff came in and out who spoke limited English. She had no access to a proper shower and was left to support herself with meals and finances as though she were an adult. I have hundreds of other examples.

Let us zoom out. One problem we need to look at, which I would ask you, my Lords, to think about, is our legislation. The legislation that governs us is the Children Act, a brilliant piece of legislation created by Baroness Butler-Sloss in 1989 and revised in 2004 and again in 2014. It does not really take into account or understand the complexity of children we have now. It does not account for asylum, which is why we have the Home Office trying to home children when it should be nowhere near them; it should be children’s social care. It does not take into account all the ultra-violent children being radicalised online or the extra-familial harms rather than intra-familial harms. If you are going to change social care and how civil servants and front-line people work, you need to deal with the Children Act. That would be my first recommendation.

My second point is organisational. We have regional care co-operatives. Some work—such as Manchester—in terms of size, but there are few. Some are random and voluntary. We have health footprints which are completely different to education, social care and local authority footprints. Maybe devolution is a chance to say, “Let’s put the footprints together and have an outcomes test for these children and a deputy mayor for children in each area who could oversee this and sort it out”. That is big and going high.

If we narrow down to illegal placements, we need more placements from social enterprises or joint specialist places. We need some good local authorities; for example, Kirklees is setting up its own, as is my hometown of Scunthorpe. Is this not a chance to grip it differently and set up the secure children’s homes, which could be justice, health, complex needs or social care beds, set up your specialist fostering and make those homes centres of support for those specialist foster carers so they do not break down? They are simple things that could really work.

The other thing I would say is that Ofsted must not, and should not, deem that a local authority is meeting expectations if it is using illegal homes. It is a simple no. Are we saying that we can take our most vulnerable children and put them somewhere illegal? No. I have responded to the consultation on this; it has been well received. I know the sector will worry, but we need councils to support their DCSs to make sure there is provision.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: On that point we have quizzed directors of children’s services, as you say, and the DfE on this very point and pointed out to them that they are routinely using illegal homes. I actually have no sympathy for their argument, but they say, “On a Friday afternoon, what else am I supposed to do?” As I understand it, you are saying they should have thought of that before Friday afternoon.

Dame Rachel de Souza: We write strategic plans, think about sufficiency and plan ahead. What on earth is the department doing? Frankly, the Department for Education is deafening in its silence on all these issues that I have raised and actually needs challenging. I was shocked by the fact that its arms were not around the children whom the Home Office was dealing with when we had the hotels and the missing children or the kids bouncing back from Prevent.

I am speaking to Georgia Gould and I have spoken to the new Secretary of State for Education, and there is certainly a will around prison education, for example. It is a catalogue of failure. I have been Children’s Commissioner for five and a half years and I have had eight Secretaries of State for Education, including three in one week, and five Prime Ministers. Let us be honest: the Civil Service is only as good as the Ministers who grip it. We need to see some grip.

Lord Mohammed of Tinsley: Thank you for speaking so passionately; I was nodding away when you were speaking. It is really important that we have a Children’s Commissioner who absolutely speaks up for young people because they definitely need a voice. A concern we have had as a committee is about the outcomes, which you touched on in your initial response—ie Ofsted says a home is brilliant or good, but then we look at the outcomes and they just do not translate, particularly for those looked-after young people who tend to be NEET. How can we challenge that? It is okay to keep people secure, safe and fed but we are not equipping them for the real world. What needs to change?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I know the new framework is being worked on by Ofsted. The HMCI understands that he works for children; it is really important that we do in these roles. He needs to show great courage in drawing the line in the sand—particularly in the children’s social care area—around things such as illegal children’s homes. I have responded to his consultation on this. Inclusion is becoming part of the framework. A lot can be done on the school side around really focusing on looked-after and other children.

I was slightly concerned about the idea of getting rid of gradings of local authority areas, not on the school front—I am not talking about that today—but on the local authority area, because we are going to be able to grip it even less. Again, there is this culture of minimum standards across children’s social care, which HMCI came in wanting to challenge, instead of a culture of how we can reform this and make this really good.

We have an opportunity with the new framework and we should challenge HMCI and the team around him. Do not forget: HMCIs are often people like me who have come from education. They have advisers around them whom they have to rely on and we need to make sure that all those people are challenged by shining a bright light on data. I have had to work really hard to get hold of some data, such as on the illegal children’s homes. We need better data, and one of my recommendations is getting that unique ID really working so we can see and ask questions of it.

Baroness Hollins: Thank you for giving children and young people a voice; it is really important. You have talked a bit about regional care co-operatives. Could you say a bit more about the impact they could have on the supply of suitable placements, particularly for children with complex needs?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I was supportive of Josh MacAlister’s regional care co-operative idea in 2021. For once, the head went up and it was recognising that we do not slice children by social care, health and so on; we need to look at the complexity of their needs. The potential is there for some really creative and interesting work. As I was alluding to, we could be looking at beds that are really for specialist children and supporting families. When you think about those £10,000-a-day and £1 million placements, just think about if you could give even just some of that money to a family and support them through a regional care co-operative. There are so many things that we could do to help.

The regional care co-operatives are potentially a really good thing, but I will make two points, to keep it short. One is that they are an idea on paper, not a reality. We have one or two trials that are very different and voluntary and their footprints do not match with areas. We must take this opportunity to match the footprints up. The Ministry of Justice has just come in and said it is going to do something about children in remand and remand placements completely separate from children’s social care. What are we doing? It is a good idea and the right idea and it needs to happen. Delivery would be my biggest criticism: there is none.

Baroness Hollins: They have the potential and presumably that potential could also include trying to help children move out of secure placements, including psychiatric placements. I just wonder what the size of that issue currently is and whether you know how many are being kept in long-term segregation or solitary confinement.

Dame Rachel de Souza: We have that number, and I will write to the committee and share that with you. I make it my business to visit them. Can I just commend your fantastic work? We have taken your recommendation on, absolutely agree with it and want to apply it across to children in justice, particularly children kept in isolation on psychiatric wards; that is why I visit them. We did our last big survey—The Big Ambition—just before the election and we found the unhappiest children are those children, so I just wanted to say thank you for your work on that.

The regional care co-operatives offer us an opportunity but we need to be ambitious about delivery, data, who these children are and focus. We need the centre holding it to account. We need five-year strategies where we are building, knowing the data we have. We need a unique ID with a digitised red book that makes every parent feel better and the background of it being the sharing of data about these complex children. There are things we can do, and we used to do it.

The Chair: Can I just come in very briefly on your point about coterminous boundaries, which I absolutely support? My understanding was that the Manchester pilot—I will declare an interest: I live there—is coterminous with Greater Manchester ICB, the mayor’s footprint and all that, which enables that co-operation to be much more effective in theory.

Dame Rachel de Souza: That is absolutely right. Let me give you a couple of examples of how we made that work even while that was in development. When Andy Burnham was mayor, he appointed me as the chair of the Greater Manchester Combined Authority attendance alliance. I was able to get the nurses, dentists, all the health professionals, 440 schools and the DCSs around the table and share, which none of them had ever done before.

They all told me why they thought children were not attending school. We had our superb little attendance team—the best team in the DfE—which did the data on exactly who missed school and why. Manchester was saying, “The academies are excluding everyone”, or, “The health is this”, or, “Everyone’s too poor”, and all those things had little bits of truth. I showed them that, basically, if their children attended on Friday as they attended on Tuesday, they would be the best-attending area in the country and everybody went, “Oh!” They did amazing work just being able to bring all those partners together. Manchester was also piloting how to do a unique ID and some places are trying to push ahead. It is so sensible just to put everyone together. It just works however it is done, and devolution is an opportunity.

Lord Mohammed of Tinsley: We have had a lot of discussion around Ofsted. How effective is Ofsted in responding to the challenges of residential care markets at the moment?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I am really pleased to see that Ofsted has started its first tranche of a small number of prosecutions around illegal homes and that its consultation is out on illegal homes. I am just going to come clean with you: I was an academy principal who loved reform, worked with business and ran a trust of schools but never would I ever have allowed profit-making in schools and I was never asked to.

Some 80% of children’s care homes are run by a small number of hedge funders that sit behind this. I could have turned a blind eye to profit-making. I would not, but one could if you were so worried about getting people in after local authorities and charities left because of the abuse scandal; that was when profit-making companies came in. Profiteering is appalling and there is no place for it.

When I arrived in the department in 2021, I first called in the civil servants who were in charge of the contracts and said, “Right, what are the rules? What profits are allowed? What are you doing?” They were lovely. They were not senior civil servants; they were two youngsters holding that. They had the commercial sense of policy civil servants.

I was so concerned when I dug in and found the small number of companies profiteering that I went and spoke to the Secretary of State at the time and hit the roof. I also went to former Ministers who I had worked with for a long time and said, “What on earth have you been doing? How have you allowed this?” It was just oversight, because everyone’s eyes were on education and not these vulnerable children. These vulnerable children are not their political place.

Ofsted has made a good start, but we should not just leave it to Ofsted; we have decisions as a nation. We know who the corporate parents are, but we need to decide what the model should be that works for children’s social care. I am all for innovation and different kinds of people getting involved; I have done it in education. The experience and outcomes for children are not good enough. I could spend two hours just giving you stories, but I will not.

Lord Mohammed of Tinsley: Can I just press you on an issue around Ofsted’s responsibilities of both inspection and registration? Do you think that role works at the moment, or do you think there should be a separation between those who register and those who then go and inspect?

Dame Rachel de Souza: It is tricky. If I had a criticism of Ofsted, it would be the delay with registering homes; that is just simple and straightforward. Hurry up and register them. It acts with integrity, but I am afraid it is that mindset that I see right through children’s social care of minimum standard rather than, “What’s the optimum? Is this good enough for our child?”

Lord Mohammed of Tinsley: What is the role of CQC in terms of inspecting? It has come up before in our inquiry.

Dame Rachel de Souza: Joint homes need joint inspection, both for set-up and afterwards. It can take time but it should be doable. With decent planning, it should not be a problem.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: Going back to Lord Mohammed’s first question, you were a very brilliant headteacher and manager of academies and your school was inspected by Ofsted and came out well, no doubt repeatedly. The school was not registered by Ofsted, so that was totally different.

Dame Rachel de Souza: Free schools have a DfE registration. You kept it separately; that sounds sensible and it worked. If I were setting up a new school, DfE would come and do registration but inspection was separate. It would at least avoid any conflict, would it not?

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: When free schools were set up, that is exactly how it worked.

Dame Rachel de Souza: We went through that, and there were clear lines in the sand about set-up and inspection.

The Chair: Presumably, there is a capacity issue—whether it be Ofsted and/or you—to deal with the demand for that registration to be taken quickly.

Dame Rachel de Souza: Yes. Use some of the £350 million, right.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: We have heard from some local authorities that have now bought and prepared their own homes and yet cannot get Ofsted to come and inspect them. I was talking to a chief executive who said, “I’ve just said we are going to open it because it is better to have the children locally than 90 miles or 150 miles away, and my director of social services says that it’s of high quality”. Do you think there is a way that we could get the inspection for opening a home by local authority leads looking at other local authorities? It strikes me there are 10 places for children currently not being used in the place I am thinking about, and they have two children in illegal homes.

Dame Rachel de Souza: I saw something similar on the Isle of Wight. I am up for considering any mechanism but just think about the unintended consequences. Something that worked well with the free schools was having the DfE do it because it had lots of capacity. You do not want to create new problems by solving old ones. It could be a framework that local authorities could apply or maybe there is some way of dealing with it where there is someone neutral and trustworthy looking at it. We must never forget these are our most vulnerable children.

Baroness Nichols of Selby: I have worked for a local authority for 42 years now, not in children’s care but I worked in adult social care. For me it is the changes from each Government and the times that you have to change with it that impacts the services that you provide. The point was raised about CQC and whether internal people could look at that; that was taken away from local authorities many years ago. We had registration and inspectors within the council and it was deemed that it was too close to the council for it to be doing it.

From my point of view it seemed to work much better than it is now. I totally agree with you about the profit element: there should not be anyone making any money out of children who are in such difficult circumstances; it is just abhorrent. I just wondered what your thoughts were about all the changes over the years and how that has impacted where one change has not been implemented and completed before we go on to something different.

My own local authority has bought many houses and things because it has needed to have children in there on their own. It still has a couple of homes but it is really difficult. One had four beds but we had to go to six because there was a need for that; that impacts the spaces and makes it even worse for people. It has just been such a mess.

Dame Rachel de Souza: Thanks for those observations; they tally with what I hear from children. I sound like I have been really critical. but actually I am really proud that our directors of children’s services have kept going and that we have councils that are passionate about doing things. I speak for children and I still know we are not where we should be.

You put your finger on exactly why. There has been so much change and it has almost become, “Oh, I’m holding this together, it’s knitted together and please don’t ask me to change anything else”. Lots of areas will say, “This is so hard”. We have to recognise just how hard everyone is working.

The challenge is the money is going in the wrong places and we need to think about how to do this better and help to do it better. It does not help if our senior Civil Service is sector-facing and not being real about what reform would take and what needs to happen. That was really one of my key points. I am very grateful for everything DCSs and local authorities are doing for their children. Every time I read one of those cases—such as the Sara Sharif case—you think of that junior social worker with a huge pile when we could have had a unique ID that would flag stuff.

Baroness Redfern: I have a very quick question. You welcomed the introduction of powers for the Secretary of State for Education to cap the level of profit. How would that work?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I have been quite frank here that there should not be profit-making at all. When the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 was going through, I supported some bits and was critical about some bits but I was very clear that we would need more legislation. It was a new Government’s first go at doing children’s policy and there were some bits that were somewhat vague like that.

If your test of policy is what is actually happening in delivery, it has not been worked out how that would work. I have had various conversations about it. Personally, I would have avoided profit-making and started to build up social enterprises and others that were really interested and well-equipped to do this.

Baroness Redfern: That is technically what you would class as profit, is it not?

Dame Rachel de Souza: Yes.

Baroness Nichols of Selby: You have obviously mentioned foster care and we are well aware that there is a lack of foster carers throughout the country. The aim is to recruit 10,000 new foster carers, but we have had those kinds of figures before. I just wondered what your thoughts were around the reasons people are not wanting to foster anymore and whether it is linked to the complexity of some children who are needing those placements.

Dame Rachel de Souza: Those 10,000 new placements are welcome; I am really pleased about it. We have a passionate Children’s Minister who has made this his thing and wants to do it, so who could not support it? I was somewhat disappointed that we have a campaign without a proper website and pick-up of potential foster parents.

When I talk to foster parents, the thing that puts them off the most is that they get bounced around a local authority’s website and asked about their finances in the first conversation. Now Foster—the Katie Waldegrave project on how we can actually welcome people in—has been really good on some of these. We need a bit of clarity there with a good website and to make sure we are meeting and greeting potential new foster parents properly.

Most children going into fostering nowadays are complex children. Everything has been tried to help them stay at home. It is either going to be neglect, intrafamilial harm, behavioural or special education. We need specialist foster carers and we will always need specialist homes. If we plan those homes really well and support foster carers and families with that new provision, we could actually start to meet the need.

We are getting lots of breakdowns of foster care relationships because of, for one thing, the internet. Frankly, that is why adoptions are breaking down; children are finding information, and there is this desperate need to do that. We are going to have to do fostering, adoption and all these things in a different way. Breakdowns also happen because of complexity.

As Baroness Hollins knows, severely disabled children would want to be at home but sometimes cannot or need some respite or help. It is often older teenagers if it is not children with special needs or behavioural issues where families cannot cope. We could be making that foster and children’s home relationship so much more carefully planned to what is needed.

The Chair: Do you think the 10,000 campaign is explaining well enough the range of opportunities to foster? Rather than taking one club approach and saying, “We need 10,000 more”, is it giving the information about the range of opportunities that foster parents could have?

Dame Rachel de Souza: Let me give you an analogy. Back in January 2024, I was on the attendance alliance and the DfE did a “Moments Matter, Attendance Counts” campaign. Unfortunately, the campaign said, “Even if he has a runny nose, take him into school”. Parents went mad; everyone hated it. It should have been, “Go to school; you’ll do these great things. You could have a future”.

There is a little of that with this foster campaign, with lack of clarity around, “Is this your for ever child? Are you going to become a foster carer who is going to have lots of children?” I have had a first look. I commend the Minister for doing this campaign and seeking 10,000 children. We need to learn from our campaign mistakes in the past and make sure it is really carefully thought out.

Baroness Hollins: While you are talking, it makes me think about the importance of support for young families to try to avoid family breakdown. I just wondered whether there needs to be some more joined-up thinking about this and whether you have any thoughts. How many children are in care because their families did not get the support they needed?

Dame Rachel de Souza: Some children I worry about the most are what we call children in need. They are a large group of 1 million or so and if you look at their outcomes they are pretty bad. They are children on the edge of care who are in families who need support.

Two years ago, I did a piece of work called “The Children’s Plan”, where I used my statutory powers to ask every headteacher in the country to respond to their biggest concerns. Some 90% of headteachers responded, which was massive. The biggest concerns were wider support for children with special educational needs, support for children in poverty and children’s social care support wrapping around children. The heads said, “We’ll help. We’ll be at the centre of this”. Many years ago, we started looking at full service extended schools to do this. The heads said, “We’ll do it, but our biggest problems for our children are this”.

Another point to back up what you are saying is that everybody would say to me about the children’s lottery that, “The bottom places are just poverty”. Poverty was a big factor, but not the only one. Haringey Council came 15th best in the country. There is lots of poverty in Haringey, but do you know what it does? Massive investment in early years, supporting families and early years’ education, and it has paid off. Hounslow—next door—is in the bottom 10.

We know how to make a difference. We have the evidence of decades. Again, it is using schools, trusted places or whatever community facilities they are to bring people in. We could avoid so much of what we see. There are good policies—such as the family help policies—that are starting to happen, but they need to be rocket-boosted.

The Chair: Do you see some new initiatives really trying to recreate Sure Start but it is not co-ordinated or comprehensive across the country?

Dame Rachel de Souza: Yes. As my final project, I have just done a survey of children in this country asking various questions such as what they would like for the future. I am going to present it in Parliament on 8 February; you are all welcome. I literally went everywhere from Hadrian’s Wall to Land’s End, so I have seen every family hub. We get fantastic outcomes in local areas—whether it is Manchester or Blackpool—where the children’s centres that were maybe once Sure Starts but now call themselves something else, schools, the council and everyone come together and support children and families from birth right through to 18.

One thing we know from the data is there is nothing like early years’ education for outcomes for children. We must not be afraid of talking about families. I did a family review for Kemi Badenoch in 2022 when she was Equalities Minister. The people of this nation told me the thing they care about most is family in all its forms. They want to receive support first from their family, then trusted friends. If they have to receive support from the state, they want it to feel local, familial and sustained. We just have to dive in there.

Baroness Redfern: What additional focus or further changes would you like to see in the coming months and years to improve outcomes for children in care?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I was pleased to see corporate parenting in the Bill; I want to see it delivered. When I talk to children in care in prison or hospitals who cannot go home, I do not see corporate parenting as it should be. I visit every children’s hospital.

I have been going to see a trauma surgeon who had great outcomes for kids who had been stabbed. The chief nurse took me down to a ward and a little girl walked past me with curly hair—a sweet little thing—attached to a machine. She said, “Hi”. I said, “Oh, who’s that?” The nurse said, “Oh, she lives here”. I said, “What do you mean she lives here?” That little girl could have been at home; her parents did not want her. Social care did not have any beds. She had never felt grass between her toes or been out to play. That was 2025.

It is obvious what we need to do: true corporate parenting. We need to treat children in care as if they were our own. We need to recognise—across services—the reality of what is happening to children. I have upset Baroness Hollins now. That is not the only one. I have looked at every single child in hospital because they cannot be at home.

Let us reform the Children Act by taking in extra-familial harms, get the overlay of services so they can work together and underpin it properly with a proper unique ID that is functioning—not a race to the bottom, but a, “What could a great thing look like for parents and professionals?” Then let us treat every child in care as if they were our own. Some will need to be in homes; some will need to be in justice beds, but we should be fighting for a family for them all. That is what the people of this country believe is the best way to bring up children.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: In several answers you keep coming back to unique IDs and data systems. As you will remember from your school time, we set out that whole unit in the late 1990s. It takes a while to come into practice, but once it is there it is fundamental to informing individual providers and the whole system. You get data that you could never get before. I have seen this in American states, in Pakistan, in the Home Office with crime. Do you think one of our top recommendations should be to get that unique identifier and data system? It is very boring work at the beginning, but once it flies it is amazing.

Dame Rachel de Souza: It is so essential. First, Sir Michael, I just want to pay my respects. I am not a tech person, but your work using data—particularly in Punjab and other places—is an inspiration to us all. I cannot believe that I have to look at Punjab to think about how to make our data better, but it is true and joined up better so there you are. I recommended the unique ID from the second I walked through this door because I could see the silos; the silos are real.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: Why have we not been able to get there?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I am near the end of my term, so I can be honest and upset everybody. Health was dying to do it because it gets this stuff and said, “We could use a health number. We could do this”. There were lots of bright people in health. Children’s social care owned it. It had mid-ranking civil servants put on it and my constant push is, “Do not let this be a race to the bottom”.

Everybody started worrying about sharing of data. I said, “We are allowed to share data”. It was great to see it in the manifesto; I supported it but we are still no further forward. My push is like yours: we do not go for minimum standards; let us share a little data between health and whatever if we dare. It needs to be digitised red book and every parent needs to be able to look across. In your area, where are the libraries and the children’s things? It is about the sharing of the data.

I said to Bridget Phillipson at the time, “If you just do this, in five years this would be the one thing that would be transformative”. I am afraid we are not there yet. I have hope because Mayor Burnham was working with me on it before he went into role as Prime Minister. I just hope he has not forgotten it; maybe we could remind him. He was going to try to do a local one, but we need a national one. It is just so obvious.

I sit with Laurence Taylor from Counter Terrorism Policing looking at ultra-violent children. I reported on Prevent and we discovered that most children now are not fundamentalist Islamists or far-right; they are a funny mix, violence-obsessed and recruited on the internet. I said, “Find me those children; let’s talk to them”. When I traced every kid who had been referred to Prevent and the 85% who bounced back—many were those ultra-violent children—the data went nowhere.

In the Southport inquiry, I said, “We need unique ID, one front door, named DCS”. The Southport killer bounced back so many times and had bits of whatever and nobody joined it up. Unique ID is life and death and it is a great experience for every parent and child.

The Chair: We are about to debate the single patient record in the Health Bill. Do you think this is the latest opportunity to try to make progress on this agenda, which I totally support?

Dame Rachel de Souza: I know children’s social care will not thank me for this, but if it cannot do it, give it to health. Another service could lead. In Australia and other places, health leads in multi-agency and does really well. This could be the moment.

The Chair: It should not be siloed to health, as you are suggesting.

Dame Rachel de Souza: It has to be everyone.

The Chair: It needs to open up from that point.

Dame Rachel de Souza: Please put justice in. I still have children I find whose parents have been taken to prison. They are sitting at home and nobody knows they are there. There has to be a way that the safeguarding and care of those children becomes a priority and a unique ID could help a lot.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: The learning opportunity—when it has run for 20 years—will be amazing because if somebody ends up in a particular state of affairs when they are 25 you can look back and see the entire trajectory they have been through.

Baroness Nichols of Selby: People hide behind data protection and are not prepared to take risks. When you look at the issues that we have talked about today, what is wrong with all that data being shared with each agency that is involved? It horrifies me that for everything you look at somebody will say, “You can’t do that because of data protection”. It has just gone mad.

Dame Rachel de Souza: I visited Bedfordshire Police, which is fab; its VRU is great. It was not a small spreadsheet; it was a big one. That is just lovely Bedfordshire.
