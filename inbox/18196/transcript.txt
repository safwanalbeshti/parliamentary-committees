Q2 Chair: First, I want to say thank you for your service. I know it is not easy to do the work that you do. I am going to start with a question on wildfires. I am not sure whether you also went to the Government’s wildfire summit on Friday. If you did, it would be good if you could weave some of the answers through when responding.

The wildfires took place over the summer. Can you describe the scenes that you saw as a firefighter and cover how that is impacted your work and what you would like to see the Government implement? I understand that on Friday the summit discussed the wildfire action plan. I understand that that is something that the fire service sector has been calling for. If that is the case, tell me what you would like to see in it.

Simon Tuhill: Thank you for the question. Perhaps I will pick that one up initially. I attended the major incident wildfire in Stourbridge in the west midlands. It is fair to say that the scenes that day will stay with me for ever. I have nearly 29 years’ service in the fire and rescue sector, and the things I saw that day were something that I have never seen before. The scene of devastation across such a wide area of a relatively urban part of the west midlands was deeply upsetting.

The firefighters were magnificent. My firefighters went above and beyond what could be reasonably expected. The incident was physically demanding for them. It was the hottest day of the year and very windy. Not only was it physically demanding—and there were firefighters who had to be treated by paramedic colleagues for heat exhaustion—but it was also psychologically really taxing.

At that incident, 20 residential properties were fully consumed and destroyed by fire, and that is something that my firefighters took really personally. We had members of staff crying at the scene because they felt they were not doing the most they could, and of course they were working in incredibly difficult conditions.

At one stage, almost every single resource in West Midlands Fire Service was deployed not just to Stourbridge, but to the nine other significant fires going on across the west midlands, including three other major incidents, and the hundreds of other fires that were taking place, even those smaller fires. The impact on us was huge and the impact on my staff has been quite profound.

To speak to the Government’s commitment around the wildfire action plan, the fire sector really supports that. We know that fire and rescue services are not going to be able to fix the wildfire challenge on our own. This takes a whole-of-society approach. That includes fire and rescue being adequately resourced and prepared to deal with wildfires, meaning that we have the right PPE for our firefighters, the right vehicles, the right equipment and indeed the right numbers of firefighters. That day showed, not just in the west midlands but across the whole of the country, a sector that was incredibly stretched due to the loss of firefighters over the last 15 to 20 years.

It is not just the fire and rescue sector that can do the response part of wildfires. We also need government support around bringing together the whole of the community. That includes local authorities that can support with public space protection orders so that we can enforce people behaving responsibly when we are at those times of significant wildfire risk. It includes the use of national alerts to let people know what they should and should not do. It includes landowners, the Countryside Alliance and the National Farmers Union thinking about responsible land management, particularly in the west midlands where there is urban-rural interface. This is not just burning in farmers’ fields, peatland or moorland, but it is impacting on residential properties, which is something that many services are now starting to face as we go forward.

Then there is the civic duty of the public behaving responsibly. The wildfire in Stourbridge was deemed to be caused accidentally, but over the course of the summer we had several wildfires caused by people behaving a bit more irresponsibly, with the use of disposable barbecues or careless disposal of smoking materials, or indeed deliberately.

One significant incident that happened on the 13th in Highter’s Heath in south Birmingham that resulted in the total loss of six houses was deemed to have been started deliberately. There is a whole-society approach not just around regulation but also society having that civic duty to come together as climate change makes the role of the fire service more complex.

Q3 Chair: That is really helpful, particularly your perspective on the whole-society approach. You mentioned the national emergency alerts. As we saw for the first time last year—I believe it was in August—national emergency alerts were issued in relation to the wildfires. There was a bit of criticism, as you know, in terms of whether it met the threshold and why the alert was issued, particularly with the wildfire. Do you know whether you have done any lessons learned to see whether this was effective and is something that should be embedded in the system going forward?

Simon Tuhill: The sector’s belief is that that message was effective. In fact, we would have advocated for it to have happened earlier. That day there were wildfires going on across the whole of the UK and major incidents declared in many fire and rescue services. That message, telling people what they should and should not do, was one of the crucial bits of public messaging that enabled us to get the hook in to speak to the public about how we would like them to behave at a time of extreme risk of wildfire.

Q4 Alan Strickland: Building on what you have mentioned so far, I am interested to hear, given the lessons from the wildfires and that climate change means we might expect more of those, how your plans and tactics might change. Is there a need for different innovative methods? I am thinking about the commitment around military air support. Different fire brigades during the summer were testing out different new bits of kit. How might we expect your approach to change and what support might you need from Government to help you do that?

Jonathan Smith: Funnily enough, back in 2022 the London Fire Brigade experienced significant challenges with wildfires, as colleagues on the panel will remember. Particularly 19 July 2022 will live long in the memory. There was 40° to 41° heat that firefighters had to operate in.

We have had to completely overhaul our operational response to deal with these challenges. In a bizarre way the fact that the London fire commissioner is having a conversation about wildfire probably tells you where we now are. Six or seven years ago, we were not having that conversation. We had had some significant grass fires and some significant challenges previously, but nothing to what we saw back in 2022. We as an organisation, specifically the LFB, had to learn a number of lessons to make sure that we were as prepared as possible. Over the last three to four years, we have overhauled our training provision. We have brought in new kit and equipment. We have different vehicles. Our incident commanders deal with wildfire in a different way.

As you will appreciate, we had been set up predominantly as an urban fire and rescue service. We were still dealing with the aftermath from the Grenfell Tower inquiry and the subsequent recommendations that the LFB was having to deliver against. To then find ourselves dealing with the incidents that we faced back in 2022 has meant we have had to re-posture ourselves as an organisation. Now, as we approach the summer period, there is a long run-up.

Simon’s point is right: this is a whole-society approach. One thing that we now do is to write to all the local borough chief executives to talk about how we manage open green spaces and encourage people to behave in a different way when we get into the summer. The emergency alert is something where, as a country, we have to get into that habit. In southern Europe, for example, that is a very common occurrence. These alerts will be sent out and it changes the way that people behave and think about wildfire.

If I talk parochially from the LFB’s perspective, we have had to completely overhaul our operational posture for the summer period. We brought in significant numbers of firefighters over the summer period because we were anticipating significant heat. On the hottest day of the year, which I think was 13 August, we got up to 38°C or 39°C. We use a system called recall to duty, so we have 2,500 firefighters who are signed up to a voluntary recall to duty. When we anticipate particular challenges around our operational capability, we are able to bring additional resource in. It meant that, on that day, we crewed every single pumping appliance and every single specialist vehicle to try to maintain the position that we had in London.

If I may go through the call figures, in July we were 40% up on the previous three-year average. In August we were 49% up on the previous three-year average. We have done 20% more wildfires in 2026 than we did in 2022, which is the biggest challenge to us as an organisation. We are having to think very differently about how we posture ourselves as an organisation to deal with what is now, to use that hideous phrase, the new normal.

We are thinking about next summer now. I know that we will need additional PPE. We will need additional kit and equipment. I very much welcome the Government’s positioning on wildfire at the moment, particularly the summit, but we would need to see the resource flow in to fire and rescue services right across the country in order to deal with what is now a significant challenge. This has been rising for some time. We experienced it particularly in 2022, where we lost over 40 houses in Wennington and Dagenham in particular, because the fire had spread in a way that we simply had not anticipated before.

This year we have been able to hold it in London and support the national endeavour. We have sent 300 LFB firefighters to major incidents in England and the south of Wales. Nearly 60 vehicles have been deployed to support the national resilience effort. That is what this is going to take. This is a UK issue for us to contend with. The national resilience of UK fire and rescue services has been severely tested over the summer period. We now have to think very seriously about how we make sure every fire and rescue service has the resource it needs to deal with these issues, which we know we are going to face for the foreseeable future.

Q5 Chair: I wanted to follow up on what both Jonathan and Simon said, particularly about the increase in resources, the different vehicles and PPE. Have you identified new technology that you think should be factored into part of what the Government are looking at, which will help mitigate particularly stuff such as wildfires and other things that you are responding to?

Jon Lacey: An alternative option is a predominantly on-call fire service. Most people in Suffolk Fire and Rescue Service have day jobs and then respond by pagers to the emergencies as and when they happen. They are hugely dedicated people giving up their time to respond to emergencies every day of the week and evenings. Under normal circumstances that works quite well, but on the Dunwich fire during the summer that we experienced in Suffolk the turnaround of staff was hugely challenging.

Technology can help to a point there. As soon as we brought air assets in to the Dunwich fire, that quenched a large amount of the fire quite quickly. We had to take that at a risk of cost, because Suffolk Fire and Rescue Service already runs at a budget pressure this year, meaning that any other resources that we bring in for spate conditions, such as wildfires, floodings or coastal inundations, come at an extra cost that we then need to find through our fire authority, which of course is challenging.

In terms of the technology available, it is encouraging to hear that the RAF may well be providing us with firefighting aerial capability. The challenge there is that that may then come at a cost to local authorities to be able to fund that, which will be impossible because we are already running at pressure.

Drone capabilities and technology such as robotics also can play their part in wildfires and other emergencies. The challenge there is that certainly local authorities and fire authorities do not have the capital investment to bring in this technology. We certainly do not have the capital and revenue budget to keep up with emerging technology that could be so useful to us and could prevent fatalities in the public, but also firefighter fatalities.

The budget also comes into effect where we have seen cutbacks over recent years, and certainly over the decades, where firefighter numbers have been reduced. That provides its challenges. Dunwich is an example. Where we have on-call firefighters, you can only rotate them around a shift so many times before you are putting their pager down to bring them back to another fire, or to the Dunwich fire only a few hours after they just left.

We think that national resilience and the capability there should be looked at for investment to make sure that, when you press the button, it is not just London that is supporting but all the other fire services. Another 14 came to assist Dunwich. Just as Dunwich finished, we then put Suffolk firefighters across the country to Hampshire and to Wales to help them when we were already stretched, because we knew they needed that help.

There is technology out there. It needs to be worked through carefully. Support through capital and revenue investment will help us to embrace that, take it on board and move it forward at pace.

Q6 Sagal Abdi-Wali: I wanted to come in on something that Mr Smith mentioned about being prepared for next summer. We are seeing a lot of extreme weather. It is a lot more frequent now and it is going to probably impact us in the winter as well, especially with flooding. I understand that that is not a statutory duty for the fire brigade. I wanted to understand how you are prepared for other extreme weather incidents, especially flooding if it is not part of your duty.

Jonathan Smith: It is a key question for us. Yes, we are now anticipating warmer, wetter winters as well as longer, drier summers. The kit and equipment that we have purchased to deal with wildfires—we have these terrain support vehicles—during the summer give us a four-by-four capability to deal with wildfires and in the winter give us an enhanced flooding capability. We are looking now at our next generation of pumping appliances, which are going to need to be as multifaceted as possible. We are going to have to make sure that we are postured correctly in the summer period to deal with wildfires, but now there is what we are seeing in the autumn and winter.

There was a week in April that sums up where we are at the moment. On the Monday night, we had flash flooding in south-west London and what are referred to as spate conditions, so we had significant numbers of calls coming into the control room. We had to then think about how we were deploying our resources. At the end of that week we then got temperatures of 30° to 32°, all in the same period. In a microcosm, that gives you an understanding of some of the challenges for all fire and rescue services.

This is a global issue clearly, not just for the UK, but it is something we are having to think about very carefully in our longer-term strategic planning and when we are buying kit and equipment. There is the PPE challenge. What we are looking at next year is lightweight PPE that can be used in a wildfire scenario, but in a whole range of other rescue scenarios as well, so it is multi-purpose, because that is the nature of modern firefighting.

Last year, we went to 44 different incident types as a fire and rescue service. The role continues to expand. The risks and challenges that fire and rescue services are dealing with continue to expand. That is something we have to think about very carefully in how we plan for the future.

Kathryn Billing: In Cornwall we had the first two named storms that hit us this week. For areas such as Cornwall, rurality really matters. We have dispersed communities, long travel distances and critical infrastructure vulnerabilities among our communities, and they become isolated really quickly. We have touched on wildfires and then gone more broadly into storms and flooding.

We have to look at the risks that we are facing as a fire and rescue service. They are changing significantly and quickly. The question for all of us is whether the resilience arrangements that we have in place are changing quickly enough to keep up with that pace of change. If we are serious about committing to building resilient communities, which touches on wildfires, flooding and storms—it is the whole piece—from my perspective fire and rescue services need to be seen as part of the nation’s resilience arrangements and critical to that infrastructure, not simply about that emergency response piece.

If we look at it at the macro level, we support nationally. We are sat there as a critical part of the UK’s resilience architecture. It is not just the fire piece. It is the critical national infrastructure, the support for energy resilience, and food security more broadly. We respond to the impacts of extreme weather, as we have just pointed out. If we then look at that meso level, we bring together partners through our local resilience arrangements. We connect places and communities. We strengthen resilience and we are there really often to support community cohesion. At the micro level, we work directly with individuals, the most vulnerable people and families, to safeguard and build capacity.

The value from the fire and rescue service perspective is that we operate all three of those levels. We do that simultaneously. We are trusted and respected. We are embedded nationally, but also embedded within our communities. I have 31 fire stations. They are absolutely at the heart of our communities. My firefighters are predominantly on-call. They are the people; they are the communities. That resilience cannot rely on good will alone. It requires sustained and sustainable investment. From my perspective, the fire and rescue service is absolutely critical to the nation’s resilience.

Q7 Mr Mohindra: I am going to move on to the fire safety risks from e-bikes and e-scooters. What are the trends you are seeing locally in the incidence of fires involving e-bikes and e-scooters?

Jonathan Smith: Someone described e-bikes and e-scooters perfectly to me the other day when they said that that has become the new chip pan fire. It is lithium-ion batteries more broadly actually, the truth be told, not just confined to e-bikes and e-scooters. It is a challenge, because we want to support green technology. We want to see that continue to evolve, but there have been very significant risks with e-bikes and e-scooters, particularly on the black market.

Regulation has a key role to play here. We are really keen to see that the secondary legislation around the Product Regulation and Metrology Act now comes forward. There is a key role, particularly when it comes to the regulation of the online marketplace. We really need to see that come through. At the moment, there are too many counterfeit products on the market.

There is still a lot of education required to make sure that people understand the dangers of self-conversion kits with bikes, and the importance of charging e-bikes and e-scooters properly. We have run a significant campaign called ChargeSafe, which is directly focused on the issue around e-bikes and e-scooters, and particularly safe charging in HMOs and other shared accommodation, where we have e-bikes and e-scooters being stored in escape routes. We are pointing that out and working with housing providers.

It is definitely something we have to keep coming around to. Back in 2020, the London Fire Brigade did 20 e-bike or e-scooter fires. In 2025, we did 210, so it has been going on an upward trajectory. Actually this year, and we think it may well be down to some of the education that we have been doing, it has started to come down slightly, but at this moment in time we have to be relentless about it.

My big ask is that we push forward with the secondary legislation and see that come online. I was having a conversation with the commissioner of the Singapore Civil Defence Force at the World Fire Congress, which was held in London about three weeks ago. I was asking him how Singapore deals with e-bikes and e-scooters. It is through very strict regulation. There are only certain manufacturers that people can purchase e-bikes and e-scooters from. It is very clear. There is proper kitemarking and real strict safety standards that sit around it. There is a huge role for education, but also a huge role in regulation as far as we are concerned.

It is a broader question around lithium-ion batteries, not just e-bikes and e-scooters. Another trend we have seen over the summer period is recycling sites that go up in fire where people are not discarding lithium-ion batteries properly. There is a whole range of challenges that come with new and emerging technology, but of course we want to see that new and emerging technology come through because, going back to our previous conversation around the impact of climate change, we know that that is the route forward. There is a whole range of measures we would want to see, not least, in the regulatory space, to see that secondary legislation come forward, which I think will then make a big difference.

Jon Lacey: Jonathan has explained that exceptionally well about the increase of e-bikes, the challenges that they face and the fatalities, sadly, that they cause. “Counterfeit” was a word that was mentioned by Jonathan there. That is why it needs to be part of a whole system approach to dealing with e-bikes and lithium-ion batteries that are not legitimate batteries manufactured in the correct way.

For a long time, the fire service has worked with partners across the system to deal with faulty washing machines, dishwashers and a whole load of other things. That is why we need close partnership working with trading standards across the board to make sure that we are all working on this together, that whoever has the legitimate powers is able to deal with these, that the resources at the import area, such as Felixstowe port, are taken seriously, and that we have the right resources in there to prevent them entering the country in the first place.

Q8 Mr Mohindra: Do you think we need to get to a kitemark standard? Mr Smith referred to that.

Jonathan Smith: You look at the role that regulation can play. A lot of this is to do with education, and giving people very simple, straightforward instructions—so that they are able to recognise straightaway whether something meets a certain safety standard—is definitely something I would support. Having listened to international experiences, that seems to be the way that they have seen a significant reduction, as in the Singaporean example that I gave, in the numbers of e-bike and e-scooter fires that they were dealing with. I would certainly welcome that.

Q9 Mr Mohindra: Excuse my ignorance. Are e-bikes and e-scooters a problem nationwide?

Simon Tuhill: Yes. Like Jonathan, West Midlands Fire Service responds to numerous incidents. We tragically had a fatal fire in November 2024 where a young couple and their dogs died as a result of an e-bike being charged in the hallway of their family home. We have launched a really big national campaign—“Fast. Fierce. Fatal.”—which explicitly addresses the challenges around lithium-ion battery fires. As Jon says, they are a problem for us throughout the system. They are a problem when they cause fires in people’s homes, but at the end of their lives when they end up in recycling plants they are a huge issue as well. When they catch fire, we can be on site for many days. That is something where the entire UK fire and rescue service is facing a pressure.

We have talked about the changing landscapes of climate change. This is one of those weird ones where it is an impact of climate change that is not really weather-related. New technology such as that is something that the fire service is having to pick up, alongside lots of other things that we deal with now. Thirty years ago, the fire service did not really deal with cyber threats, as an example. Now my service spends hundreds of thousands of pounds protecting our systems from attack so that our systems are able to take the 999 calls and we can mobilise fire engines. That was something we never used to spend money on and now we do, and we do not get funded to do that.

Alongside the reducing budgets that we have had since 2011, we are also dealing with new threats, whether that is lithium-ion battery fires, cyber or a way more complex built environment now. There is the proliferation of houses of multiple occupation, particularly in more urban areas. Those are all emerging risks for fire and rescue services, and we are dealing with those with less money in the first place.

Q10 Mr Mohindra: Before I hand over, I have one separate question. The life of someone who is fighting fire has changed significantly over the years. How much would you guess is actually spent firefighting versus spent educating and on prevention?

Jonathan Smith: We operate to risk, rather than demand, as an emergency service. When you look at the percentages, even in some of London’s busiest stations you are looking at around 7% to 8% in terms of emergency response. When you start talking about the role that firefighters have around prevention and protection in particular, again in a heavily urban and very complicated built environment such as London, they are now spending nearly 20% of their time on prevention and protection work.

The big one is around operational training, which is always the bit that gets missed. The only way fire and rescue services can keep pace with this changing nature of risk, as Kath was alluding to, is through much more regular training. A question we were debating the other day is that, in two or three years’ time, we will be sending firefighters to risks that we do not yet know about. We are going to have to be as agile as an organisation can be so that we can re-posture, as we have done in LFB for wildfire, for any particular challenge that comes over the horizon.

The big thing for us as an organisation is that, when you need the fire service, you need it, and you need it in numbers, as we demonstrated over the summer period. One of the things that came out of the wildfire summit held last week was that we have to have the ability to flex, as a sector, when the demand demands it, so in a summer period have the option to draw down on national funding to bring in additional firefighters to deal with peak demand, and then have an opportunity to do something differently when that demand is not there—for example, more prevention work, protection work, work in other areas or time available for training.

It is one of these things where we are postured for risk, as we should be. When you need the fire service, you need it there in numbers and you need them well trained, fit and able to deal with what is in front of them. At the same time, the role of the fire service continues to evolve. We have to have that agility in our training provision to make sure we can keep pace with it.

Kathryn Billing: The majority—over 70%—of my service is on-call. That means that they do around about 120 hours on call a week. There is not a lot of time left in a week. They will be called when they are needed. In that context, arguably 100% of their time is dedicated to emergency response, because we will call them when we need them.

I would also add to that point that we need the ability to flex. We did not declare a major incident during the wildfire. We were very close. I had some of my on-call firefighters working in excess of 12, 18 or 20 hours, and less than six hours later they were back again. That is great, but is it resilient? Is it appropriate for us to be asking our people to work in those challenging conditions? Throughout the storms, I had over 250 firefighters out at the weekend. That is half my staff dealing with the storms. Again, it is that resilience piece. On Jonathan’s point, the ability to flex is important.

At the World Fire Congress, Sweden talked about its resilience model. It has people who almost commit to community service. They have different routes of how their young people are trained and educated. Some go into the public service life, but, for those who do not, when the nation needs them or the communities need them, they have the ability to call on them because they are already trained in certain roles.

Simon Tuhill: I was going to reinforce Jon’s point around the fire service being a three-legged stool. You have response, which is the fire engines on blue lights that people classically think the fire service is about. Equally important is the prevention work that we do, often with the most vulnerable communities. The best fire is the one that we have prevented, because we have done the prevention work in the first place. Then there are our protection colleagues, who use the legislation to ensure building safety. It is a three-legged stool. If you take any one of those away, the fire service will fall over.

The demands on us now are so much greater that sometimes we are having to make difficult decisions about resourcing. Jon and Kath have talked about seasonal demand and temporal demand. We know that, in west midlands, we attend 50% more incidents and there is more risk in August than there is in February. We attend 70% of our incidents between 10 am and 10 pm, and that is when almost all of our community work happens as well. By and large, fire and rescue services have quite a static model of their distribution of firefighters. Without investment in the fire service, all of us, as chief fire officers, are having to make difficult decisions around where we put our firefighters and assets.

I would like to have more firefighters available in the summer, as well as maintain the numbers in the winter. At the moment, I might have to make difficult decisions about moving my workforce around, based on the fact that our funding has not increased in line with CPI. One stat that I have written down here is that, since 2011, if our revenue budget had gone up with inflation, I would have £36 million more to run West Midlands Fire Service. My budget is £150 million. That is a substantial loss in real-term spending power that we have had to absorb in the west midlands.

Q11 Chris Curtis: Can I follow up quickly? Jumping back on e-scooters and the fire risk, like many other places in the country, Milton Keynes has a real problem with illegal e-scooter use on many of our roads and pavements. Because of the fire risk, it creates real challenges for the police in terms of confiscating and disposing of those e-scooters, which makes it very difficult to stop people using them. Do you have any thoughts on how we can better support the police with that process, given the fire risks?

Jonathan Smith: This question is interesting, because this was one of the areas that we discussed at the World Fire Congress three or four weeks ago. There is no actual silver bullet for dealing with lithium-ion battery fires, and e-bike and e-scooter fires in particular. A number of options are available. For example, in London, we work very closely with the Met police in having secure compounds. There are sensible things that you can do. You can have an external compound that is away from any life risk, and sleeping risk in particular. You make sure that you separate out the lithium-ion battery or the e-bike or e-scooter from anything else that could be combustible. Of course, those storage facilities do not exist in the numbers that we require.

There is a lot of science going into dealing with this issue of lithium-ion batteries. There are dry powders that are now available. There are particular fire blankets that are available. We have an ultra-high-pressure lance that we can use that can penetrate a battery and flood it, in effect, to prevent it becoming a fire risk.

When we are talking to any colleagues, including housing providers—we do a lot of work here with Peabody—we recommend very safe and secure storage for e-bikes and e-scooters that is away from the living accommodation. The key thing is that it needs to be away from the living accommodation. We need to make sure that it is not compromising means of escape. We need to separate out where e-bikes and e-scooters are, and put additional mitigations in place that will stop any spread of fire.

In terms of actually resolving the issue of lithium-ion batteries themselves, science is still playing a bit of catch-up, if I am honest with you, in terms of giving us, as a fire and rescue service, the additional measures that we need.
