Dr Igi Moon: I guess at the moment we have a broad remit that offers private services, NHS services and voluntary sector services. On the MOU, we have organisations that have been brought together with the idea that some of them work specifically around sexual orientation and/or gender identity. Together, we try to make sure we cover different issues that may arise. I will stop there; I don’t want to labour this task.

Dr Alison Berner: With regard to NHS provision, I will speak simply regarding gender identity healthcare. We have a number of gender identity clinics or gender identity services—the names can vary slightly—operating across the UK. The current number is approximately 10. Some are fully commissioned services and others are pilots. They are seeing individuals over the age of 18.

There are also a number of new, young persons gender identity services that have emerged over the last two years, following the closure of young persons services at the Tavistock. Those services are not currently referring onwards for any other form of medical intervention.

In the case of adult services, hormonal therapies are then recommended and delivered by general practice. Recommendations around surgeries are then passed to a waiting list management service, which then passes those on to a number of surgical providers for both gender-affirming chest surgeries and genital surgeries.

Chair: Lord Hope, do you want to come in on that point?

Lord Hope of Craighead: Yes, I am interested in the provisions in the Bill that deal with conduct, which is provided as part of a health service. Should it distinguish between the two? Are there differences between the two services?

Dr Alison Berner: We have a remit within gender-affirming healthcare to arrive at a diagnosis, and that is to allow for the provision of medical care—so, for hormones and surgeries. We have to have a diagnostic label to recommend treatment. Within the context of those services, sexual orientation may also be discussed, but we are not arriving at, per se, a diagnosis. However, in any healthcare service, either of those things might be discussed, and an individual could be subject to a conversion therapy practice reasonably. The Bill is talking about conversion therapy, and, rather than protecting against one or the other, I do not see any need to distinguish between the two under healthcare services.

Lord Hope of Craighead: Is there another distinction that might be drawn on the current of age? I am thinking of children approaching or just on the development of puberty in particular, and the emotional problems that they might have. Is that distinct from adults who might come to you who are seeking a transgender identity?

Dr Alison Berner: Our remit in healthcare is incredibly clear, in that we are within the process of assessing and providing space for reflection and arriving at our conclusion. However, what we are not seeking to do at any point, regardless of the person’s age, is convince them of a particular identity or make them a particular identity. That is very distinct. That is our position regardless of age, and therefore we do not need, in my view, an age distinction.

Dr Igi Moon: I would completely agree with that. I want to be very mindful here that, although I am speaking for the MOU, different organisations on the MOU may take different positions when they submit their material. I want to make it very clear that I am representing the MOU, but I am not speaking for every organisation on it. I would agree with what Alison has just said. From my experience of working within gender clinics, it would seem that sexual orientation and/or gender identity overlap in different ways. It is not in our training and our remit to try to impose anything within that setting; it is to explore and work with people so that they can feel safe enough in that space to be who they are. That is not an easy thing to do, and that is why our training is so important and why we are here today. We do not want to be making somebody believe that they are something that they are not.

Chair: That is helpful.

Dr Igi Moon: In terms of the MOU not separating out children and adults, the purpose of the MOU was to say that no sexual orientation or gender identity is preferable than any other at any stage of development. With the organisations on the MOU, we have always agreed that there is not an age definition. That partly means that the organisations on the MOU work independently of the MOU to address issues related to young people or older people through their regulatory bodies and through their own remits, so that different organisations will shape that in whichever way they wish to do it. The idea was never to impose an age limitation on the MOU as a document. The freedom—the strength of it—is that it does not really want to be involved in that.

Should there be? It is not something that on the MOU we have felt we have wanted to achieve. At the moment, where we are at is that the document serves a position of ethicality and makes sure that people are very well informed. It is about making sure that people can explore and that they are there to offer a space that accommodates what people need—if that’s of any help.

Naz Shah: Yes, that is really helpful. Dr Igi Moon: Sorry, in what area?

I completely understand that, with some of the noise that has been around, therapists may believe that they are unable to be in a setting with a client and offer therapy. But if we look at the Bill and what it is trying to achieve, the idea of falling far below your professional standard offers quite an opportunity for therapists to be how they wish to be in a therapy setting. They are regulated, and we would say all therapists need to be statutorily regulated, either through the GMC or the HCPC, or belong to a body such as the PSA, which all of us do on the MOU.

At the moment, the idea that therapists would not be able to go into a setting and just make a mess for fear of something dreadful happening is not accurate. I have been training counselling psychologists for more than a decade. I am fully aware of the mess that people can make in the work that we do in a broad range of settings. That was not helped by training around gender, sexuality, ethnicity and race not being part of curriculum development. Part of the MOU was to look at curriculum development within different organisations and people would feed that back.

People have to be allowed to muck up without fear that they will be challenged or removed from the professional body. This Bill, in our opinion, would not do that because of the regulatory standards and the fact that we have to be responsible for each other in this profession. If people are being abusive, that is a different matter. Hopefully, in the context of the Bill, we all agree that therapists should not find themselves being chilled by conversations. Dr Alison Berner: Provided that they are a regulated professional, that provides the ability to enforce the healthcare practice appropriately. Chair: Who wants to go first?

Dr Igi Moon: I am quite happy to go first. When we speak of gender-affirming therapy, we can enter into quite a discussion about what that might mean in terms of “affirming” and “therapy”. One of the issues that has often come up, which I think Baroness Cass moved towards, is this idea of a split between “affirming” and “exploration”. We would agree with Baroness Cass that that split has not really helped.

Most of us who work in the world of therapy, which I have done for probably about 40 years, would say that in our training, for all those who are in training—clinical, counselling, psychologists, psychotherapists, therapists who are counsellors—there is an expectation that we will provide an integrative approach in most cases, unless people are very purist and want to particularly go into a psychoanalytical approach. Most of us would say that it is person-centred—it is centred around the person. We want to be able to provide a space where people can explore the issues that may well be related to gender. Gender may be an issue, but it may not be an issue as a standalone. It is our remit to ensure that, in training and in being with a client, they are offered a very safe space where they can explore who they are in the way that they like, taking the time that they want with the decisions that may well need to be thought through very carefully and spoken about with others—young people in particular. Older people equally need to find a very sensitive, empathic space where they can be understood.

The idea that somehow there is a gender-affirming care and exploration approach is muddying the water, it seems. The strength of the MOU is that it brings together lots of organisations that provide different approaches. Yesterday—I am quite happy to say this, but you might not feel that there is the time—the British Association for Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy provided a really good example of what happens when we have somebody who may be presenting about their gender but may equally be experiencing OCD and ADHD, and their mental health is such that they are fixed on the idea that their thinking patterns—their ruminations—are about, “I am afraid of being homosexual; I am afraid of being transgender.” The rumination—the thinking patterns—mean that your therapist would be able to explore, but in a way that shows that their training has allowed them to explore in such a way that it is sensitive and empathic, and allows us to say to that person, “Okay, gender and sexuality are not necessarily about the content; we can explore and look at that, but exploration may not be the answer for that. We may need to address your thinking patterns and the OCD and put in place particular strategies and look at that.”

I guess I am saying that for those of us who are working in this field, those organisations on the MOU take lots of different approaches, and I do not think it would be incumbent on those organisations to say, “You are this sort of organisation and you are that.” For all of us who ever wish to be in therapy and for those of us who work in therapy, it is an incredibly difficult space to sometimes be in, and we do not always get it right, but we do try to provide a space where people would feel safe and where they can be who they want to be.

Lord Isaac: Can I quickly come in? When, if ever, do you think conversion practices might arise in the approach that you are talking about?

Dr Igi Moon: In terms of gender-affirming care?

Lord Isaac: Yes.

Dr Igi Moon: Conversion practices are really down to the person who is actually doing some of the work. Most of us who work in a therapeutic setting come from thorough training, and we are regulated and belong to certain bodies. If it were to be that a person presented and did conversion therapy, I think they would be held to account by the organisation, regulatory body or the place in which they work. I do not sense that any therapist would want to be providing affirmative therapy. We want to provide exploration, and we want to provide safety.

I looked at HCPC, which is my regulator, and it gives extremely effective examples. In the work I do, I am expected to share in supervision what I am doing with my client load in a multidisciplinary team, where we actually work together to find out what is happening for this person in whatever context. I would hope that we address this seriously, but I would not want anybody to be offering conversion practices.

Lord Isaac: I am certainly not advocating it. I am keen to hear what Dr Berner has to say.

Dr Alison Berner: I think Dr Moon has put that incredibly eloquently. I would add that the big distinction we are talking about here is that, in gender-affirming therapy, you are exploring. The individual comes to their conclusions, and you are then providing a menu of options that might be the right approach for that person at the right time, in a form of support. That differs greatly from leading someone to a particular conclusion about their identify, particularly if that differs from the person’s preceding conclusion, and you are trying to force that on them. I accept that that can sometimes appear in insidious ways, but I think, at the core of it, that is the dividing line.

Chair: That is the nub.

Lord Isaac: That is very helpful.

Dr Igi Moon: In our discussions, this is where the work of the MOU is not just powerful but incredibly thoughtful. The different organisations provide substantial outlines of how we can think about the work that we do.

BABCP will provide this, but I just want to highlight its point. We have spoken about this on the MOU, but it says that it is for therapists and clinicians to “engage in open-ended, evidence-informed and empirically grounded therapeutic exploration of a client’s thoughts, feelings, identity, beliefs or experiences where this is client-led, free from coercion or predetermined outcomes, and consistent with valid consent and professional standards.” As a coalition, we felt that summed up how we should think about the work we do, in any setting and with any population.

Chair: That is helpful.

Baroness Browning: This follows on very nicely from what Lord Isaac asked about affirmation. I am aware of psychologists and therapists who have left their professional bodies because of the need of affirmation. How does that fit with what you have just told us?

Dr Igi Moon: I think it is very sad that people feel that they have to leave their profession, and that it is because of affirmation. I am quite open to speaking with different people about what that might mean.

In the context of the training that I have offered, the regulatory bodies that I have been involved with and the organisations that we represent on the MOU, affirmation is something that all of us do in the therapy and psychology world. I worked in alcohol and drugs for 38 years, so if somebody came in and said to me, “I’ve got a problem with alcohol”, it is not for me to make any judgment about that. It is for me to say, “Okay, how can we explore that? What would that mean for you? How is your life impacted by that statement? How do you feel?” That would be affirmation.

I worked in lots of different voluntary settings with LGBT people, and somebody could come in and say, as people do, “I’m really concerned about the way I am feeling about my body.” That would mean that we have to explore together what that might mean for that person in a very sensitive way, and not with judgment. When I say “affirmation”, I think we mean acceptance and empathy. If somebody were to use that in the wrong way, we would have to consider that in relation to this Bill and regulatory practices, and hopefully with other colleagues. Supervision is a space where we openly speak about the work that we do. I work with a lot of people who are in training and they present quite challenging issues.

We have to remember that none of this is easy—the work we do with each other, the things that we talk about and the conversations we have. It is certainly not easy in therapy when somebody comes in and they are shaking and frightened, and may never have spoken about their life to anybody, and they are saying to me, Alison or any of the people they may be speaking with, “This is who I feel I am.” The shame and level of discontentment that may well be there is incredibly hard work, and I do not think that any therapist should be undermined for that. I think that the Bill offers an opportunity for us to bring an end to practices that are not acceptable and build on that together—we have a vision, so we have this as a plan and we collaborate; that is what we do in therapy.

Baroness Browning: Would it be the case from what you have said that for any condition—not just gender-related issues, but, for example, potential cases of suicide or people who have been gaslighted—your stance, which you just described, is that you accept that that is why they have come to you, and from there, you make an exploration of why that situation exists, not in a judgmental way, but in order to identify how you take that patient or client forward? Dr Igi Moon: If somebody were potentially suicidal, these are questions that we would ask as part of a risk assessment in a very gentle way. One would hope that most of us who practise in the UK, because of the regulations in place and the work the Government has done, can sit with our clients in the way that you suggested and be open and accepting and explore what is happening.

As I said earlier, that is not to say that that is always that easy; we are aware that there are some people for whom spaces where it is about being verbal, where they can say how they feel, is not actually all that easy. I worked with somebody many years ago whose ability to imagine their future was completely unavailable—they had very bad autism. Asking certain questions will inflame how they feel in that setting, so the purpose of training is to be incredibly sensitive to the needs of all of us—any of us—who are in therapy.

Dr Igi Moon: I think the Bill is immensely warranted. It is incredibly well thought through. I am not saying it is absolutely perfect. I am sure that submissions will point what organisations feel. What will it do? For 14 to 15 years, we have wanted there to be something in place. Why? I was going to start today by saying that we have to look back to the people who have dedicated, in some cases, their entire lives to try to bring about a ban on conversion practices. Why have they done that? Mike King, who was a psychiatrist, did a study back in 2009 that asked therapists whether they would do conversion therapy, and they said no—although, he did not put it like that; he put it in the case of, “Would you want to change somebody’s orientation?”. He then found that 17% of therapists did actually offer to change somebody’s sexual orientation. Mike King was outstanding. He went out there at a time when gay men were not allowed to be a parent of a child and when HIV was considered to be something that you could put on somebody’s death certificate. Some people have safeguarding concerns about certain pathways associated with gender-affirming care—just more generally. We want to protect trans people from abusive conversion practices.

Within the group of young people I work with who are questioning their gender identity or are firmly aligned with their gender identity, I know it to be true that their identities may not continue forever or across time. There is a group of young people who will present as trans or questioning at one time who will no longer identify that way in the future. We do not know what percentage that is because there is no good-quality research in this area, but I have met many of them. Therefore, we need to rigorously explore gender identity and the experience of that with young people, particularly when they want to embark on altering their bodies in development.

A lot of young people at the moment use DIY hormones, which might be a safeguarding issue. It might be something parents are very concerned about if young people are injecting things they have bought on the internet. I can imagine that there would be alarming and distressing conversations in the home, and I wonder how this Bill would affect parents’ sense of safety in having those kinds of discussions.

We have to be aware that some young people who have not had their gender identity rigorously, safely and kindly explored with clinicians, and then go through a medical pathway, later regret it in adulthood. We need to think about how we can protect those people too. I have worked with detransitioners who are devastated at having gone through the medical pathway, even though they very much wanted to do it in their adolescence. Those young people tell me that they wish they had been challenged and had much a more rigorous exploration of what might have led to their identity at that point. We really need to protect the detransitioners and future detransitioners as well as the young people who will potentially be trans for life.

We have to remember that children are inherently going through identity exploration. Identity is usually the end of a process, not the beginning, and we need to be really thoughtful about the Bill not inadvertently putting therapists off thinking about that with young people and about the potential change that can happen to identity in adolescence and beyond.

Dr Anna Hutchinson: It is an interesting feature of the Bill that professionals have some protection, just by virtue of being healthcare professionals.
