Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Nathan Mathiot, Gareth Hedicker and Simon R. Tucker.

Chair: Welcome to today’s session of the Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee as we open our inquiry into export support. We thank our witnesses very much indeed for joining us. The first panel is going to look at the particular opportunities and challenges around defence exports. Nathan, you are from ADS. Just give us a sense of what the opportunities for the UK look like in the defence export space.

Nathan Mathiot: Thank you for having me. For our members looking ahead in the defence space, we have been on an upward trajectory in terms of growth. From an export perspective, we have seen an increase of around a third in the last decade. Defence exports are a bit of a funny thing because they are quite lumpy in terms of how they translate on a year-on-year basis, so we count them on a five-year rolling average; we assess that that was £10.8 billion-worth of export sales, on a five-year basis, in 2025.

We are seeing a shift in the market towards Europe as a whole. Traditionally, the middle east has been the main market focus for the defence sector as a whole, with a big lean towards air as the main component of that, but we are seeing a shift and the companies here will, I think, represent that. Overall, there is a growth trajectory. There is a challenge in terms of how the UK—we work and hunt in packs, it is sometimes said—is going to pursue those opportunities—

Chair: We are going to drill into that in some detail, but basically, when you look at the next five years, what do you see by way of prospects for the UK defence industry?

Nathan Mathiot: The expectation is of continuing reasonably steady growth as a whole, predominantly driven by Europe as the main market.

Chair: Gareth, you are from Babcock. What is Babcock’s view of the next five to 10 years?

Gareth Hedicker: At the moment, we can see growth, particularly in the shipbuilding space and in maritime protection—I will call it that, because that is the big ships but also the hybrid Navy and everything around that. Right now, we are in the middle of a number of multibillion-pound export campaigns. We have one with Denmark, as well as with New Zealand, Chile and the Indonesia maritime framework. From our point of view, export is absolutely key at the moment; it is our opportunity to grow and expand.

Simon Tucker: Thank you for having me. Nothing but growth—my company just announced a 46% year-on-year increase this morning. We have grown from £14 million to £118 million in three years. We are building the Indonesian coastguard surveillance system, which was $180 million, financed by UKEF. There was $200 million for Kuwait; I was in Kuwait on Sunday. We see nothing but growth. We have a pipeline of about £2.5 billion that we are struggling to deliver upon, so there is no problem with that. Exports and demand for what we are producing are massive. We have gone from three people in Somerset to 200 now and 300 by the end of next year.

Chair: Gareth painted a picture of quite diverse sources of demand globally. Is it a similar story for you?

Simon Tucker: We are probably a bit more focused than Babcock. We are about border and territory surveillance. That involves intelligence. For example, in Kuwait, we are building a complete surveillance system so that anywhere between Iran and Iraq and all Kuwaiti waters is covered 24/7 by a combination of USVs that we supply as well as radars and cameras. You can spot, as we did the other day, an Iranian fishing boat, which was purporting to be fishing but actually, from its behaviour, the analytics said, “This is lining up to launch a drone,” and it was dealt with accordingly.

Chair: That is super useful. So it is quite a positive outlook in terms of export growth.

Antonia Bance: Thinking about some of the key facilitators, do you think that the scale and types of financial support that you can access from UK Export Finance are sufficient? Do you think the applications are approved and the finance is provided quickly enough?

Simon Tucker: Yes and no. I think the menu of options is broad. Take my business: there are bonding requirements. We have a pending contract, for example, coming up next year; it is $800 million, for which we will have to put an $80 million bond down. That is quite a big ask for a small company like us. UKEF can support a percentage of that. That is the difference between doing those projects and not doing those projects. In some countries, like Indonesia, UKEF was able to support Santander to lend the money in order to buy the project. The menu is excellent.

The second part of your question is: what could be improved? It is probably the speed of execution, frankly. We are a private company. We have got to pay. It can take a year to two years for these things to go from signing the contract to when a loan agreement is completed.

Antonia Bance: I am sorry—did you say a year?

Simon Tucker: Or two years. Yes, that is the reality.

Chair: That sounds impossible to deal with. How are you dealing with that?

Simon Tucker: It is tricky.

Chair: But how are you cash-flowing that?

Simon Tucker: You have to rely on other things and investors. It is very hard for an SME. We have gone through that pain barrier—we have now become a bit more of a substantial business so we can finance ourselves—but if you are a slightly smaller company, it becomes problematic. We will hold back on some of our investments to wait until that loan agreement is through.

The other thing is the lack of surety—when do you press the button to hire people? For example, we are about to hire another 50 people. Do we hire them now, or do we wait until the loan agreement is signed? If you ask anybody about a loan agreement and what date it is going to be signed, they will say, “Well, we’re not sure.” You will never get a date; that is the reality.

As a small business without a vast balance sheet and a stack of cash, how do you manage that? You go back to your shareholders. With a lot of smaller British companies today—you often see this in the papers—we have got super clever people coming with great ideas, and then the complaint is the Americans come and buy it and take it. That is because there is that gap of financing. That is because you have to survive. If you are a bigger company, such as BAE or Babcock, you have got a bit more of a balance sheet than we have, I dare say, and you can survive that. There is this gap, and that is why things get bought by Americans, because they will put the capital in.

Antonia Bance: I knew it was bad; I had not realised it was that bad.

Simon Tucker: I do not want to come here and slate them—they have supported us with bonding and things like that—but it does take a while.

Nathan Mathiot: Simon paints a good and accurate picture of the coalface. We saw a significant expansion in the amount of finance available to UK Export Finance earlier this year. There were big numbers: £50 billion extra, going from £80 billion to £130 billion as a whole, for a new defence fund. The question is the translation into the customer experience and how that gets delivered in a smooth and speedy fashion so that companies can secure export opportunities. We can increase the number ad infinitum, but it will not necessarily have a meaningful impact on UK companies having a good stab at this.

This Government have clearly identified that that is a significant way to pump-prime and support companies as part of their growth journey as a whole. But it all comes back to having a coherent customer experience so that when a company identifies an opportunity, they can move speedily. That is not just about money at the end of the day.

Gareth Hedicker: At the opposite end of the scale, UKEF was a fundamental part of our £4 billion framework deal with Indonesia. That was made possible with UKEF. We have some SMEs, but to your point, the SMEs contract with us so they would not see similar issues in that space. Certainly from our point of view, UKEF made it possible to do that.

Antonia Bance: Is it your experience that the length of waiting time for the contract to be signed is as Mr Tucker set out?

Gareth Hedicker: I would have to look at that and come back. Again, some of it would probably be about the size of our company.

Antonia Bance: As you say, the cash-flow position would be significantly less difficult for a company of your size, but it is a significant impediment for an SME.

Chair: That is all quite alarming. Simon, do you think you are losing sales, or do you hold back from closing sales, because of the worry that you will not be able to cash-flow it because the finance takes so long to come through?

Simon Tucker: I do not think we are losing sales. In our little niche, there is no one who can really do what we are doing, so the customer will wait. But in time, competition will come into the international market, and therefore we could start to lose business or we might lose capacity capability within our business because we cannot invest because we are waiting for the cash flow. You can also lose momentum with a sale because you can get a customer who is really interested and has the budget available, but it cannot be moved.

Chair: So this lack of speed creates a vulnerability for exports.

Simon Tucker: For sure.

Chair: Nathan, do you pick up this problem from any other smaller members? Is this a pattern?

Nathan Mathiot: Speed for the export support system as a whole is a question. You can bring export licensing into this equation, appreciating that it has a different set of considerations as a whole. On the picture you painted with UK Export Finance, the money is there in theory—

Nathan Mathiot: It is worth saying that the defence export system as a whole has been through a big change. I am not sure if I would paint it as a persistent, widespread problem, but we have certainly heard of other examples.

Leigh Ingham: We are anticipating the DSRB being founded and the agreement being signed in Luxembourg towards the end of this month. I will start with you, Simon.

Simon Tucker: I do not know what that is.

Chair: DSRB is the Canadian multilateral defence bank.

Simon Tucker: The one that we did not decide to join—I read about that.

Simon Tucker: I think anything that takes away the opportunity to do export financing is bad—full stop. If somebody comes up with a finance package and they can deliver—even if they are not quite as good as us on the product—that country will go that way. Financing is a big part of it, so anything that detracts from it is bad.

Nathan Mathiot: We have spoken about this to the Committee before, but from an ADS perspective, we have been hopeful that there will be a pathway, particularly with the change of Government here in the UK, towards UK participation in the DSRB alongside the MDM. We have never really seen a particular tension between the two—they have slightly different focuses on capital mobilisation and demand aggregation. I have read the same reports in the media as you have; that would be a shame, but it was good to see the Chancellor putting some extra firepower behind the MDM. It is not really one or the other, in our minds: they are trying to solve different parts of the whole equation. If we go with the MDM but perhaps come back round, that may be an alternative route.

Leigh Ingham: Why do you think that the Government would decide not to go down the DSRB route?

Nathan Mathiot: I suspect, if I was being simplistic, that there is a money question at the heart of this. A UK contribution has to be discussed as part of it, and I recognise that the Government is trying to square off where it thinks it can make the best investments. We are also waiting on the defence finance and investment strategy, which has been pushed to the side somewhat. You would expect us, from the defence sector perspective, to advocate for all the possible options that are on the table, and that is what we will continue to do.

Leigh Ingham: Gareth, do you want to add anything on the DSRB?

Gareth Hedicker: I think we are in a slightly different space, because financing is one element, but a lot of our larger deals are Government to Government or navy to navy and it is more about partnership. Yes, financing is an important element of it, but for us, it is more the ability to have an integrated solution.

Again, our concern is probably more around how we approach an export campaign and go in as Team UK, which is more important in some of those partnerships we are trying to have. A lot of what we sell is not really about a product any more. There is a product there, but it is more about sovereign capability, the other services for partnership and the support, and about having the Government, the Navy, an industrial partner and academia. That is probably why we see it as an element rather than something that will make a massive difference either way in some of the deals we are doing.

Leigh Ingham: How does UK Government support compare to other countries such as France?

Gareth Hedicker: In terms of the overall campaign, I would say that when we perform as Team UK, we are world class and we are leading. I think that Norway with BAE is an example of that; we are hoping to do the same thing in Denmark. Again, I think that is an example of super-aligned support working together with a common goal.

If we are looking at who to stay on the front foot for and who to benchmark against, the French are a good example, purely because they do that really well. They do a seamless campaign at all levels of Government and bring in wider industry and academia. I think that we are doing that, and Norway with BAE and the Type 26 is a good example, and we want to do the same in Denmark. If we wanted to pick someone to keep benchmarking against, the French would be one, but there is also South Korea and a number of other countries. When we are joined up and at our best, we are world class. It is just about how we keep that integration going and keep developing together.

Chair: Reading between the lines of your answer, the implication is that we do not always achieve it in quite the way the French do.

Gareth Hedicker: We are never going to win everything. I think the difference is that we probably work at it on the bigger campaigns. If you imagine a campaign where it is not just about an initial product but about a long-term partnership and support piece, the trick for us will be looking at how we then sustain it beyond the initial announcements. Again, we are no different to an SME or anyone else. After the initial announcement is when we actually get into the contract, so we have not actually won at that point. We then have another nine to 12 months, and it can run on for some of those support contracts.

We have all the elements and we can do it, but it is probably about how we do it consistently and do it on the slightly smaller campaigns, where you still want an element of that. But you obviously cannot put everything into every campaign all the time.

Nathan Mathiot: There is a lot to agree with in what Gareth just said. It is the consistency that is the question. We see some big wins in the Indonesia contract, in Norway, which Gareth made reference to, and in the Typhoons to Turkey. There have been some big export sales in the last year that speak positively to the partnership needed between Government and industry to land those. Defence is an odd market, in the sense that it is not necessarily the conventional tariff and non-tariff barriers that act as a hindrance to entering market; there is a political layer to this that makes this Government and industry partnership so important. It is about the consistency of that experience.

The reason you will often hear British industry talk about the French doing it so much better is that they have been traditionally less squeamish about treating this as a collective exercise, in pursuing a win that benefits everybody, albeit you slice up the pie in slightly different ways, and being willing to back a particular viable prospect—a UK winner, so to speak—go after that, put all the instruments of the state behind it and work across sectors. We have certainly seen that France will connect the dots between a civil nuclear partnership, combat air and so on. They connect the dots in a way that we are on the journey to, but we have not necessarily caught up to the same place.

Leigh Ingham: And that dot connecting is happening at a Government level.

Nathan Mathiot: Yes. When we say Team UK, we are basically saying that MOD has a role, BIST has a role, the Foreign Office has a role and the Treasury potentially has a role. There are multiple Departments; it does not sit with just the Business Department, with its focus on exports, or the MOD, because it is about defence. It relies on other Government Departments and agencies to weigh in, such as UK Export Finance, to support where appropriate.

Simon Tucker: I agree with pretty much everything everybody said there. As I started saying, we have the menu of everything needed, and there is a process to deliver it, but we are more farmers than hunters. Therefore, we are not proactive enough.

For sure, when I call UKEF and say, “Can we have a new performance bond?”, they will react immediately, and I or my finance director will get a reply straightaway, but then the process lumbers along and takes quite a bit of time. There is that lack of proactiveness and the process that then slows it down. Solve those two problems, and you have a hunting organisation that supports Team UK, particularly SMEs. That then becomes a real winning opportunity.

Chair: When we talk to frontline staff on the ground in countries, they can give us, very eloquently, a catalogue of opportunities they think we ought to go for. Where is it going wrong? They are obviously really keen to get going, but you are describing a situation where that back-up does not quite follow through.

Simon Tucker: I am not going to name names—

Chair: You are under privilege, so you can name whatever names you like.

Simon Tucker: Well, I am not going to name names. The reality is perhaps that what you are being told paints a nice picture of lots of meetings and opportunities, but the difference I saw is that the French will actively knock on the door and say, “Have you moved that bit of paper from that office to that office and dealt with that issue?”

We have gone backwards and forwards on emails on a particular clause for two weeks. Why do we not all just sit in a room and do it for a day? In Indonesia, it took my company to suggest that, fly everyone in and rent the hotel room so we could sit and go through it. We made the most progress, which would have otherwise taken six months. The reality of what is happening on the ground is that we have a tendency to hold back a bit. We were discussing this outside the room: maybe that is just the way it works. Solve that and become a bit more of a hunting organisation, and we will start to be like the French and, as Gareth mentioned, South Korea.

Nesil Caliskan: Building on what the Chair asked, I am very interested in this idea about UK partnerships and the robustness with which those work to seek out opportunity. I was going to ask about the agility in the industry to respond to the ever-evolving geopolitical context, but it sounds more like—do contradict this if it is not true—it is a lack of agility on the Government side that is hindering things. From your perspective, how agile is the industry in terms of being able to adapt to the fast pace of change in the political context?

Nathan Mathiot: I suppose it is easy for us to lob rocks from the outside on some of the challenges.

Chair: We call it scrutiny and constructive criticism.

Nathan Mathiot: Absolutely: constructive criticism. Thank you, Chair.

As I mentioned before, with the Defence ecosystem, one of the challenges has been that it has gone through a big reform process. There has been the establishment of the National Armaments Director Group and cross-departmental changes—this Committee has borne witness to that, in some sense. There has potentially been a degree of inertia injected into the system, just in grappling with some of those organisational changes. That will change and improve over time.

The overall creation of a defence export office—we do not know the formal name of it; that is in provisional terms—in MOD was the right idea, in terms of putting a bit more accountability and responsibility behind that Department for driving defence export wins. But that has possibly meant that, in terms of the connection to other parts of Government, we have not quite got the wiring. There used to be an interministerial group for defence and security exports, which helped to drive better cross-system collaboration in pursuit of export campaigns. Chair: Let us take a specific case study.

Leigh Ingham: You mentioned South Korea. Obviously, the UK and South Korea put together the bid for the Canadian submarines; that recently went to Germany. Gareth Hedicker: Similar to that example about submarine support is the Swedish loss for us on shipbuilding. With any loss, there are always different reasons around why you lost. It is never one reason; there are probably a number of things.
