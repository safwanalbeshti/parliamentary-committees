Examination of witnesses

Dr Nora von Ingersleben-Seip and Adam Leon Smith.

The Chair: We are very appreciative of both of you for taking time to address us today in this session looking at the global regulation of artificial intelligence. We were previously looking at the whole question of multilateralism in the world; we decided that we would look specifically at the way in which the world as a whole, multilaterally, was dealing with the problems and dilemmas thrown up by artificial intelligence. You are very welcome here today. This is being livestreamed on Parliament television, and I believe that there is an audience out there watching it. You will get a transcript of what you say today, and you can let us know of any minor technical changes when you see that. When members of the committee are asking questions, they must declare any interest in advance; to start off, I declare that I am a senior counsellor with the Cohen Group in Washington, DC.

The first question is about technical standards. It has to be said that every week is an education for members of this committee, and I do not think any of us really were fully appreciative of how big and important the whole question about technical standards was. Why is it so important for artificial intelligence, and why has it achieved such a dramatic importance? Could you possibly outline for us the progress that has been made in establishing these standards for what we now know as artificial intelligence? Perhaps you could start off in the room, and introduce yourself before you start.

Adam Leon Smith: Thank you, Lord Chair. I am deputy chair of the UK’s national AI standards committee, which is hosted by the British Standards Institution, BSI. I am also chair of the global AIQI Consortium, which is all about the quality infrastructure that supports those standards that are developed for AI. I will talk more about the quality infrastructure concept a little bit as we go through.

To start with, I think a standard is simply an agreed way of doing things. It is just a document with agreement between a number of stakeholders. It underpins digital technology without computers being able to talk to each other in the same way; without standards, we would not have the innovation that we have now with the internet and things like that. Standards can complement regulation, and that is quite an important goal for them. In fact, 90% of world trade is subject to technical barriers, which are usually related to international standards.

Standards do not have to be international formal standards, but those are the most powerful. Those are agreed through a very formal process and usually turn high-level principles into tangible technical requirements, which can then support things such as audit, which then generates trust, compliance and numerous different goals in the supply chain. Standards already support internationally recognised approaches in many areas.

I mentioned the quality infrastructure—that is metrology, which is done by the National Physical Laboratory in the UK; standards, which are primarily by the BSI in the UK; testing, inspection and certification, which range from forensic crime laboratories through to artificial intelligence; and, finally and importantly, the system of accreditation. Accreditation is important because it is about ensuring that the people checking the standards have been implemented correctly are qualified to do so, are impartial and are doing so in an internationally accepted way, so that a certificate you get in one country is worth the same in another country. There are numerous angles to international policy in quality infrastructure, which starts with formal, consensus-based standards.

One area where standards are very heavily used is in product safety law. The concept of generally acknowledged state of the art is something you find everywhere in product safety—however, the state of the art is not the state of science but the state of generally acknowledged science, which typically means standardisation, typically through these formal international processes.

Importantly, these are multi-stakeholder environments. I am not sure they fit the definition of multilateral in the context of ISO/IEC and CEN-CENELEC, but they probably would fit the definition of multilateral elsewhere. In Europe, there is a particular regulatory approach, which you can see in historical UK regulation as well: regulation outlines hazards and outlines outcomes that manufacturers—or AI developers in this case—should meet. Those outcomes are not technical; they are specified by the legislator. Underpinning those, we then develop technical standards against that regulation, which then gives a presumption of conformity. An AI company can implement a standard, and then the burden of proof is on the regulator to prove that the company has done something wrong; it does not have to prove that the company has done something right.

I link this comment on product safety to the report out today from the Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee called Guardrails for Growth: AI Safety and Security. The report says this: “Digital products have become essential to modern life, but unlike almost every other consumer product, they are not governed by the established framework of Product Safety”.

If you will allow me to set out the landscape of international standards a little bit more, ISO/IEC sub-committee 42 is absolutely key. It works on horizontal standards, with 63 voting countries and 22 observing countries. The power of saying “85 countries have agreed this” is huge in the market. So far, it has published 44 deliverables and has another 54 on the way. I will give a couple of examples. Probably the most well known is the ISO/IEC 42001 AI management system standard. Another emerging example is the ISO/IEC 42119 series on testing and evaluating artificial intelligence. You will usually hear me talk about ISO/IEC as a pair, because they work together on information technology; there are also ISO and IEC, which do different things. For instance, IEC is focused more on electrical devices.

In Europe we have CEN-CENELEC, which is the direct equivalent of ISO/IEC but obviously has a smaller number of countries. In its AI committee, we have 35 countries, and its work includes 10 prominent product safety standards for AI that underpin the EU AI Act. This focus is not specific to frontier or generative AI but absolutely includes it. ISO/IEC and CEN-CENELEC have co-operation mechanisms, which allows CEN-CENELEC to launch projects in ISO, which is great because it helps us get international consistency. ISO/IEC and CEN-CENELEC are important because they follow particular rules of technical consensus-based decision-making, national expert delegations, one vote per country and public inquiry, and governmental involvement is usually quite limited, at least explicitly.

ITU-T is the United Nations telecommunication standardisation body. It is multilateral in the sense that it is formed of government-appointed delegations, but they tend to produce recommendations, not auditable standards that generate trust in the supply chain. Other organisations exist, such as IEEE and ETSI, which are primarily membership driven rather than representing national delegations—although in ETSI there are some exceptions to that.

Still more types of standards can emerge from industry actors just sitting down and agreeing something. Those are typically called de facto standards. Whereas formal standards give government, civil society and smaller developing countries a seat at the table, de facto standards move faster. Formal standards trade speed for legitimacy and stability. For example, the World Trade Organization’s Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade recognises formal international standards as the basis for technical regulation.

This is my last comment: you asked me about progress. If you ask me about progress in CEN-CENELEC, I can give you numbers. I can tell you how close to done we are, because we have a goal, which is a regulation that sets out hazards that must be mitigated and outcomes that must be achieved. From a UK perspective, I do not know how to answer the question: what standards do we exactly want? It is hard to get a consistent answer on that from people.

The Chair: It is maybe one of the questions we can ask the Government as and when we when we get them. Dr von Ingersleben-Seip—I hope I pronounced that right—would you like to add to this?

Dr Nora von Ingersleben-Seip: Let me briefly introduce myself. I am an assistant professor of public policy and innovation at Leiden University and I study who writes technical standards for AI and with what consequences. Many of the answers I will give are based on a study I conducted with Roxana Radu from Oxford University and Orla Hennessy from the University of Amsterdam, where we looked at leadership positions across six main bodies making AI standards: CEN-CENELEC, ETSI, IEEE, IETF, ISO/IEC and ITU-T. Many of the numbers and findings I will be sharing are from that study.

To answer your question and to add to what Adam already said, as I think he laid a lot of the groundwork here, I think standards matter because in the absence of an international AI governance treaty, they are often the only internationally agreed rules. They give firms, regulators and Governments a common language and, as Adam mentioned, WTO rules require Governments to base regulations on international standards. These standards define what “risk”, “safety” and “trustworthiness” mean in practice. In the EU, they are directly tied to the AI Act, which requires that the high-level principles from the Act are operationalised through standards.

I would say that progress has been real but uneven in practice. In our study we found that there is a lot of progress and activity on AI standardisation. ITU-T either has published or has in progress more than 460 AI standards. There are 163 in IEEE, 81 in ISO/IEC’s sub-committee 42 and about 40 in the European CEN-CENELEC committee. Most of those standards cover terminology, management systems, risk processes and sector applications. As Adam already mentioned, I think the most well-known standard is probably ISO/IEC 42001 on AI management systems.

Where progress is needed, and is lacking at the moment, is on evaluating the most advanced models at an early stage. That is where the next area of work needs to be. The UK has already had a nice success here, namely that the UK-led code of practice for cyber security became the basis of an ETSI standard, according to the AI Security Institute.

In summary, there is lots of activity and there is important progress, but the area where we need to push forward is on evaluating the most advanced models, and that is still at an early stage.

Baroness Crawley: Adam talked about the need to get consistency internationally in standards, and much has been written about how international standards have become an area of geopolitical contestation, especially between China and the West. Does this dynamic exist when it comes to the development of AI standards, or do you see standards as an area where there is a possibility of global co-operation? This is a question for both of you: Adam then Nora.

Adam Leon Smith: There are definitely different priorities, and not just between China and the West. The EU is focused on product safety requirements, the US has a keen focus on frontier, and the UK and Singapore are notably more focused on assurance through testing and evaluation. Many other countries have less discernible agendas. It is true that sometimes Governments use standards to try to gain control of a topic—it can be anything you like—and the only way the UK can counter that is by pushing back through strong participation in the process, which is exactly what we do. At the end of the day, international standards is a place for coming together, finding what we can agree on and building on that, and geopolitics is the context that we live in.

The first project I led in international standards was on unwanted bias in AI systems. Getting a very diverse group of countries together to discuss that topic was very interesting, and I definitely learned a lot. We managed to agree that, while we might not agree on the social aspects of bias, there is a mathematical thing called bias that is in these systems and can lead to all these different problems. Regardless of your view of the social issues around bias, it is still important to you because it affects accuracy. We were able to agree quite a lot, just not what particular bias risks were important. I think that is true in many spaces.

But in many meetings, it is not geopolitics only that we have to think about—corporate multinationals and other interest group agendas are usually more obvious than governmental ones. A common dividing line is the need for binding requirements in AI standards. The vast majority of published AI standards have none, and that means that the standards cannot be used to build trust in a supply chain or a system.

Again, I would point to today’s BIST Select Committee report that says AI has no borders. We cannot control it unilaterally, but we can shape international approaches globally through standard setting and other levers. The only place the world really seems to be coming together on these binding requirements is standards for assurance practices—testing and evaluation—which the UK has a particular interest in.

Dr Nora von Ingersleben-Seip: I agree with a lot of what Adam said. There is both contestation about who leads and where and co-operation. What Adam said really squared with the study that I published in 2023 where I showed that there is co-operation on technical standard setting, including among rivals from countries such as China and the US, which regularly work together on technical standard setting in bodies such as ISO, ITU and so on.

What I would say, though, is that there is also a problem with agreeing on higher-level principles in intergovernmental forums, so it is much harder to actually get standards that encode contested ethical principles. As Adam mentioned, there are no binding standards, so it is really difficult to get binding standards.

From my newer data, we are seeing different spheres of influence developing, if you will. Our data showed that about three-quarters of leadership positions in ITU-T—the ITU standards sector—are held by Chinese organisations. On the other hand, there are no Chinese leaders in CEN-CENELEC where the EU’s harmonisation practices push towards European leadership. Huawei is among the most frequent leaders in other bodies. I would not call it one race; I would say there are different spheres of influence in different standard-setting organisations. Co-operation is most plausible on measurement, interoperability and perhaps security baselines, and least plausible in standards and code-contested values.

There is one caveat with regard to the data that we collected. Our data shows who holds leadership positions; it does not show that results were steered in any particular direction. None the less, we see clear patterns when it comes to leadership positions.

Baroness Crawley: How is it possible to agree standards that underpin principles if you cannot agree the principles?

Adam Leon Smith: That is why, when you ask me about European standardisation, I can tell you about progress; with international standardisation, that is much harder. But as I think Nora mentioned there, when I talk about testing and evaluation, I am talking about measurement as well, because measuring is something we can agree on across borders without having those principles in place.

Lord Lamont of Lerwick: How would you characterise UK policy and strategy when it comes to international standardisation efforts on AI? Are the UK Government fully alive to the importance of standards as a vehicle for promoting safety and international trade and co-operation?

Adam Leon Smith: I will answer that in three ways: UK policy, how I would characterise the manifestation of that policy, and government leadership in standards. The digital standards strategy that what was DSIT issued earlier this year has four key objectives: sustained UK participation, ensuring that the ecosystem works—that it is effective, efficient, responsive, collaborative, expert-led, merit-based and open—ensuring that the standards reflect our values, and demonstrating UK leadership. That is the overall government strategy. The UK supports the robust ecosystem of technical standards.

To put it into my own observations, the BSI remains a full member of ISO/IEC and CEN-CENELEC, which is quite an unusual position. It gives us a direct voice in EU as well as international standards, and this should be protected; it is very important because it is such a big market for us.

The UK has some fantastic assets: the BSI, the UK Accreditation Service, the AI Standards Hub, the AI Security Institute, the National Physical Laboratory and the Alan Turing Institute. The effectiveness of all these players is highlighted in the Tony Blair Institute for Global Change report of about a month ago, which talked about AI assurance ecosystems for safer and accelerated AI adoption.

To come to the point on leadership, we have officer roles across many key projects and committees, and indeed at the SDO—standards development organisation—leadership level. That is across ISO/IEC, CEN-CENELEC, ETSI and ITU-T. But those leadership roles, at least when the UK performs them, are not about setting the content of the standard; they are about facilitating international consensus on the standard—or, at least, the UK leadership roles are about that.

Dr Nora von Ingersleben-Seip: I would say that the UK Government are very aware of the importance of standards and AI standardisation in particular, and in recent weeks and months they have linked them explicitly to frontier AI. The Prime Minister gave a speech in front of the United Nations General Assembly on 22 September, in which he committed the UK to work towards a single set of global principles and standards, and the AI Security Institute in the UK has set out a strategy which essentially says that there should be fast agreement on best practices in conjunction with industry, and then later these best practices should be standardised in formal organisations once things are stable and interoperability is required.

I pointed already to the concrete successes that the UK has had with the code of practice that has been the basis of an ETSI standard, but I would also point to three gaps. First, there is the terminology of the UK Government, which is “principles and standards”. Although I understand why that terminology is chosen, there are two different sets of standards: the high-level principles that are agreed in intergovernmental fora and technical standards. The terminology is also somewhat ambiguous, and it does not make it clear which commitments are actually needed. It could be referring to political commitments or to technical standardisation, and technical standard setting in ISO/IEC, ETSI and so on needs different tools from political agreement on things such as ethical principles for AI.

The second gap is that participation in standardisation is extremely resource intensive. So, while UK organisations hold a decent share of leadership positions in ETSI in particular, but also in ISO/IEC and IEEE, they hold none in the ITU standards sector. This can be a problem because being in the ITU-T is important for engagement with the global South, but it is not clear whether any resources are being committed to allow UK standards experts to participate in different bodies in a sustained manner.

That brings me to my third point. I have seen little public evidence of which bodies the UK tends or intends to prioritise or of how it intends, if at all, to sustain expert participation over years. This is extremely important because our research showed that influence in standard setting really comes from being in the room, and being in the room in a continuous manner, so to speak. Being there over years is what brings influence in standard setting.

Lord Lamont of Lerwick: Perhaps I can just ask a very brief follow-up question to Adam. In your opening statement, you referred to bias and talked about bias as distinct from content—I was not sure that I quite understood. In our briefing referring to AI standard IEEE 7003, it talked about the standard providing guidance on how to create algorithms that are free of bias. Forgive my ignorance, but how can you create an algorithm that is free of bias? Can you enlarge on that point? I thought that one of the points of algorithms was that they were often reinforcing momentum.

Adam Leon Smith: You cannot do that. You can move the bias around so that it affects different things in different ways, but you can never have a machine-learning model that is not biased, because bias is how it works, through statistical bias.

Lord Lamont of Lerwick: Right. Thank you.

Baroness Crawley: You can explain that later, Lord Lamont.

The Chair: Would you like to explain it?

Adam Leon Smith: Machine-learning models are trained on large volumes of data. Any kind of dataset you take is a sample of the world; it is not the world. It contains biases towards the way the data is collected—very simply, much of the data on the internet is in English, so it has a bias towards English. You can then say, “Well, let’s take a whole load of French and train it towards that” and it will get better at French, but it will probably get less good at English. So, by doing things to try to change that bias, you have unexpected and different effects, and you can only ever decide which biases you really do not want to have, such as ones that might lead to safety or health issues, and mitigate those specifically, but that is often to the detriment of accuracy in other areas.

The Chair: I think that is now clearer.

Baroness Fraser of Craigmaddie: Adam, you mentioned in your previous answer that the UK has some fantastic assets. I want to come to one: the AI Standards Hub, managed by the BSI. I understand that that was designed to help UK-based stakeholders navigate and inform the international standard efforts. How successful has this been? Can you give us any examples of clear achievements or areas that could be improved, and how do assets like that enable the UK to be well placed or to be in the room, as Dr Nora said, when we are agreeing standards?

Adam Leon Smith: The first thing is that our domestic capacity drives our international success. So, while often initiatives are domestic in nature, they are directly correlated to our influence in international standards, and that influence can sometimes be a little bit intangible and hard to measure. But specific achievements include a searchable standards database—that has gone down very well with a lot of stakeholders—free training and community events, and international partnerships. In particular, I want to highlight the international AI Standards Hub events. No other country in the world is putting on global conferences specifically about AI standards, so it really attracted the attention of the stakeholders globally that are working in standards, and it has strengthened cross-border co-operation and reinforced perceptions of the UK as a constructive and technically credible contributor to the discussions. A government evaluation report contains lots of stats on this.

Going forward, the focus of the hub needs to balance domestic priorities and international engagement, as it has done, at least in the latter period of its work. But it also needs to become focused on converting interested UK domestic experts into participants in the actual process. Without the experts, international standards are nothing.

I would also highlight that other key initiatives about domestic capability are having a similar international effect. One is the BCS-led AI Assurance Stakeholder Consortium, which is about taking the standards that we are developing and looking at what skills and competencies we need to scale the profession in the UK, and we are doing that before the standards are finished.

Also, the NPL’s Centre for AI Measurement is fantastic. It has been funded for some work that it is just starting and, by the looks of things, I will be calling the people there every few weeks asking them to input into a technical problem that I am experiencing in standards.

Baroness Fraser of Craigmaddie: Dr Nora, are events an example to show that, if you need everybody to be in the room, you have to provide a room for them to be in?

Dr Nora von Ingersleben-Seip: Yes, I absolutely agree, and I strongly agree with what Adam said. The hub has done a really good job at showing what a capacity-building initiative can do. So, as Adam mentioned, it has built a searchable database of standards, it has provided training and it has functioned as a community. By its own account, it has well over 1,000 registered members and it convenes international events, such as the 2025 global summit. That is important to get people into a room. Also, a pilot evaluation done in 2023 found that around 70% of respondents felt that it was helping the UK community to engage with and influence standards.

Now, I would recommend, similarly to Adam, that the focus should also be on outcomes and not only on activity. Our research strongly suggests that to influence international standardisation you have to be in the room in these international standardisation organisations. Time and money are a scarce resource, especially for SMEs, academics and civil society. They cannot participate in these international standard-setting processes. Our data showed that only 3% of leadership positions in the six bodies that we looked at are held by civil society organisations.

Training is good and helps to understand standards, but it does not pay for expert time—to put it a bit bluntly—so I would recommend a longer-term evaluation that tracks outcomes. Specifically, I would look at how many UK experts hold roles in working groups and how many UK proposals are adopted. Finally, I would make sure to commit funding for sustained participation.

Again, I have to say a caveat about our data. Our data did not directly evaluate the standards hub, so this is from my own research. However, I feel that, if you follow these recommendations, you can make a real difference to the UK’s influence in international standardisation through the hub and beyond.

Baroness Prashar: Can we explore the role of the national AI institutes? They are a fairly new vehicle but are beginning to play an increasingly important role in assessment and evaluation. Do you see that they are likely to shape the international standard-making environment? Also, a number of witnesses have said to us in the past that the UK AI Security Institute is quite important and leading work on this. Does it add extra weight to what we do?

Adam Leon Smith: These AI institutes develop evaluation methods specifically for frontier AI. The UK’s AISI, for instance, has developed an open source framework called Inspect that is very popular and used internationally. Yes, we need this in standards, because standards need stable and repeatable methods. We need these institutes to generate methods that flow into the formal system, rather than build a parallel one with no accreditation or international mutual recognition.

However, the UK AISI has had very little involvement in international standards to date, at least not through the UK committee or any international committees that I attend. There is a wide variety of stakeholders in the standards meeting, though. I have the independent frontier testing labs, frontier AI companies and hyperscalers. The UK AISI would definitely give UK experts more technical credibility in that room.

I understand that the UK AISI has received new funding that specifically talks about technical standards, which I am quite excited about. I also see that it is hiring for a head of technical standards and best practices, so I hope to see more of the organisation.

Also, AISI’s focus is only on frontier AI. There is plenty of AI out there that is not frontier and that poses real risks to health, safety and human rights. AI is being built into lifts, motors and hearing aids. Many electrical devices around you will be using AI in the decades to come. For this wider problem, the NPL is doing exactly the measurement science work that we need and feeding it into formal international technical standards.

Dr Nora von Ingersleben-Seip: These institutes are a new and interesting kind of actor. They are essentially government organisations with technical teams and, crucially, post-deployment access to frontier models. This is something that standards volunteers typically lack, and I think that gives you real authority in how to test advanced systems.

As Adam already mentioned, the AI Security Institute has released the Inspect framework that, by its own account, is now the standard tool for leading third-party evaluators. The institute has also evaluated over 55 models before release and, this year, it co-ordinated the international network, which published a best practice document in July. So you are seeing real impact there.

We also see in our data that the number of government leaders in international standardisation organisations has increased. It was 4.5% in 2023 and now, two years later, has risen to over 10%. We do not know whether this has to do with the AI Security Institute.

I would say that the UK is really strong in evaluation science, but it is a different story in formal SDO leadership, where you have a lot of UK leaders in some organisations but not so many in others. However, I think the security institute gives you real convening power. You now have the network co-ordinator role, which brings together the different AI security institutes and gives you influence on the international stage.

Baroness Prashar: May I ask one supplementary of Adam? It started out as a safety institute and the name was changed to security institute. Was that just a change in name or a change in focus?

Adam Leon Smith: I do not know. I assume, based on the communications at the time, that it was a change in focus, but I am not sure.

Lord Darroch of Kew: This is a question about private sector involvement in AI governance and standard setting specifically. It can be argued that there are two risks here—either the biggest players and corporations in the private sector are able to exert excessive influence over the process and the outcome or, on the other hand, they do not engage, which means that whatever the product of the process is does not have much real value. How is that playing out at the moment in the exercises that you have described going on? Do you see examples of either excessive influence from the big players or non-engagement and, therefore, a limited-value product at the end?

Adam Leon Smith: Yes, I see both. These things all happen. First, there is always a risk in standards of anti-competitive and collusive behaviour, as well as simply the risk that big industry argues against public policy. The large firms can fund experts and, to reiterate what Nora was saying, the time commitment is not one meeting here; it is three years of continuous attendance at meetings to represent a position. It is a huge investment. The larger firms can obviously do that, but SMEs, consumers and civil society struggle to match that kind of time commitment.

There are some levers available. For instance, the BSI funds a consumer network and it helps pay travel expenses sometimes, although they are relatively small in comparison to the actual costs. There is also StandICT, which is a Horizon initiative—we were briefly out of that but are now back in—where UK resident experts can apply for up to €20,000 a year of funding for their time spent working on international standards.

In terms of how we manage this in standards, where there are particularly strong agendas, we rely on the technical consensus process. So it is not simply enough to argue a point; you have to argue a technical point based on sound arguments, and unanimity does not have to be achieved. That kind of gets difficult when you are dealing with a multinational that can send 10 experts to 10 different countries, who represent those 10 countries in your meeting but you maybe think that they all from the same team.

There is also the risk of insufficient industry engagement, which you pointed to. I particularly see this in areas that are not traditionally regulated by standards. For example, in European standards, there is nobody from an HR or employment AI background, because they simply do not seem to have realised that they are about to be regulated—or at least they have not found the way to the room where the rules are being discussed. That committee is also very diverse, more so than ISO/IEC, because it is legally required to include certain stakeholders, trade unions and consumers. We also have equality regulators in there. Many of the civil society bodies, and even some SME experts, are funded by that Horizon funding.

The other bulwark we have against this is “one country, one vote”. Standard development organisations where countries vote mean that the national mirror committee balances interests before positions reach the international level, which limits the capture that a single firm can achieve, as long as the system is managed robustly.

There is a whole other element here: the independence of actually checking that the standards have been implemented correctly. The quality infrastructure comes with extensive checks before anyone is allowed to certify a test result or a process, because we have all the lessons of the last 30 sectors that were regulated using a product safety approach.

It is a very complex problem. It is solved only by diverse participation, which is itself possible only with government funding.

Dr Nora von Ingersleben-Seip: I would add that what we found in our data is a pretty nuanced pattern. When looking at the leadership positions in the six bodies that we studied, we saw that business is the largest group when it comes to leadership: 46% of leadership positions are being held by business organisations. These are actually most frequently leaders from the telecoms, hardware and consulting sectors, so I am talking here about firms such as Huawei, China Telecom, IBM and Accenture. I already mentioned that Huawei is among the firms with the most leadership positions across the standards bodies that we looked into.

There is another development that I would like to bring your attention to. A lot of standardisation, especially when it comes to what the frontier labs are doing, is now happening outside the traditional standards bodies. We see that, for example, Anthropic has developed the model context protocol, which it has donated to the Linux Foundation. There are other venues now that the frontier labs have started, such as the Frontier Model Forum. What we see is not a clear pattern of capture or insufficient commercial engagement; it is a pattern of bypass. Standards are being made outside the traditional organisations.

When it comes to the traditional organisations, we see that the frontier labs are not in leadership positions for the most part. According to some studies, 10 out of the 20 leading frontier labs are from the US, but the only US frontier lab we saw in leadership positions is Microsoft. That does not mean that frontier labs are not present in these traditional standard-setting processes at all, or that they do not have influence. Our interview showed very clearly that you can have influence from the back of the room, so to speak. So you are not in an official leadership position, because these leadership positions also come with a high level of scrutiny; rather, you are a “shooter from the back”, as some of our interviewees put it.

These frontier labs might be engaging in traditional standardisation processes more than our data on leadership position shows. In fact, there have been civil society studies that have shown that big tech has quite a lot of influence in the European committees—CEN-CENELEC. But, at the same time, they are engaging in these new venues and making de facto standards in these new venues, such as the model context protocol. It is a pattern of bypass, as I said.

As Adam mentioned, what is really important here is to fund UK experts to be present in all these forums over time.
