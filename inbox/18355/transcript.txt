Sir Oliver Dowden made representations.

Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Backbench Business Committee, where we will be considering applications from colleagues for debates in the main Chamber and in Westminster Hall on a Tuesday morning and a Thursday afternoon. The first application is from Sir Oliver Dowden, a request for a debate in the Chamber on the proliferation of unregulated small houses in multiple occupation. Please present your case, Oliver.

Sir Oliver Dowden: From discussing this issue with colleagues over many weeks, I think that many colleagues have similar problems in their constituencies, where people are purchasing family homes in their constituency, gaining planning permission to convert them and then having very large numbers of often single occupants, who are not suitable for family areas, causing antisocial behaviour. There is a lot of frustration from colleagues across party about the impact that that is having on their local areas, and a lot of ideas for how we could strengthen powers for local councils to address that, whether through tightening the licensing or the way that article 4 directions operate.

There have been previous general debates on this issue, including in Westminster Hall, but I think colleagues would benefit from the opportunity to debate in more detail specific measures that could be taken to tighten up the regulation of houses in multiple occupation. From my discussions with colleagues, this is something that cuts across all areas of the country and cuts across party lines, so I think it is something that all colleagues would be interested in. That is why I am seeking the Committee’s permission to hold a debate on the topic.

Will Stone: Thank you for bringing forward this debate; it is an incredibly important subject. We have a monumental list of debates waiting for the Chamber. Would you be content with another Westminster Hall debate, or would you rather wait? It could take a while.

Sir Oliver Dowden: I think I would rather wait, because we have had debates in Westminster Hall, and I have sought to draft a motion that enables us to debate these issues in some more detail. Particularly given that it has been debated in Westminster Hall, I am content to wait, to enable colleagues to have time in the main Chamber.

Chair: As Will says, there is a long waiting list for the Chamber, and a very short waiting list for Westminster Hall, but the Clerks will be in touch with you in due course.

Chris Bloore and Linsey Farnsworth made representations.

Chair: The second application is from Chris Bloore—please come forward. It is an application for a debate in the Chamber on dementia prevention, research, diagnosis and care. Over to you, Chris.

Chris Bloore: I hope everyone has been able to read the application; I want to speak to where this debate came from. I am grateful for the Committee’s time and I thank colleagues across the House for backing this debate application, including my hon. Friend Linsey Farnsworth, whose family’s experience of dementia has made her a formidable voice.

I will start in Redditch: two brothers in my constituency, Jordan and Cian Adams, ran the London Marathon—one of them with a fridge on his back—because their mum, Geraldine, died of frontotemporal dementia at just 52. FTD does not begin with forgetting; it begins with changes in character and behaviour, so it is often missed and it strikes people in their prime—their 40s, 50s and 60s. Jordan and Cian carry the same gene and expect symptoms in their 40s. They then ran 33 marathons in 33 days, one in every county of Ireland, raising nearly £2 million for research. Facing the worst odds of their lives, they chose to fight, and that is the best of my constituency.

This is the national picture: 1 million people live with dementia, more than half a million of them diagnosed in England. Every three minutes somebody else develops it, as one in three of us born today will. Hardly a family in my constituency will be untouched. The decisions that will shape the next decade are now falling due: the first modern service framework for dementia and frailty is due by the end of this year, with no ringfenced funding and a national dementia tsar yet to be appointed.

NICE has already declined the first drug that can slow Alzheimer’s, while blood tests that could transform early diagnosis wait in the wings. Without proper scrutiny, we risk seeing two tiers forming: those who can pay privately for precious time, and those who wait and watch. For Jordan and Cian, for Redditch and the rest of my constituency, and for every family affected across Britain, I think it is time now for Parliament to have its say on the future of dementia care.

Chair: Do you wish to add anything, Linsey?

Linsey Farnsworth: Yes; I am grateful to my hon. Friend for putting forward this application on this incredibly important topic. My experience is lived experience: my father has frontotemporal dementia, which was originally misdiagnosed twice, and my father-in-law has Alzheimer’s disease. They were diagnosed within a few months of each other, back in 2016, so I have been living with this for some time.

However, it is more important to say that this is something that affects families across the country. It is the biggest killer in the UK. Only yesterday, the Daily Mail reported, and the Alzheimer’s Society highlighted, that just 47.8% of respondents to the GP patient survey who had dementia or Alzheimer’s felt confident in managing issues caused by their condition. When we compare that with the respondents on cancer care, 82% of those patients were confident. There is already a huge disparity in the care that is provided.

I would submit that this is a key moment in time to have the debate, given all the measures my hon. Friend outlined in his application. The last dementia-related debate that I am aware of in the Chamber was more than a year ago now, when a Liberal Democrat MP—her constituency fails me at the moment; I do apologise, Chair—put forward a Backbench Business debate in June 2025. That debate was specifically on dementia care. The debate we are proposing is on prevention, diagnosis, treatment, care and the whole pathway for a dementia patient’s life. Given the wide range of contributions that would be possible, and the issues attached to that whole pathway, I would ask you for time in the Chamber to be set aside for this important topic.

My hon. Friend has 42 Members signed up to speak in the debate; there were many others who expressed an interest, but could not commit for reasons of their positions within their party, but it is fair to say that there is a lot of cross-Bench support for this topic. I will probably stop there.

Martin Vickers: Chris, you have a good line-up of 42 people who have indicated support. They are of course, as it says at the top of the form, signing not just in support of the debate, but to speak. We have had a number of recent debates where very few of the people who signed up have actually attended. Are you reasonably confident that you can get a good turnout from those 42?

Chris Bloore: Yes. I think the last application that I submitted to this Committee was my Royal Mail debate, where I had 80 or so signatures and I think well over 100 people actually spoke. This is an incredibly important crossroads in terms of what happens with dementia care and Alzheimer’s, and we would be looking to try to double the number of people who have put their names to this application to come and contribute, whether through speeches or interventions. We are saying to the Government, “This is the time to make the right decisions for the future.” It is incumbent on us to ensure that there are enough people in that Chamber to make that point strongly.

Chair: Are there any other questions from colleagues? No. The Clerks will be in touch with you in due course; I will just warn you that obviously we have a long waiting list for the main Chamber and a very short waiting list for Westminster Hall. If you were minded to take a Westminster Hall debate, it would not prevent you from having a Chamber debate later in the Session, if the debate was oversubscribed.

Chris Bloore: Thank you, Mr Chairman; thank you, Committee.

Chair: That completes the public business of the Committee. We will now go into private session to consider the applications and the allocation of time.
