Witnesses: Flo Lloyd-Hughes, Natalie Pirks and Fiona Tomas.

Chair: Good afternoon and welcome to the Women and Equalities Committee. This is my first evidence session as Chair, so I would like to say a huge thank you to my predecessor, Sarah Owen, for her phenomenal service and fantastic leadership of this Committee. She will be missed, but I am extremely excited to be stepping into her shoes.

Today we are holding a session for our inquiry on routes into non-participatory roles in sport for girls and women. In the evidence we have heard so far, we can see that women are underrepresented in those roles, face many barriers and sometimes overt discrimination. We are really looking forward to hearing from our first panel today. We will hear from Flo Lloyd-Hughes, who is a football broadcaster and founder of The Cutback; Natalie Pirks, who is a former BBC Sport correspondent; and Fiona Tomas, women’s sport reporter at the Telegraph. Thank you so much, all of you, for coming. We are really grateful to you for giving up your time, and we look forward to asking you questions this afternoon.

Q164 Rachel Taylor: Flo, what is your assessment about the accessibility of sports media roles today for young women looking to break into the profession?

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: It is very different, depending on what landscape you look at. If you look at legacy media in the broadest sense, so print and newspapers, there are no strong routes in. It is predominantly older white male terrain, and that is probably represented in press boxes and in big awards recognising and acknowledging the achievements of those in the sports press. If you were to walk into many newsrooms, you would also see that. There is that reality of numbers, but there is also a perception that impacts the wider interest in those roles. As someone who chats to young people, goes into schools, delivers workshops and talks to them about the jobs in this landscape they might be interested in, a lot is focused on content creation in the digital world and not necessarily traditional media roles. There is obviously huge opportunity in the growing digital world that we live in, in terms of how you can be part of the wider sports media conversation and get jobs that way. But when you look at traditional media, young people probably do not see it as a landscape for them anymore.

Fiona Tomas: I would very much echo what Flo has said. For women in the industry who cover women’s sport on a regular basis, they are probably mixing with press packs that are very much more gender diverse than press packs that solely cover men’s sport. It is getting harder, not just for women but for young people in general, to break into the sports media industry because of the rise of AI, and lots of people are masquerading as journalists these days but in reality they are content creators on social media.

Q165 Rachel Taylor: Natalie, do you feel that is true? Do you think it is also true in technical roles, such as camera operators or producers?

Natalie Pirks: I am obviously a lot older than Fiona and Flo, and my experience of working in sports broadcasting lasts two decades. When I started, I was very much one of one, maybe one of two, in a press box or press room. You have to be a certain type of woman to rub along with this very male press pack. Now there is a landscape, certainly in print journalism, where they are losing headcount rather than adding people, so it becomes a lot harder to break into those traditional legacy media roles. There are now a lot more women in press boxes. I will go into a press box or a press conference and there will be maybe five or six women. We all know each other. We all talk. That feels more positive. When you go to events, there are a lot more of us, and that does feel like a step forward.

Equally, when it comes to what type of women are now getting those roles and what type of women are coming in, that feels like a bit of a step back because journalism itself is not quite what it was. For example, if you are a woman taking a journalism degree and you want to work in sport, the advice I would give to women now is very different from the advice I would have given a decade ago, such as getting your journalism degree, working really hard, going into a print room and doing your work experience. It is just not the same anymore; it has become better and harder at the same time.

Q166 Rachel Taylor: Do you think there has been a change with the rise in the viewing of women’s sport? Do you think that has changed the routes in for people?

Natalie Pirks: I do not work exclusively in women’s sport. You two work a bit more in that world, what do you think?

Fiona Tomas: To a certain extent, yes. There has been a small uptick in the number of women-specific roles when it comes to digital and print. I would argue that everyone talks about this broadcast boom, but there has been a print lag when it comes to the coverage of women’s sport and how much it gets these days. You can pick up any newspaper on the shelves today, flick through the back pages, and I guarantee that there are a couple of female bylines, if that. That has been the same for quite a while now.

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: I would say that, in the last four years, you have seen a huge shift. Probably before the women’s Euros in 2022, we were not at a stage where there was a national correspondent for women’s football at every single newspaper. We have now got to that stage and some newspapers even have two, so that is huge progress, but it does not necessarily filter out across the board. As Fiona mentioned, men’s Premier League football still dictates the vast majority of coverage.

The Women’s Sport Trust released its most recent visibility report last week. It is constantly crunching the numbers on how much coverage women’s sport is actually getting, whether that is newspaper or viewing figures. So much of that relies on how the market is looking. Obviously, a lot of legacy media have to shift their focus to a more digital element and therefore sometimes women’s sport or more women getting into those roles might suffer. There is sometimes an unfortunate assumption that women’s sport and women working within women’s sport and media roles are the same. I will not speak for the others on the panel, but sometimes you feel like you are fighting the assumption and they often go hand in hand, but they are separate in that there are lots of women who work predominantly in the men’s game. That was the case for me, probably up until the women’s Euros, when it shifted because there was more media spend going towards women’s football. Up until then, pretty much all my work was in the men’s game.

Q167 Rachel Taylor: How important are mentors and networks within the industry for young women who are aspiring sports journalists to navigate through their careers? Do you think there are enough of them?

Natalie Pirks: They are hugely important. I belong to WIF, Women in Football, and that is an invaluable network of women. It started out as a few women in a pub who were just fed up and had a conversation; it has now grown to thousands of women who work in sport.

We have to talk about the importance of male allies as well because sometimes it can perhaps come across that men are difficult, or men are the barriers. I have obviously experienced that in my 25 years, but I have also experienced brilliant male allies, men who absolutely want to make a difference. They know there is a need to employ more women and bring more women through. We have to acknowledge that as well.

Going back to what you were saying, there is a saying in news: “If it bleeds, it leads”. When it comes to sport, it is Premier League football and it often leaves very little space for anything else. Women’s sport certainly gets a bit of a raw deal when it comes to coverage. Unless they are winning or doing something exceptional, it is probably not going to get on. That is the reality. When it comes to this digital space that we are now living in, if people are not clicking on it, it is perceived as people not being interested in it so it gets pushed down the agenda.

Fiona Tomas: On that point, women’s sport is now at the stage where coverage tends to spike around major events. It is true for football, rugby and cricket. It is not too dissimilar from Olympic coverage really. It is very hard to sustain a consistent cut-through during regular seasons because leagues such as the Premier League are still so dominant. Football is king, it always will be. I know from a domestic perspective at the Telegraph that we cover women’s sport on a weekly basis from the run-of-the-mill season, but it is very difficult to cut through during regular months when there is not a World Cup or Euros happening.

Q168 Chair: Flo, I was really interested in the point you made that you have to fight against this assumption, just because there is more interest in women’s sport, that it is going to mean there are more women in sports roles. I invite the panel to say something further about that. Why do you think that might be? Why would you not see an increasing number of women working in sport if there has also been that expansion of interest in women’s sport?

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: A lot of it comes down to the reality of the economy of the media landscape. Obviously, we are witnessing huge cost cutting at the BBC at present, and changes are being made in relation to that. Women’s sport, and minority sports in general, often bear the brunt of that, and that is the unfortunate reality of the ecosystem. For example, even the BBC’s coverage around the WSL has a short window of pre-match and a short window of post-match. It is not necessarily always guaranteed to be on BBC1 or BBC2, and that over time takes its toll on the number of eyeballs that are going to see it, and obviously things around the number of contracts that can be awarded to pundits to be on that coverage and so on. That is all operating within the challenges of the wider media landscape that we are seeing, and the reality is that very few major sports in this country, apart from the Premier League, have the opportunity to be as aggressive in the media market. Everyone else is fighting for eyeballs, whether that is men’s cricket or rugby, against that beast. There is not always a run-off, and that wider digital view on things means that it has, in a way, been democratised because you can jump in and tell those stories, but it has probably removed the allowance of the legacy media to do that.

Natalie Pirks: The only thing I would add is that, from my perspective as a broadcaster, I do not think you have to be a woman to cover women’s sports, and this is industry-wide; I am not speaking just as a former BBC person. Industry-wide, you would put your woman on it. It is lovely to be able to go and cover women’s sport, and I have done it my whole career and it is brilliant, but you may be slightly doing it a disservice if you do not put your sports editor on a women’s story if it is big enough. Why are you not putting a man on it as well? It does not have to be the woman you have in that day, and I have seen that happen across the industry. I would say there is that element to it as well. As much as we want women to be covering these stories, it does not have to be.

Fiona Tomas: I joined the Telegraph seven years ago, and it was a newly created role called women’s sports reporter. I was one of two. We still have two at the paper but, over time, I have morphed into all sport. While it was my big break, per se, because I came from local news, at the time it felt really novel. Having written for a national for seven years now, there is this strongly held belief that women can only cover women’s sport. They have their expertise questioned more when they are sent out to men’s sport. Obviously, there are exceptions. As Natalie touched on earlier, there are probably a handful of women who are regularly covering Premier League football, from a print perspective anyway—it is different for digital and broadcast. That has a knock-on effect further up the food chain. There are fewer women in editor roles and fewer women commissioners on sports desks. That ultimately means that, historically, fewer female issues are covered by the mainstream media. I am fortunate enough to have been mentored by some brilliant women in sports journalism such as Anna Kessel. She is always pushing the female sport science take, such as how athletic performance is impacted by the contraceptive pill or when a woman is on their period.

Going back to the male allyship angle that Natalie touched on, I have been fortunate enough to have some quite eye-opening, brilliant conversations with male colleagues who want to know more about how these female-specific issues are relevant to sport. If they come to you for advice because they are a bit underconfident on how to approach these subjects, that is totally fine by me. It is not to say they can’t, but there has historically been a belief that women can only cover women’s sport and it is just not true.

Q169 Nadia Whittome: The Committee has heard previously, and from the three of you today, about how women in sports media roles are still facing gender bias, such as having their expertise and credibility questioned and undermined. We heard previously from academics at Nottingham Trent University that men are often seen as an automatic authority on sports and sports journalism, whereas women are having to prove themselves just to get a seat at the table. How far, in your experience, does gender bias shape women’s careers in sports media roles?

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: We have already spoken about some of that, especially around expertise and being pigeonholed based on your gender. For me, personally, a huge angle is around social media safety. That is something that is very much gender-specific in sport, especially football, but is also probably something that impacts a lot of minorities, whether that is people from different races or ethnicities, different religions, sexualities and anyone trans working in football especially. That is something I have experienced massively. It is obviously not something that is exclusive to sport or football, but it is something that I have experienced and is common when you have a more public-facing role within journalism. How you are treated on social media is a huge barrier to wanting to work in sport and media.

Given my experiences, I am not sure what I would say to the next generation. I say to them, “You need to go in eyes wide open about the realities of existing on X, Instagram or TikTok in this society that we live in and how people are going to view your opinions on football, your analysis or anything really, because that is one of the things you have to deal with, the realities of working in this industry. From the minor things to the more extreme end of the scale, which is certainly stuff that I have experienced where you are having to get police or lawyers involved because of those experiences.” There are lots of people in our field who have experienced very similar things.

Nadia Whittome: I am very sorry that you have had those experiences. I just want to express my solidarity with you. It is absolutely disgraceful.

Natalie Pirks: I am very fortunate. I have never had anything like that because I have worked in news for the majority of my career, which meant I was not giving my opinion. I now have a podcast where I give my opinion—

Nadia Whittome: I listen to it.

Natalie Pirks: Thank you. Boy, do people like to tell me I am wrong. Even if I have expressed an opinion that is exactly the same as my male co-star, I will be told that I am the one in the wrong, especially when we did an episode on women’s football; that was enlightening! It is not something I have experienced badly. I have had all the usual misogyny but never anything like that, thankfully.

Q170 Nadia Whittome: I bet you are told you are wrong by men who never watch the women’s game and do not like the women’s game.

Natalie Pirks: Often, if I have done a live broadcast about something, someone says that it was not good or whatever. That must be what it is like to be a footballer when someone who has never played the game is telling them they are very bad at their job. I am sorry, but until you do live broadcasting to millions of people in a stadium where you cannot hear yourself think, do not come back to me.

Nadia Whittome: Yes, it is very “I would have got that penalty” kind of energy, isn’t it?

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: The fact that Natalie is saying, “the usual misogyny”, shows that we normalise it so much because we see it as part of the job. I am sure there are lots of young people who are now so exposed to some of the darker side of social media that they, too, may see it as part of the reality of existing on the internet, whatever field they are in. It is a challenge that does not necessarily have an answer, whether that is the work that Women in Football have tried to do or the Online Safety Act and things such as that. We have normalised it so much now as part of our jobs.

Q171 Nadia Whittome: Fiona, you have already spoken about this quite a bit, but is there anything you want to add?

Fiona Tomas: The reality is you need a thick skin to work in this industry and not be affected by the issues we have just talked about. I am quite fortunate, like Natalie, not to have experienced the scale of abuse that Flo referred to. Unfortunately, it is symptomatic of the world we live in. In terms of young people trying to break into this industry, as we have already touched on, it is unfortunately shrinking. Jobs are being cut left, right and centre. It puts pressure on you to push your own brand as a journalist. The role of a traditional print journalist has had to change so much to adapt to the boom in social media.

An upside from a women’s sport coverage perspective, to counter all this negative chat, is the fact that we are seeing a lot more athlete-led storytelling, which has been crucial to the rise in women’s sport coverage. The bottom line is you need a thick skin.

Q172 Nadia Whittome: In your experience, are there differences between the culture in sports journalism and other areas of journalism?

Fiona Tomas: I have only ever worked in sports, but we know that women are terribly underrepresented across the journalism sector as a whole. The figure from the most recent research is that women account for something like 10% of all print sports journalists, which is still really woeful.

With regard to things such as workplace culture, I have been in media suites where—I am sure you guys have as well—you are the only woman. It happened to me earlier this year when I walked in and there was a hospitality angel working at this particular stadium. She had locked the ladies’ toilet. She said, “I have locked it for you because the men use it.” When she first came up to me, and she still works at the same ground, I did not question it at the time because I was young, eager, starting out, and wanting to make an impression and not cause a fuss. The fact that it is still happening, I am starting to question it because I am older and more experienced. I would not expect to walk into the Telegraph and the men be using the women’s toilets, for example, so why should it happen in other areas of work such as media suites and things like that? That example is probably the tip of the iceberg. I am sure that, if you spoke to many women across the industry, especially those who cover solely very male-dominated sports such as F1 or the Premier League, they would have much worse stories to tell. It is just an example of how woefully women are underrepresented in sports print journalism.

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: We touched on it earlier but, when thinking about the gender diversity of sports press rooms, we have got to a positive place, but we need to make sure that we do not risk othering women’s sport and women who work in sport. We have created a platform for a lot more women to work in the industry by crafting more roles for women’s sport coverage. For example, if I go to a women’s game, the vast majority in the press box will be women. It will be a very white press box, and that is a huge challenge for the media industry moving forward. It is probably 60:40 women, maybe even more, which is great, but there is a risk of continuing to other that so more women get pushed into it. More women go into those opportunities, and that makes the men’s side of sport even more male-heavy. That would be one to consider: making sure that there is not a lack of opportunity for women because they continue to be pushed elsewhere.

Q173 Christine Jardine: I used to be a journalist and, as part of my career, I did sport at a time when there were almost no women, and there was no coverage of women’s sports, full stop, other than golf and curling. I wondered from what you are saying about women being pushed into covering women’s sports, at the time when women started to break through there was a kind of “We have to do this” attitude in broadcasting. Does that still persist? Is there still a resistance to the sort of developments we want to see, with women having access to all areas in their careers and being able to progress? Do you find there is still an underlying almost resentment sometimes?

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: Do you mean resentment among women who only—

Christine Jardine: Not among the women, but towards the women who are coming through?

Natalie Pirks: I have seen examples across the broadcast industry of, “We have one, so we do not need any more because we have ticked that box.” I genuinely feel, particularly in newsrooms that I have worked in, there is a real desire to bring more women through and to give them opportunities. I still hear too often of WhatsApp groups that the men are in, or the golf days they take to have conversations that women are just naturally excluded from, decisions that are being made by very senior men that affect women’s careers. As a slightly older broadcaster now, we could all probably sit here and name the women who are prominent in sports broadcasting and are 50 or over. We could do it on one hand. I feel that that is a problem because, while the pathways need to be good, there needs to be a world where you do not reach 45 and feel like your career is over. I do not think that has got much better. In the digital world that we are now living in, and with that push for new talent coming through, it is almost like one in, one out. We probably need to be much better as an industry at looking after women who reach a certain age and want to stay. A lot of friends that I have, and we talk about this a lot, are going freelance or becoming self-employed, like I have just recently done, because they want a better work-life balance, they want to be with their children, but they also feel their career has reached a natural stop and they cannot really see what that next bit is. We are not particularly good as an industry at doing that either.

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: To follow up on what Natalie said, that was very much at the forefront when I started my media business two years ago. I wanted to create more coverage opportunities for women’s football, but I also realised, as a woman working in broadcasting, you age out very quickly. There is always a younger, fresher, more interesting person behind you. It is the same for much of the media industry, TV and film. That is the way of the visual landscape. I was very conscious of how I pivot ahead of time, because the reality is that very few people in broadcasting are going to be working even in their mid-40s. That is a pretty scary reality when most of us are going to have to work until our 70s. It is a challenge for that more public-facing broadcast side for sure.

Natalie Pirks: If we have worked really hard as an industry to bring these women through, train them and get them to a point where they are actually very good at their job and then watch them walk out the door, what was it for? What were all those years for if we are letting women walk away?

Fiona Tomas: Natalie will be able to speak more on this point, but I have certainly witnessed from older colleagues in the industry how hard it is to get back into the game once you have kids as a woman. I have seen women editors have to rush out of a news conference because they have to do the school run, and feeling really guilty about it. As a young woman looking up to these women, it makes me wonder what it is going to look like for me in five or 10 years’ time. There is a lot of travel involved in doing this job; you are required to cover events, sometimes many miles away from home, and have extended periods away. We know that, as a society, most of the caregiving roles still fall on women’s shoulders. It is just not the same for men in the industry who become fathers. They take their paternity leave, and then they just waltz back in. I am not saying it is not challenging for them, but I do not think it is the same for women.

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: I had a friend who recently went up for a role and there was an assumption that, because she is a parent, she would not be up for it because of the travel. Even then there is an assumption about your limitations before you get started.

Q174 Richard Quigley: Something struck me in what you said, Natalie, about the usual misogyny. That sent a bit of a chill. As politicians, we get lots of abuse, but I know Nadia will get more abuse because she is a woman. It is just not acceptable anywhere in society to say “usual misogyny”, is it? Something you did say, which my wife and children will be cheering at home, is that men are difficult and get in the way. I can only agree with that, and they will be really glad you pointed it out.

On employment conditions in your industry, do you think the provision, or lack thereof, of things such as job security, flexibility, maternity pay—all the things you expect in a normal job—is preventing women from entering?

Natalie Pirks: I was hugely fortunate in that, when I had my two daughters, I was working at ITV/ITN and the BBC, both of whom looked after me very well. Both gave me the exact maternity leave I wanted and supported me. I went straight back into my role and had no issues. If I was about to embark on my journey as a mother now, I do not know—again not about those individual newsrooms but industry-wide—that I would feel as comfortable or as confident. If I was freelance, I would be incredibly worried. Fiona is absolutely spot on. I actually planned both my pregnancies around the Olympics, which is how ridiculous my life was.

Richard Quigley: Well done. That is quite an achievement.

Natalie Pirks: Someone told me it was really bad timing, and I actually said, “It is a feat of human engineering, actually.”

Richard Quigley: I bet it was a bloke who said that.

Natalie Pirks: It was a man, funnily enough. With my first, I took only six months because I needed to be back for London; you cannot miss the London Olympics. It was not enough, and it broke my heart. The second time I took nine and a half months, and the first thing I did was fly to Rio and present the 1 o’clock news for 15 minutes with no autocue and no monitor. I was so scared, and someone remarked to me afterwards, “You should probably smile more.” [Laughter.]

Richard Quigley: That is not funny. It was a laugh of desperation.

Natalie Pirks: They are a dear friend of mine, but again it was a man. It was a different time, but my point is that, when you talk about the usual misogyny, it is so much water off a duck’s back now that it is something we just deal with. I did a story about sexism in sport where this very prominent woman in her sport was being chanted at regularly. Every time she went to a football match, she was getting awful sexist abuse. When I had this conversation internally, a woman said to me, “Well, I go to football every week and you just hear that all the time. What is the issue?” We are so used to it that it has become a bit normalised.

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: There is a certain resilience that you build up, so you are probably desensitised to certain things, normalising the misogyny and what you experience because it becomes so regular and common. As a freelancer and being self-employed, you do not have any of that security. That is something I think about a lot. If I were to experience long-term sickness, I have no security, no maternity leave. If I do not work, I do not make any money. The vast majority who work in sports and the media industry are freelancers.

Q175 Richard Quigley: I am doing some work on the creative industries. What you find happening is that the people entering the creative industry are the ones whose parents can pay for them to do it, and there is a danger that you are not being rewarded for your specialism or ability to be a journalist; you are being rewarded because your parents can afford to pay. It is a bit of a concern that an industry is disappearing in that way.

Q176 Staying on self-employment and freelancing, is it harder for women than it would be for your male counterparts? You will know the answer to that; I am not trying to lead the question. If so, in what ways is it harder to stay in the industry if you are freelance and self-employed?

Flo Lloyd-Hughes: It is certainly harder when it comes to maternity, because you have to make those decisions. I have heard very similar stories to Natalie’s in terms of people who rush to get back to work because they are worried that, if they do not turn up, someone else will take their place. It is probably very similar to how a player feels when they get injured; if they do not come back, they are not going to get back in the squad or get their place on the pitch. That is certainly the reality of being self-employed. It is very different if you are a father or a mother, because it is that physical impact that you have as a parent.

As Fiona said, the woman is more often than not the main caregiver in the family. That is a big element of it. Some places are really supportive and others are not. Because there are so few jobs now, it increases that competition, uncertainty and insecurity about your place. Social media has added to that too because, as someone who is freelance, the comparison culture is always there because you are saying, “Oh that person is doing this, and this person is doing that. I need to make sure I say yes to this, otherwise I will not get it.” There is a constant cycle of things playing on your mind.

Q177 Richard Quigley: On that point, as we move towards digital media and the way we all consume news in whatever form, has that widened or narrowed the opportunity for women in sports journalism?

Fiona Tomas: It has widened opportunities for fans and those who consume women’s sport, because we know that the fandom in women’s sport is very different from fandom in men’s sport. A prime example being it is very niche and you do not read a lot about it in newspapers, because predominantly male desks will not commission it. It is very unlikely, because men have never played it, that they have ever been to a netball match or know much about the sport itself. I know for a fact that Sky has broadcast netball for the past two decades and has blown up its YouTube channel, which has the largest female audience across all the sports that it shows. It has noticed that women will not necessarily watch the sporting event live, but they will come back to it because they have other commitments, whether that is putting a kid to bed or whatever it might be. The boom in social and digital has definitely led to an uptick in the cut-through when it comes to women’s sport reaching more people. People are engaging with it more. The counter to that is that they are scrutinised more, they receive more abuse, more pile-ons, those sorts of things.

Chair: I will try to finish this session in about 15 minutes, so if Kim and Rosie could try to keep their sections within that, it would be great.

Q178 Kim Leadbeater: Thank you for giving such honest and open evidence. We could probably have pretty much the same conversation about women in politics, women in sport and women in broadcasting, and I think it is really fascinating. You have three areas there where we probably have a lot of shared experiences, shall we say? Diving into that a little further, how significant a factor would you say online abuse is in shaping women’s experience of your profession at the moment?

Natalie Pirks: If I was starting out and seeing the level of abuse that future me would get, it would not make me want to do it as a job. I cannot say I want my children to become camera-facing journalists, if I am honest, because it is not particularly nice.

When I started out, my role models were women like Clare Balding, Gabby Logan and, lastly, the brilliant Kelly Cates, obviously. They are all still brilliant at their jobs today, but we did not have social media then, so I could not see what anyone thought of Clare two minutes after she came off her Wimbledon broadcast because it did not exist. People coming into this industry now are living in a very different landscape from the one I came into. I came into one without too many women but with brilliant role models. I was very much on my own in press boxes and having to learn to rub along with men and get their help. I am forever grateful to the men in those press boxes who really helped me in my early career, but now, if I could go back and see the abuse that women get, I feel it would be a barrier. I am not sure I would want to do it as much as I did when I started out. How about you guys?

Fiona Tomas: I would probably say I encounter it less than the two women either side of me, and that is purely because I work in print journalism. For example, I have a major problem going into schools in Tottenham, in Brixton, wherever there are very diverse communities, and saying, “You are not going to be something, you are better off becoming a professional footballer or athlete before you get a chance to be a presenter.” That cannot be the situation.
