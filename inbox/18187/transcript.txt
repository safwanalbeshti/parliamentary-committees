Q1 Chair: Welcome, everyone, to this morning’s session of the Public Accounts Committee. Apologies from Sir Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, the Chair, who is away on parliamentary business. I am Clive Betts; as deputy Chair of the Committee, I am chairing it this morning.

We are going to look at upgrading the grid, which might sound like a very general and uninteresting subject but is quite important to everyone listening to this at home, as well as to everyone in this room today. The electricity transmission network, otherwise known as the grid, is a system of cables, substations, overhead lines and pylons that moves electricity around the country. It is really important to get to people’s homes and businesses and make them work. The Government are currently pursuing a major upgrade of the grid to make sure that we can deliver 95% clean electricity by 2030, which is going to mean £70 billion of investment between 2025 and 2031 to make sure that that investment takes place.

We are going to look at the challenges facing the Government today—DESNZ is on the panel, which I will introduce in a minute—and at whether there is a credible plan to do that on time and within budget and whether the benefits will be delivered to consumers of electricity. As well as DESNZ, we have Ofgem and the National Energy System Operator here today.

I will ask the witnesses to introduce themselves now and, when we come to each of the organisations, to say, in two sentences, what they do. Most people might have heard of the Department; they might have heard of Ofgem as the organisation that puts their bills up every so often; and they might not have heard of NESO at all. If you can, please try to put that in context, because lots of these acronyms go around and people often have no idea what they amount to.

Jonathan Brearley: I am Jonathan Brearley, permanent secretary of DESNZ, the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero. We set the policy for the energy system.

Emily Bourne: Good morning. I am Emily Bourne, director for electricity systems and networks at DESNZ.

Tim Jarvis: I am Tim Jarvis, chief executive of Ofgem. Ofgem regulates the gas and electricity markets. That includes the retail markets, the people we pay our bills to. In this context, we also regulate the networks, including the transmission network operators.

Beatrice Filkin: I am Beatrice Filkin. I am the director of major projects at Ofgem, which means that I am responsible for regulating all of the big transmission upgrades as well as other big projects that we are delivering, such as Sizewell.

Fintan Slye: I am Fintan Slye, chief executive of NESO, the National Energy System Operator. We are a relatively new public corporation, established in public ownership in 2024. We are responsible for strategic planning of the energy system over the long term. We also operate the electricity system through the control room in real time and provide advice to Government and Ofgem around the development of the energy system over the long term.

Q2 Chair: We will unpack some of those responsibilities as we go through to find out exactly who does what and how you relate to each other, which is one of the big challenges here.

Permanent Secretary, there is a big challenge, is there not? There are 88 upgrade projects deemed to be necessary by 2030 to keep the grid working in a way that enables all the new renewable projects to be connected to it. What needs to be done to make sure that that happens?

Jonathan Brearley: It is worth stepping back and looking, first of all, at why we are doing this. As the Secretary of State has said, we see the clean power goal as one that makes sure that we will get a cost-effective system, particularly in the context of the geopolitical changes that we are seeing.

If I were to lay the programme out as a whole, the first challenge has been to make sure that we unlock the investment for the companies to be able to build the grid. If you look at the reforms that we have made over the last five or six years, we have, through Ofgem’s work on regulation, achieved that, so the investment is flowing, and the companies get the money that they need to deliver what they need to do.

Going forward, this is, for me, all about our collective efforts to oversee this delivery programme. As the report says, within DESNZ we will be continuing to build on the work that we have done so far to make sure that we have oversight of the portfolio as a whole, which includes generation and other projects. Ofgem needs to make sure that it gives networks the right incentives to deliver, and NESO needs to make sure that it plays its role in co-ordinating and making sure that people can connect to the grid and make all the technical changes that need to be made.

There is a joint effort here, but if I had to summarise it in one broad term, it is the delivery arrangements that we are putting in place.

Q3 Chair: What happens if you do not deliver these projects by 2030?

Jonathan Brearley: We have to recognise that there are always going to be risks around a programme this big, some of which are within our control or that of the network companies, and some of which are outside it.

I should make it really clear that, in this system, the transmission companies are ultimately responsible for making sure that these are delivered on time. If they are not delivered, we have a co-ordination job and have to make sure, as best we can, that the generation and the network align and that we minimise any further consequences, which is complex. For example, if you look at some of the benefits that new generation will bring, we need to offset that against constraint costs that may appear, for example if the generation is earlier than some of the network, but that is the work that we need to do together.

Chair: That did not sound terribly reassuring.

Jonathan Brearley: In a sense, if these are not delivered on time, it is our job to manage the programme, to minimise costs for customers and to minimise risk to the system, which is what we will do.

We need to do it as quickly as we can, and delays have consequences, but our job is to make sure that we make that investment available, and then hold to account those responsible for building out the network in terms of time of delivery and of costs. Broadly, our role in this whole process is to really focus on the network upgrade and make sure that it is happening to time and to cost.

Q5 Chair: What does “hold to account” mean? You have no levers or powers over them, have you?

Tim Jarvis: We have quite significant powers over them in the sense that we approve projects and funding. We also have a role in real time in balancing the system on a day-to-day, minute-by-minute basis in the control room, dispatching the generation, and controlling the outages on the transmission system and flows over interconnectors and so on to make sure that the system is in balance at all times.

Jonathan Brearley: I will start, and Fintan and Tim might want to come in from their perspective. This is a major programme overall. Tim Jarvis: Just to step back a little, the challenge that we have collectively is that building out the transmission network takes a long time. The third issue, as you alluded to, is planning and consents. These are big projects and the Government have taken some steps there, and we are looking to see how that plays through.

We are trying to get these projects going as quickly as possible, and then holding the transmission companies to account for delivering, but it is challenging, for the reasons that I have set out.
