Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: David Hale, Dr Geoff Tily and Louise Hellem.

Q79 Chair: Welcome to the Treasury Select Committee on Tuesday 20 October 2026. Today we are continuing with our pre-Budget scrutiny sessions. I am delighted to welcome an expert set of witnesses to help us in preparing for next week’s Budget, but also for when the Chancellor of the Exchequer appears in front of us after the Budget. He will be appearing in front of us on 11 November.

I am delighted to welcome our witnesses. Dr Geoff Tily is the head of economics at the TUC, or the Trades Union Congress; Louise Hellem is the chief economist at the Confederation of British Industry, also known as the CBI; and David Hale is the Government affairs director at the Federation of Small Businesses, often referred to as the FSB. We try to spell out initials, so bear that in mind when we get into our alphabet soup of organisations.

To start, Dr Tily, how would you describe the outlook for businesses in the UK at the moment?

Dr Tily: We think that the economy has performed better than expected over this year, but there are still a number of challenges that we hope are dealt with in the Budget. You are asking about businesses, but the position on households is very important here. We have to keep in mind how hard hit households have been for nearly 20 years. We have an unprecedented living standards crisis.

As I am sure you know, real pay has barely moved since before the financial crisis. Household disposable incomes, which include benefits and taxes, have also not moved over the same period. There are plenty of figures out there showing the scale of hardship. A telling one, I think, is that 8 million households do not think that they could finance an unexpected bill for £850.

We have put out a Budget statement, which I think we have shared with the Committee. In that statement we also elaborate some additional costs that households have been facing since interest rates and prices have gone up. We find that mortgages in particular have gone up by an extremely large amount. They have gone up from roughly a monthly payment of £600 to a monthly payment of £900, which amounts to £3,600 in a year. Maybe I will not go through all the costs, but there are large costs on mortgage, on energy, on those who have loans, and on water.

In terms of the economy, we outline a causality from low incomes to a lack of consumer spending, where we see that the UK is performing particularly badly compared to other countries.

Q80 Chair: You are saying that that lack of consumer spending is having an impact on businesses, presumably.

Dr Tily: We are certainly saying that—and on the economy. There were figures the other day. The GDP figures showed that business-facing services were doing well, but consumer-facing services were still down on where they were ahead of the financial crisis. If you look over the period before the pandemic to the present, the UK has the slowest consumer spending growth out of all G7 economies. We think that this weak demand, in effect, is a critical factor in the weakness of the economy. You can do the calculations. At the moment, consumer demand is contributing about 0.7 percentage points to GDP. In between the financial crisis and the pandemic, it was contributing 1.5 percentage points. Before the financial crisis, it was contributing 2 percentage points a year.

Q81 Chair: Can I just bring you back to businesses? Most trade union members will be working for larger businesses, rather than the smaller ones. Are there any themes that you would draw out that affect those larger businesses that are employing trade union members?

Dr Tily: There are the same themes around demand and the use of AI. Another theme would be the Brexit deal, which has perhaps been less than ideal.

Q82 Chair: Louise Hellem, what is the take from the CBI?

Louise Hellem: When we speak to businesses across the economy, there is definitely a theme of resilience, but in quite a high-cost environment for firms. That is particularly around high energy costs, high employment costs and some of the policy costs around business rates and things like that as well.

We definitely see some sectors that are performing more strongly, particularly those firms in sectors such as energy, digital, defence and healthcare. As Geoff said, some of those more consumer-facing sectors are the ones that have the weakness. We have been pleasantly surprised by that resilience that we have seen across the economy, given the global and domestic headwinds that we have seen.

One reason for that, in many ways unfortunately, is that businesses are adept at crisis management now. They have developed strategies over the last few years, particularly when it comes to things such as high energy costs. We have a lot of firms that are now hedging a lot more of their energy prices and, where they are able to, having much higher stock inventories. That still has an impact on the business’s bottom line and is tying up working capital. While we are not seeing elevated levels of distress that are too high, we are seeing a lot of firms that are holding back on hiring and on investment decisions as well.

Q83 Chair: We will come to some of that a little later. Thank you very much for now.

David Hale: On the small business side, I do not think that it is any secret that confidence has been low. It has taken a hit in the last few years since coming out of the pandemic. Costs are very high, particularly fixed costs, where small businesses do not necessarily have available some of those strategies that Louise was just talking about on energy. On a personnel level, some of the labour cost issues get very difficult for small businesses because it is staff who you are close to where you see very increased costs and businesses having to put strategies in place to manage that.

In general, it is very difficult. That is not to say that businesses themselves are not optimistic. Quite often it helps to be optimistic to start and run a small business. I would not want to downplay it too much, but in general it is a low-confidence environment, a high-cost environment and even, for more businesses than not, a falling-revenue environment, which is particularly worrying.

Q84 Chair: Grant Thornton did a survey of businesses, which highlighted that the second most cited challenge they face, when asked what their biggest challenges are, is economic uncertainty. You have touched on it obliquely, but how would you interpret that for your sectors?

David Hale: Bluntly, if you were considering taking somebody on and having a cost that will sit with you for tens of thousands of pounds, you do not know what is going to happen with your energy costs. You might draw the conclusion from the last few years that there is more risk that they go up than that they go down. You do not know what is happening with other Government costs; there are worries around taxation. You do not know what is happening with the economy in general. The uncertainty makes it very difficult for people to place an investment when they know, if they place an investment, that they are stuck with paying the cost of it.

Q85 Chair: You mentioned energy prices as one of those things. Are there any things that Government have direct control of? While the Government are doing some things on energy prices, there are global shocks there. Are there specific things that you think the Government could do to help out on economic uncertainty? This year, for example, there have been no leaks around the Budget, whereas last year there was a lot of speculation and some information in the public domain, allegedly from leaks, although they never found anyone who had leaked it. Do you think that there is anything the Government could do to improve the certainty of the economic outlook?

David Hale: Yes, completely. What is really positive and potentially helpful with the new Government is the conception of having breathing space. The policies intended to create breathing space at the same time as tackling some of the long-term growth issues create an opportunity.

On breathing space, the thing that we have been asking for is an increase in small business rates relief. It has not increased since 2016. That was a long time ago in any terms, but particularly in terms of property costs and general inflation. A proper increase in that creates some immediate benefit cross-sectorally.

On energy specifically, the Government took steps with the renewables obligation for households, but not with businesses. Particularly at the small business end, small businesses tend to buy energy in a similar way to households. If the Government could do something on the renewables obligation, that would be useful on breathing space.

Going beyond that and talking about Government-imposed costs, by far the largest one that is commonly referred to would be what has happened on national insurance. We would worry around mitigations that are being put in place for small businesses not keeping pace with what is happening to wages. The employment allowance did not go up last year. If it does not go up this year but we see a general increase in wages, including regulated wages, that would be a negative.

Q86 Chair: There are things you think the Government could be doing right now to help.

David Hale: There are plenty of things.

Louise Hellem: I would echo some of that from David. There is a mix of geopolitical and domestic factors that have featured in that uncertain environment. Addressing some of the high costs that we are seeing, particularly energy costs, would be important. In particular, we would like to see policy costs move into general taxation, rather than being on businesses’ bills. We are also calling for follow-through and delivery on the big strategies that the Government have set out, such as the industrial strategy and the 10-year infrastructure plan, to show that sense of continuity and commitment.

Q87 Chair: We have 10-year plans being announced and things, but what is it you specifically need from the Government for business confidence to grow?

Louise Hellem: It is the follow-through on some of those.

Q88 Chair: When you say follow-through, what would you want to see the Government deliver, let us say, in the next two or three years, assuming that that is roughly the length of the Parliament?

Louise Hellem: Across the industrial strategy, you have those sector plans for each of them, but there are some commonalities. Some of the big ones are this point around energy and grid connections. We have seen some really great progress already on planning and infrastructure,, but we need to continue to see that and a lot more pace going into the system. We would like to see a lot more co-ordination across regulators and more accountability for them to look at not just the regulation in their own narrow area but how the whole system for that regulation fits together.

Q89 Chair: That is great. We will come to regulation in more detail. I will go back to David Hale, then Louise Hellem and then Dr Tily on the relationship with the EU. The Prime Minister has signalled that he wants to change that relationship. What would business want to see, and would that make a difference to business confidence?

David Hale: To pick up on the last point Louise made around deliverables and specifics, in the short term, getting a meaningful SPS agreement across the line is what we want to see. There is a danger of getting distracted from what can be delivered now and what would be meaningful and positive—I would point to that SPS agreement—and getting too distracted about the long term. If I was to focus on anything in relation to the EU, it would be that.

Q90 Chair: The small things matter right now. That is what you want to see.

David Hale: The small, immediate things, yes.

Louise Hellem: Businesses are very supportive of having a closer relationship with the EU. As David said, it is around tackling some of those current issues, so SPS, but also the emissions trading scheme. I think they are very supportive about thinking about some kind of youth mobility scheme as well.

A big concern that businesses have at the moment in relation to Europe, though, is around the “Made in Europe” scheme and making sure that the UK has access to that. That is beneficial not only for UK firms but for European firms as well, given the integration of supply chains that we have had. That is really top of mind at the moment.

Q91 Chair: Dr Tily, where does the TUC stand on the relationship with the European Union and the Prime Minister’s recent announcements?

Dr Tily: We are in favour of things to foster a closer relationship and better trading relationships. Could I just go back to the previous point? I wanted to agree about energy costs. We all know that energy costs are extremely difficult for British businesses. We agree with what my colleagues have said about business support, extending the British industrial manufacturing scheme to cover the whole of the manufacturing sector and generally moving costs into general taxation and away from energy costs.

We have our own proposals on a social tariff. I do not know whether you want to get into that now, but other support for living standards is important. Any support that not only can help households but will help to increase the spending on the high street and wherever is what we think is useful.

Q92 Chair: We all talk about energy costs now because particularly the war in Iran and the ongoing war in Ukraine have caused a big shock on energy prices, but these are long-term structural changes you seem to be highlighting there, Dr Tily and Louise Hellem. Am I right that you want to change things so that businesses should not be paying so much for their energy costs, but more should fall on the general taxpayer? You both said that. I want to be clear for the record. Do you have an idea of what that would cost?

Louise Hellem: From the work that we have done, if you move the renewables obligation and FIT into general taxation, we think it would be about £5 billion a year for the next five years. We have done some analysis that shows that, if you offset that through indirect taxation, you can have a positive impact on the Exchequer by £7.2 billion at the end of that period, because of the investment that you have brought forward.

Q93 Chair: To be clear, it would cost the Exchequer, or the British taxpayer, £5 billion a year so that businesses did not carry the cost, but over that five years there would be a £7.2 billion dividend.

Louise Hellem: Yes, but it assumes that you are raising that tax elsewhere within the system, so through indirect taxes. It is because it is a much more efficient way to raise taxation, rather than the economic impact of putting these costs on to energy bills directly.

Q94 Chair: Dr Tily, do you have anything to add to that?

Dr Tily: We support that scheme. The estimates of costs we had were from Make UK, which were a little lower. I guess we would also emphasise the fact that, if energy costs were successfully reduced, there should be some benefits downstream in terms of growth and so on.

Louise Hellem: I expect that the difference in the figures is because our scheme is for all sectors, rather than just for manufacturing. A big advantage of the scheme is about increasing demand. We need to increase the demand for electricity to help with the roll-out of electrification. That will also bring systems costs down if we are spreading it over a greater demand.

Chair: Colleagues will pick up on some of these points.

Q95 Ms Minns: I have some questions on regulatory frameworks. Before I ask those, Ms Hellem, you briefly touched there on grid connections, which for your members is a pretty challenging area. Has the CBI taken an initial view on Great British Grid?

Louise Hellem: We are positive where it is part of the broader system. From our perspective, it is not necessarily competition that is the issue, but actually it is about connecting—forgive the pun—together around where that demand and supply is needed within the system and, like I say, bringing a lot more pace to decisions, which is more about capacity of decision makers.

Q96 Ms Minns: Thank you. Since you mentioned it, I just wanted to quickly ask.

Let me ask you about regulatory frameworks. I have worked for nearly 20 years in regulated industries. I have sat on the other side of the table, sometimes arguing for, sometimes arguing against. Can I ask a question to you, Ms Hellem and Mr Hale, about which regulatory frameworks present the biggest friction points for business expansion?

Louise Hellem: Our general view on the regulatory system is around the overlapping regulations and requirements, and the duplication that we see across the system. Quite often they might be in the minutiae and the detail.

An area that I would point to as an example of that is the regulation around plastic packaging at the moment. We have overlapping regulations around the extended producer responsibility, plastic packaging tax and the packaging recovery note system. We need to clear out that system. What is it that we are trying to achieve? Having regulation that is just one system for businesses to engage with would be a much better way, we think, to get the same outcome, but for much less cost and complexity.

Q97 Ms Minns: That is quite a specific one. Mr Hale, would you like to comment on regulatory frameworks?

David Hale: There are two things. First, we currently are engaged with Government on a joint piece of work as a regulatory taskforce that should report soon, which we hope will address some of those issues, particularly around those smaller things that are duplicative. One that I would be really keen to draw to the Committee’s attention is that we find that, when Government look at regulatory reduction, HMRC sits exempt from that. This is purely on the administrative side. We do not think that that is right. HMRC is frequently one of the top two or three—but often the top—administrative barriers that firms, particularly new, young and small firms, report as being very difficult, and unnecessarily so. We would point to that. There are a number of different issues where we do not feel that Companies House has acted in a way that benefits the practical interests of small businesses that are using its systems.

Q98 Ms Minns: Can you explain a little more about that?

David Hale: There are new verification steps that have been brought in. I do not think that they have been particularly well designed with small businesses in mind. It used to be that Companies House was never really mentioned when regulation came up. It has traditionally been a strong performer until the last year or two. I guess that this is slightly more of an engagement point. As I say, we are engaged in a regulatory taskforce, so I hope that this is a point of the past and we can deal with it soon.

Q99 Dame Harriett Baldwin: May I ask Mr Hale another question? Small businesses are such a backbone of the UK economy and they create so many of the jobs for people as well. We are asking you for your evidence in the run-up to the 2026 Budget. I wondered whether you could cast your mind back to the 2024 and 2025 Budgets and highlight for the Committee which things in those Budgets have been helpful and which have been a challenge for small businesses.

Chair: We are talking about the future Budget. This is what today’s session is mainly about, so perhaps you could relate it to that and what might be coming up next week.

Dame Harriett Baldwin: What lessons can we learn from those Budgets that would help inform the advice you would like to give to the Chancellor ahead of the 2026 Budget?

David Hale: In many ways, it is difficult to get away from the 2024 Budget. The issue I mentioned that was not necessarily present in that was small business rates relief, but the Chancellor, in that Budget, gave herself room to make more changes to the system. I would hope that the new Chancellor would make use of that and increase small business rates relief. We have talked about the renewables obligation.

In terms of national insurance, if I was to be critical, I think the 2024 Budget was designed on a very optimistic view of where the labour market was. That has not been borne out. It is fair to say that it has done a lot of damage on the employment side, including in terms of how that is probably now starting to feed through to households but, again, at that Budget there were steps taken on the employment allowance to protect small businesses at the lower end. It being two years ago, and looking to the future, that has not kept pace with what is happening with wages, so I would look at that again.

If we look at 2024, that was where some of the regulatory labour market reforms were coming in in terms of day-one sick pay. That is quite a well-worn conversation, but there is potential to take measures such as a statutory sick pay rebate that would help ease the impact on firms and the workers that would be most impacted by those changes.

Not to go on, but the one that I would particularly draw attention to that I think has damaged small business intentions is the changes that were made to entrepreneurs or business asset disposal relief. That has had an impact, and perhaps a less well-discussed impact than the other changes on national insurance. We had the increase to 14% the April before last, and now the increase to 18%. We would be keen for the Chancellor to ease that at least to the 14% that we saw the April before last.

Q100 Dame Harriett Baldwin: I was waiting to hear the things that you thought had been positive for small businesses. Otherwise, I think your asks are all to try to mitigate some of the challenges that previous Budgets have delivered to your members.

David Hale: There is an extent of unwinding challenges that have come through Budgets. I would point to the employment allowance, which was a positive that was delivered, itself as a mitigation, but that is a positive that is delivered. All we are really keen to do is to make sure that that maintains its real value relative to wages.

Q101 Dame Harriett Baldwin: On the HMRC point you were making to my colleague, is Making Tax Digital something that you are expecting the Chancellor to make a change to in this Budget?

David Hale: I am not sure about expectations to do with this Budget. The Making Tax Digital process is an example of where we would prefer that HMRC was subject to the same regulatory ambition that the rest of Government is, at least, if not more, given where it sits. I would not say what I was expecting or otherwise in terms of MTD. Some things that are HMRC-driven that I am particularly worried about in the Budget next week are the deemed reseller rules to do with VAT, which I do not know whether we are intending to get on to. Those sorts of things would concern me at the Budget next week, primarily.

Q102 Ms Minns: Ms Hellem and Mr Hale, I want to go back to something you were talking about earlier when you expressed a view that perhaps a focus on delivery and a little less tinkering might be beneficial. Can you expand on that? What would you like to see the Government do in terms of stopping with the tinkering and delivering a period of stability in terms of policy?

Louise Hellem: Some of it, like I say, relates to those areas I mentioned before—we now have the industrial strategy and the infrastructure strategy—in terms of enabling that to really bed in and seeing the systems on the ground. Particularly when you mentioned tinkering, the tax system comes to mind within that. One that we have touched on very briefly, but not in that much detail, is around business rates. That is a system that comes up time and time again with businesses in terms of the overall cost. It is also one where we have been promised a fundamental reform.

At the moment, we seem to go from one fiscal event to another with a different part of the system being tinkered with. We would like to see more long-term reform on that, particularly to smooth a lot of the cliff edges there are around the system, particularly around the £51,000 and £500,000 thresholds.

Also, it is a system where the multipliers are not fixed within the system. You have a fiscal neutrality clause within that. It means that businesses do not know what their business rates bill is going to be year to year. We have had instances over the last couple of years, particularly for some sectors, where they have seen 100% or more increases in their business rates bill, because of either policy decisions or the decisions by the VOA. That has led to cancelling investments worth millions of pounds because they have these tax bills landed on their door. We would like to see a fundamental reform of that system, rather than potentially another merry-go-round of the tinkering this time around.

Q103 Ms Minns: So you want fundamental reform. And Mr Hale?

David Hale: I would probably have a somewhat different take from the small business side. It is more an action rather than a long-term ambition or postponement of decision point from our perspective, which is why I would say on business rates that we have the system as it is. We have an element of the system that has not kept pace, in terms of small business rates relief. Let us get on and do that. That is an easy, actionable change. It can bring the breathing space that the Government have talked about, which is helpful.

On the longer-term challenges side, we talked about national insurance and sick pay. We have challenges around the edge of the labour market, particularly related to health. At the moment, you have a situation where there are some firms—large and small—that are taking on people who are likely to have time off sick. They are structurally disadvantaged by the way we organise our labour market rules. Putting in place things such as statutory sick pay and helping the small firms that do it, in particular through the employment allowance, would be helpful.

Q104 Ms Minns: On this point about stability, we often think about it in terms of new things that the Government want business to pick up. There are a number of things—you have already highlighted a couple today—that you disagree with that Governments have done in recent years. Does your desire for stability extend to saying, “Can you just not even reverse those things that you have put in, because we would like the certainty of knowing what we are dealing with for the next two or three years?”

Louise Hellem: From our perspective, quite often when we call for stability it is about that predictability. Are the messages that the Government are giving out consistent with the policy that they are talking about? One of the big hits around national insurance was not only the cost to businesses, but the fact that the Government were saying at the time, “We want to increase growth. We want to help get more people into work.” Then we have a policy that makes it a lot harder for businesses to employ people. That is the same with some of the policies around the Employment Rights Act as well.

Businesses would like to see that pathway to reform that is in keeping with the ambitions that the Government are setting out. I do not think that that means that you cannot change anything, but it is about how you can change systems in those conversations with businesses as well, and to understand some of these issues and barriers. A lot of it then is around the simplification, rather than necessarily rates and things like that.

David Hale: What really worries me is when Government give the impression that they confuse businesses’ desire for stability with inaction on things that would help businesses. There are a number of Government policies that, in effect, ratchet up the negative risks that we have seen develop. For example, when general energy prices rise, so does what the Government are taxing. We have all seen what has happened with property prices, but we still have a small business rates system set in a 2016 framework. We know that wages have increased, but the tax measures that are there to make sure that small businesses can continue to afford them have not. We can even talk about the secondary threshold that went up. That had not gone up previously in line with wages, as it normally should have done.

At the moment, we have too many areas of Government policy that enhance instability, because they tend to ratchet with it, and not enough small changes at the tiller to make sure that there is the stability that businesses need. From the small business side, it comes back to, “I want to invest £30,000 or £40,000 in a new member of staff. Are various things, including Government, going to disrupt that so suddenly I cannot afford my mortgage?”

Q105 Ms Minns: Give me your top one that you would like a change on.

David Hale: As the immediate way to get good breathing space, I think we would point to small business rates relief, but there are others, particularly on national insurance.

Dr Tily: I wanted to make a general remark on this stability point, in contrast with the need for action. Dame Harriett was talking about the 2024 Budget. I would like to put in a good word for the 2024 Budget, when the Government made a decent effort at supporting Government consumption and Government investment. We know that public service spending has been very starved for a long time, and public service spending supports the economy from both a demand side and a supply side.

Q106 Chair: Was it the capital spending or the—

Dr Tily: It was both. I cannot remember; it was about 50/50 between investment spending and current spending. They were both needed. At the time, the decision was made to use national insurance, and I think even the IMF at the time said that that was a reasonable approach. Maybe the Government could have gone further on different taxes than national insurance, but overall it was a sensible package and a needed package.

There is still spending needed. A previous question was answered about difficulties with HMRC. I imagine that there have been HMRC staff cuts and resources have been quite badly starved. There is a need for public spending. There is a need for action on some of these things as well.

Q107 John Glen: I want to focus in on some of the measures that you have said your organisations, respectively, wanted, and then to look at the issue of the labour market. Mr Hale, the FSB has asked for an increase in the VAT threshold to £100,000 in your Budget submission. It is currently £90,000 and one of the highest among our peers. What effect would it have and how much would it cost?

David Hale: The good thing about increasing the VAT threshold is that the last time it was increased, which was very welcome, it was positive by the fifth year of the forecast.

Q108 John Glen: What were the costs in years 1 to 4 previously?

David Hale: I would have to check exactly, but in the low hundreds of millions. The Treasury, understandably, is concerned about the fifth year. That is what I would point to from the point of view of long-term growth. What pushing the threshold higher achieves is simply that you are not getting businesses that get to a point where they say, “This is too much of a headache”—there are some price effects as well—“I am just not going to work for part of the year.” Particularly now, where we see a lot of businesses that scale very quickly in terms of revenue at the smaller end, particularly with online, we are seeing people go into the VAT system who are not equipped to properly file.

Q109 John Glen: I understand the principle. You will have had a lot of engagement, I assume, with Treasury officials and you will have discussed these matters. How likely do you think it is that the Treasury is going to raise it to £100,000, or raise it anyway?

David Hale: I do not know on this occasion. There has been a welcome change in the last few years in terms of how the VAT threshold has been viewed. There is more of a general appreciation, in part because of who is bumping up against it and how that has changed, with younger people—

Q110 John Glen: It has not changed yet.

David Hale: No, but what has changed in terms of the types of businesses that are bumping up against the VAT threshold. Sorry, I was not clear.

Q111 Chair: Can you give us some examples?

David Hale: Selling online through social media and through different things means that businesses can potentially scale quite quickly from quite a low base. Traditionally, we would always ask—which is again relevant to the £100,000—for the VAT threshold to be set above the level of what one person working on their own, without staff, with a reasonable cost base, but not a particularly large one, would be able to afford. That is what we are aiming for. If £100,000 achieves that—I think it depends on what trade somebody is working for—that is what we would like to see.

Q112 Bobby Dean: I wanted to chip in on this quickly. To a lot of people’s minds, it just moves where that pinch point is. There is a different scaling challenge, but now it is at £100,000 instead of £80,000. Would the more radical thing not be to lower the threshold? Would that not get rid of a lot of the lumping problems? Maybe lower it down to, say, the median wage in the UK or something, around £30,000. Basically everybody is brought in. The price competitiveness equation changes. The threshold issue you are talking about changes. What would businesses’ reactions to that be?

David Hale: It is the structure of trading. There are far more people who have around £30,000 of independent income than have around £95,000, so you will always have a bumping effect. If you set it at zero you would have a huge one, because it would be a barrier to entering business altogether. If you set it at the median wage, it would be a barrier in practice to anyone who was like, “You know what, maybe my employed job is not working out and here is this self-employed route that I could take.” There are far fewer people who are obtaining £100,000 of revenue. If you set it at £500,000 revenue, there would be no bumping problem because there are so few people who are bumping into it as that sole trader. It is the structure that speaks to that.

Q113 John Glen: Ms Hellem, it is widely anticipated that the one tax that is going up for business is tax on banks. They represent 10% to 12% of the economy. They also fund businesses with loans, interest rates and so on. Have you done any work on the effect that this would have on the economy?

Louise Hellem: We have not done any quantitative work. This is something that has been rumoured, but it has come up as a big concern for our FS members. Also, there is a realisation that it would have an impact on all sectors, in particular on the availability of finance. It goes back to the point that I mentioned earlier around the rhetoric and objectives from Government, and then the policies that they are looking at. This week or last week, the Chancellor described the FS sector as the jewel in the UK’s crown. To do that and then, in the same breath, increase banking taxes—

Q114 John Glen: The CBI would be against an increase in bank surcharge?

Louise Hellem: We would not support an increase at all, no. That is quite negative for the UK economy.

Q150 Chris Coghlan: Is there a risk of persistent skill shortages where apprenticeships fail to develop as fast as AI? How do we adapt the workforce to cope with the pace of change?

Dr Tily: Again, the learning networks are in place and they can be used. We were wondering whether AI might come up. At the moment, we judge that the headline movements in the labour market are still dictated by mainly cyclical factors, but there are areas where AI is coming in. Certainly, we have a list of areas where AI might be affecting hiring. The approach has to be proactive. In part, it has to depend on training for skills, but it also has to depend on how AI is more broadly adopted across the economy.

There is a fear that AI is used only to save on costs, but it really should be used to help expand and to enable people to do fewer bad jobs and more good jobs. It should not be a zero-sum situation.

Q151 Chris Coghlan: On that, there is quite a lot of reporting on how AI makes it very hard for entry-level workers because those jobs are the first ones to get replaced, whereas middle managers are safer because they have seniority and judgment. They are almost directing AI instead of entry-level workers. Is there a case that you could use AI, in effect, to upskill entry-level workers so that they could operate as middle managers?

Dr Tily: How workers use AI and digital has to be a key part of skills training. There is probably an argument that AI should be a sector in its own right in the industrial strategy. There is massive uncertainty in this area, is there not? We do not know whether it is going to improve productivity. There are all these worries, as we heard from the IMF and the OECD recently, that it could be quite perilous for the economy as a whole. It is a massive issue.

Q152 Chris Coghlan: Finally, to the whole panel, I am just interested in generally hearing about your hopes and fears about the industrialisation strategy, if anyone wants to go first.

Louise Hellem: I am happy to go first. One of the big things for us—it may be a bit of a theme of our conversation today—is that you cannot have a proper reindustrialisation strategy in the UK without addressing high energy costs. It is definitely our hope, if not for this Budget, that very soon we are going to see more substantive action from Government on that. One of our immediate asks is to remove policy costs from bills, but we also want to engage with Government and think about this end-to-end approach to improve grid connectivity and make sure that we have a system that is really working together. That is both about the benefits for reindustrialisation and increasing the UK’s resilience. That would be one of the big areas for us.

David Hale: Following on from that, it needs to have some grit. It cannot be, or I would hope it does not end up being, one or two primes making announcements that sound good, but not tackling the real issues that apply across the industrial base. Energy is the one of the moment, but it is one of a few. That would be good.

Briefly, if I may, I will mention labour costs. This might be why we have had some disagreements on some aspects of policy. We really see this as a structural issue for people who are outside or in danger of being kept outside the labour force. There are real reasons why that group has found it harder to enter work over the last 20 years, and that is perhaps being accelerated. I am an optimist about AI, but what slightly worries me is that those entry-level roles where you could easily foresee AI having an impact are becoming harder to get into.

For all the focus on individual jobseekers, either you need to make your own job and be self-employed or you need somebody to give you a job. It is harder to do that from outside the labour market than it used to be. We need to look at that, if we are not to see a constraint on growth from having a smaller potential workforce.

Dr Tily: As we discussed earlier, we support measures to reduce energy costs for businesses. More broadly, we support bold action. We support putting money in there. I should also mention that it is not just about the industrial strategy; it is about living standards. We have a measure to reduce business costs and we have a proposal for a social tariff for households, which we think is very important. We think that strengthening living standards is an important part of strengthening the economy because of the shortfall in demand caused by high interest rates and so on.

I should reiterate that the Employment Rights Act is vital to action in both the short term and the long term. All the evidence these days points to the fact that what is good for workers is good for the economy.

Q153 Chair: I just want to come back on energy costs again, particularly to you, Ms Hellem. You have framed the cost of electricity as an essential competitive issue for UK businesses. Can you just crystallise what Government or the sector could do to smooth that and what the particular cliff edges are between the UK and other countries?

Louise Hellem: Industrial energy prices are 36% above the EU average and 47% above the G7 average. One of the reasons why that difference is so large is that at the moment we have the renewables obligation, the feed-in tariff and, indeed, the climate change levy all on business bills. We would like to see those all moved into general taxation. As we talked about earlier, that would come with an up-front cost.

Q154 Chair: It is those Government levies that are causing the difference.

Louise Hellem: That is the big immediate action that we would like the Government to take. There are some longer-term actions, like you say, around making sure we can have a greater roll-out of electrification through grid improvements. That is action that we need to see now but would take longer to come about.

Q155 Chair: On electrification, what incentive do Government need to create for businesses to electrify? It is not all down to Government. In the end, businesses will have to make those judgments about their own premises and business operations. What are the barriers to businesses?

Louise Hellem: One barrier at the moment is these policy costs. If you are looking at the cost of electricity versus gas, that is one thing that makes that higher. You need to reduce that spark gap. You then need to make sure that businesses have the opportunity to electrify because they have the attachment to the grid. We do not always have the right conversations. The grid says that there is not demand in this area, but businesses do not come forward with the demand if they do not think the supply is going to be there.

Q156 Chair: The last Government had a target of decarbonising the grid by 2035. The current Government had a manifesto commitment to do that by 2030. That is decarbonisation, let alone all the connectivity and other things. Do you have any sense of the timescale for, one, when this needs to be done and, two, when it could be done, given these very ambitious targets to do work on the grid?

Louise Hellem: It is right to have the ambition. We see that we are probably moving a little bit from 2030 to 2035, at least in those last few percentages. That is probably a bit more of a practical timeline.

Q157 Chair: You think only the last few percentages will be dealt with between 2030 and 2035.

Louise Hellem: It is better to get it right. There are additional costs to trying to move it forward on an accelerated timeline.

Q158 John Glen: Could we address again the issue of tax on business? Ms Hellem, the CBI has done work that shows that in this tax year, finishing at the beginning of April 2026, businesses paid £344.8 billion, 31% of all tax revenues, yet nearly £15.5 billion is spent complying with tax rules. What specific measures would you and your members like to see in the Budget to simplify the tax system?

Louise Hellem: On simplification, there are the measures that we have already talked about in terms of business rates. That would be one of the big areas, particularly around removing cliff edges in the system.

Some of the other areas that we would like to see are perhaps on the smaller end, but, for example, we still have a lot of issues from businesses, particularly on the SME end, around IR35. There are measures that you can put in place there to create safe harbours for those who are certified to be self-employed.

It would be very helpful to have advanced assurance for R&D. I would also echo the comments about the interactions with HMRC. Again, you can improve systems by having better digital tracking and things like that from inquiries that come into the system.

Q159 John Glen: Could you tell us a little bit more about that specific thing about HMRC interactions? Can you make real for the Committee what the problem is in terms of customer interaction?

Louise Hellem: There is a variety of things. You mentioned the £15 billion in terms of compliance costs. Some of these are very practical things. Within the CT regime and the compliance frameworks, there are something like 985 boxes. The regime itself can be simplified. There is an ongoing discussion at the moment around how the domestic CT regime co‑exists with the international pillar 2 regime. Again, there is a lot of duplication in the system that you can take out.

There are also some very simple issues. If your firm is in contact with HMRC, you might be talking to a different person every time you make contact. You might get a slightly different answer and you might be sent on a different loop.

John Glen: You need continuity of interface.

Louise Hellem: Yes, there could be better continuity of service. That is supposed to happen, but does not always happen. It would be good to have better digital tracking of inquiries as well.

Q160 John Glen: On business rates, I assume you want to move from some sort of slab to a slice approach.

Louise Hellem: Yes.

Q161 John Glen: Presumably, there is also a problem with businesses that are on the high street but also have an online presence. There is hybridity. Politicians often say, “We want to get at these online behemoths that do not pay enough tax,” but businesses often do both. Have you thought about that problem? How would an appropriate change affect it?

Louise Hellem: Our proposal—you called it a slab-to-slice system—is about improving the system for everybody. It is not going down the road of having different rates for different sectors or types of businesses. Indeed, we think it is quite important to make sure that you are not creating more complexities and cliff edges within the system. As you say, people might think about the shop on the high street, but there is always a warehouse at some point along the chain. We think it is much better to improve the system.

To the point about cliff edges, like I say, there are these cliff edges around £51,000 and £500,000 for property values. If you go over that by £1, you will have a much greater bill. That can catch businesses unawares or mean they have a disincentive to expand, which we think is holding back growth and investment.

Q162 John Glen: Mr Hale, there has been a significant proposal from celebrity chefs to have a 10% VAT rate for hospitality businesses. What do you think about that?

David Hale: I would not expect to see one next week at the Budget. As you will well know, in Treasury terms it is an extremely expensive policy. Would it help hospitality? We presume that it would, given the extent. When it comes to expectations, I am not expecting to see one, as disappointing as that will be for people in the sector.

Q163 John Glen: What would your top ask for the hospitality sector be? UKHospitality has been very powerful in its advocacy and criticism over the fusion of above-inflation increases in the national living wage combined with the Employment Rights Act, contrary to what Dr Tily said, and the overall operating environment on the high street. What would be the best alternative, if we are not going to have that?

David Hale: I will point out a few things, if I may. This goes back to how Government can negatively interact with the ratcheting costs that businesses are seeing.

Hospitality is a big user of energy. The renewables obligation point stands for hospitality as much, if not more, in general, as it does for other sectors, so I would include that. Small business rates relief is really important for the hospitality businesses that it will help.

On the national living wage, in particular, which is increasing, the national insurance tax take has increased particularly on that. Sometimes it is a distraction to talk about the wage when we could be talking about the tax take coming down. That is what we would point to in terms of where you can bear down on national insurance costs. The proposals on sick pay are particularly tricky in an industry that in general employs people who are more likely to have days off sick. Again, I would point to the statutory sick pay rebates.

I would hope that all of the things that I have been talking about will make a difference to hospitality, largely on fixed and staffing costs, which is where I would be more hopeful next week than on headline VAT reductions, which I am just not expecting to see.

Q164 John Glen: I am not really clear. If they are not going to reduce the national insurance employer contributions, the national living wage will go up, there will not be any changes to employment rights and sick pay will come in very early, where is the benefit going to come for hospitality?

David Hale: My strong view is that the employment allowance should be going up in line with the national living wage every year. That should be an indexed and predictable cost.

Q165 John Glen: The indexation will take less out as tax.

David Hale: Yes. On the statutory sick pay rebate, I would strongly urge the Government to introduce that. Whether or not they do, we will have to see.

Chair: You are making your pitch.

Q166 John Glen: Dr Tily, could I come to you, please? The Centre for the Analysis of Taxation—CenTax—says that an equalisation of CGT with income tax would, even with indexation and so on raise £14 billion a year. Are you disappointed that repeated Treasury briefing over the last week suggests that they are not going to raise CGT, its scope or the rate? Would that be something you would have supported?

Dr Tily: It is something that we would have supported. We recognise those figures that you have mentioned. We note that it was relatively successful when CGT rates were raised by the previous Chancellor. It raised an impressive amount of revenue over the last years. It has gone up by 89%.

Q167 John Glen: Do you have any assessment of the behavioural effects, if you were to raise it to the prevailing income tax rates? That is the proposal.

Dr Tily: We do not have our own assessment of that, but we are aware of other people’s work. In particular, there is this concern that it will somehow diminish dynamism and entrepreneurism, but IPPR—the Institute for Public Policy Research—has put out analysis showing that there are better ways to support dynamism in the economy. In general, capital gains tax is not a barrier to entrepreneurship. We continue to support an increase in capital gains tax.

Again, you have to come back to this point about the difficult position that the economy is in, the support that is needed and the need for those who can best afford it to pay some more. That is the burden of many of our policies, in our submission.

Q168 John Glen: It looks like you are going to get the bank tax that you are in favour of. It does not look like you are going to get the CGT increase that you are also in favour of. Are there any other taxes that you would like to increase on business? This is your chance to pitch it.

Chair: I am sure the Chancellor is hanging on every word of this session.

Dr Tily: One area that we have looked at—there is material on this in our submission and we are putting out some more material this week—is around corporation tax relief. We have noted that corporation tax paid seems only to be about half of the rate of corporation tax. We understand that there are lots of reliefs in operation when it comes to corporation tax, many of which are no doubt very sensible, but there does seem to be quite a large gap.

Q169 John Glen: Which ones do you not like? Which specific reliefs do you mean?

Dr Tily: To be perfectly frank, we are basically proposing a review to see why the shortfall is as large as it is.
