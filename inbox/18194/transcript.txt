Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Siobhan Casey and Kevin Higgins.

Q1 Chair: Welcome to the Northern Ireland Affairs Committee’s session on pension credit take-up in Northern Ireland. We are joined by Siobhan Casey, the Commissioner for Older People for Northern Ireland, and virtually by Kevin Higgins, Advice NI’s head of policy. Welcome to you both.

Siobhan, we do not have time for much of a preamble, but could you outline the current picture of pension credit take-up in Northern Ireland and why it matters?

Siobhan Casey: It really matters. What I think people do not understand is that receiving pension credit unlocks so many other opportunities for people. For me, though, it is also about protecting people from poverty. If we cannot reach people entitled to pension credit, that is saying a lot.

About 8% of older people in Northern Ireland are in relative poverty, and another 8% are in absolute poverty. We are seeing that, as people move from their 60s to their 70s and 80s, that is growing, and the oldest pensioners are facing the greatest financial vulnerability.

We have very low private pension rates in Northern Ireland compared with the rest of the UK. That means that people are particularly dependent on their state pension, which leaves them vulnerable to rising living costs and unexpected expenses. It is difficult to heat homes adequately and maintain a healthy diet. All those things lead to deterioration in physical and mental health, which puts pressure on public services. When public services are stretched, that makes it even harder for people, making them more vulnerable. There is growing evidence, too, of increased food insecurity among older people.

That is why getting these types of entitlements to them is really important. There is also the fact that older people are likely to live with one or more long-term health conditions or disabilities, which means that their living conditions can worsen. Many older people live on fixed incomes, so they do not have the opportunity to increase their earnings.

Why is this important? I am conscious that the Department for Communities’ analysis dates back to the early 2020s. It is looking to aggregate better data, which is really critical, but the latest trends show that we are having an increase, which is really good. We all know that the Department for Communities has a really good Make the Call campaign, and it is really trying to promote things better. However, our older population—people over 65—is increasing every year by 6,000 or 7,000. It is an ageing demographic, and we have to be aware that this is coming down the line.

Q2 Chair: Siobhan, how many eligible people are not claiming pension credit?

Siobhan Casey: Kevin will probably know this, but I think the Department for Communities has said between 23,000 and 30,000 older people.

Q3 Chair: That is a lot of people. On average, how much is each person missing out on financially?

Siobhan Casey: I will let Kevin speak to that one, because he will probably know the financials a bit better.

Q4 Chair: All right, Kevin, I am going to come to you. Why does it matter, how many eligible people are not claiming, and how much are they missing out on?

Kevin Higgins: Thank you to the Committee for allowing me to dial in remotely. I also thank your staff for all their work to set this up.

For me, the uptake issue is so important because older people are not getting what they are entitled to. They are not getting what the law says they need to live on. To me, that cuts to the heart of it. We have people out there—we are talking here about older people—who the law says require a certain amount of money due to their needs and circumstances, and they are not getting that.

We are in the cost of living crisis that Siobhan talked about. Older people spend a lot longer in their homes—particularly older older people. If you check out the price of home heating oil in Northern Ireland, it is well over £1,000 for 900 litres of oil. That is just one example. We know that about two thirds of households in Northern Ireland rely on home heating oil. That is one example of why it is so important that every penny of money that should be coming into all households, but particularly those of older people, does come in. I welcome that the Committee is focusing on pension credit uptake, because it is an issue that needs a lot of attention.

Regarding the numbers, the estimates from the Department are admittedly dated, from 2019-20. The Department is working on updating those figures, so we expect some shift, but according to those figures, 66,000—about 72%—of those entitled are receiving it. That leaves around 28% or 26,000 pensioner households that are not getting the pension credit they are entitled to. As for amounts, it is about £1.1 million a week in unclaimed pension credit, which totals about £55 million per annum in unclaimed entitlement, which is a significant amount of money—particularly on Budget day. We are all talking about money today, so it is appropriate to talk about the money that is not coming in to older people’s households.

Q5 Chair: What is the trend in pension credit take-up in recent years? Is it getting better or worse?

Siobhan Casey: It is increasing, I think in line with the promotion from the Department for Communities, and the fact that we have 6,000 or 7,000 people every year reaching 65 and coming in. The trends are going up.

Q6 Chair: Siobhan, you mentioned that, if you claim pension credit, it unlocks other packages of support. What are those other packages of support?

Siobhan Casey: Things like housing benefit and cold weather payments, the free TV licence for those aged 75 and over, and help with dental, optical and hospital travel costs. Kevin will back this up: the reason a lot of people are not claiming is that they are afraid to declare—well, first, as we spoke about outside the room, Tonia, there is their pride. They do not want to go out and say they need extra support. Then you have the whole matter of declaring savings to public authorities. People are afraid to do that because it may take away from other benefits, and because they are private. The thing is, even if you are entitled to a small amount of pension credit—you declare £1 earnings for every £500 of savings—it unlocks so much more. I really do think that is the message that we need to get out.

Q7 Simon Hoare: I want to ask about pride and the reticence that it might bring, along with the use of the word “credit”. Credit suggests debt. Have you any thoughts on that? There is a presumption that older people are less keen to apply. Is there any differential in Northern Ireland between urban and rural? It has always been my take that rural communities are more resilient—if I could use that phrase—and less inclined than urban to ask for anything. I just wonder whether you have any observations on that.

Siobhan Casey: I worked in Age NI for 15 and a half years before I took this job, and it would have been a constant thing that people were saying they did not want to take money in case it took away from schools or other areas of the economy. They did not realise that this was an entitlement. It is not a favour; it is actually an entitlement. That would have been my experience in trying to convince people: “Yes, you’re entitled to this. You worked hard for it. It is not taking away from other key services. So it’s important that you do claim.” Kevin, do you have more information on the rural-urban split?

Kevin Higgins: Thank you for the question. My sense is that, in rural Northern Ireland, there are certain issues that may make it a bit more difficult for older people to get the information and advice that they might need in order to go ahead and maximise their rights and entitlements. An example is access to services, being able to travel to get to them, and reliance on public transport. That can be difficult; sometimes there are issues with that. Maybe there is just one bus to a certain place and one going back in the evening. Travel can be very difficult. In urban areas, there will be more accessible services, the likes of advice services and advice centres, where you can pop in and seek the information that you may need. There could be more resilience, I agree, in rural areas, but that resilience might mean that people are not pursuing their rights and entitlements; they are getting by.

That takes me to some of the other issues. You are right about a lot of older people having a sense of independence. They have worked all their days; they have paid their big stamp and their national insurance, and they want to have the respect and the dignity and the right to be able to live on the stamp and the insurance. That feeds into their being reluctant and thinking, “Why should I have to divulge all my financial information—my bank accounts and so on?”

Interestingly, there is another issue, which is almost guilt. Older people are very aware of what has happened generally over the last 10 or 15 years in terms of cuts to the social security system, particularly for working-age people. To a large extent, with the triple lock, older people have been protected, but working-age people have experienced various austerity measures, cuts, reforms, freezes and so on. Older people are very aware of that, and I think that has fed into this as well. There is a “Well, I have enough to do me” attitude. They are seeing other people finding things a lot tighter or a lot more difficult than they are, and therefore there is a certain reluctance to come forward.

Q8 Simon Hoare: I am not going to get into the weeds of the overall welfare budget, because I think that the data points in the opposite direction to the one that you suggest, but let me ask this very specific question of you both. A yes or no answer will suffice. In your assessment, is there a problem with the use of the word “credit”?

Siobhan Casey: It is not something that has come to my attention before.

Kevin Higgins: No, I don’t think so. Very often what I find that people are coming through our doors about is cold, hard cash. People are struggling to pay bills, get their oil tank filled and so on. At this stage, because we are in this cost of living crisis, things like what you have mentioned are maybe less important. If we were not in the situation that we are in, things like that potentially would come to the surface and the name of things could be important, but at the moment we are in a place where it is potentially less important.

Q9 Mr Kohler: Thank you for joining us, Kevin and Siobhan. I am wondering to what extent this is a Northern Ireland problem or a UK-wide problem. Do you have any figures on the uptake in GB, or the regions of GB, compared with Northern Ireland? Is it better or worse, or do we not know?

Kevin Higgins: We should know. I have not analysed the figures, but there will be uptake figures. I know that the Government have just recently, in GB, launched a pension credit uptake campaign. I can certainly get the numbers for the Committee, but that is not something I have analysed in preparation for this meeting.

Chair: It would be great if you can write to us on that, Kevin.

Q10 Robin Swann: Good morning, Siobhan and Kevin. You have touched on the impact on eligible pensioners who do not claim pension credit, as that unlocking mechanism opens up other avenues for support. In your assessment, how much is the low uptake of pension credit contributing to pensioner poverty and fuel poverty? Kevin, you touched briefly on the price of oil, but what about the overall impact?

Siobhan Casey: I think it is definitely impacting it. There is the heating cost, but we can also look at the figures around food insecurity. Older people are the largest group going to the likes of the Trussell Trust. There is a 91% increase in those going for food support. Then there is the reduction in the winter fuel payment too, so there is certainly an impact on people.

Kevin Higgins: I totally agree with that. The independent advice sector has done some benefit uptake work. The Northern Ireland Audit Office says that, in one year, there was something like £17 million generated—this is extra money in people’s pockets—during the benefit uptake campaign with the independent advice sector. It is currently estimated—although the number could be very different from this—that the unclaimed amount is about £55 million. If you do a targeted uptake campaign that generates around £15 million or £20 million, you will have a significant impact on the amount of money that is going unclaimed.

I completely agree with you: imagine the impact that that £15 million or £20 million will have on the mental health and wellbeing of older people. They will feel, “I don’t have to get 500 litres or 200 litres; I can fill the tank up. I can get proper food in the fridge. I can get taxis to go to GP and dental appointments.” It would be a fantastic boost if we could maximise the uptake for older people.

Siobhan Casey: Kevin mentioned taxis, and it is about their social life too. Community transport is being used up taking people to health appointments. Particularly in rural areas, Simon, we are trying to get people to use community transport to do their shopping, to get to the hairdresser or to do whatever. That extra money gives them that bit more independence, so it is really important.

Q11 Robin Swann: Do you have any information about the eligibility threshold? Would raising it by a few pounds bring more pensioners into eligibility?

Siobhan Casey: Kevin and I did some work on that a couple of years ago. The figures for the people not meeting the threshold were quite staggering then, so I think that needs to be looked at.

Kevin Higgins: Yes. The point about thresholds is very interesting. The Committee will know about the new state pension that has come in for people who retired after 2016. For those with a full contribution on the new state pension, the weekly amount is £241.30. The standard minimum guarantee in pension credit for a single person is £238—just a couple of pounds below. That speaks very clearly to your point about amounts of money. It is good that people claim their state pension, but on the downside there is scope to be able to get a top-up of pension credit. As we have touched on, that is so important for the likes of passporting.

In Northern Ireland, we have a home heating oil support scheme. No doubt, there will be other targeted schemes to help with the cost of living in the Budget, but you can bet that a lot of those schemes will require you to be on pension credit to get an entitlement. That reinforces how important it is that people get their pension credit entitlement. If they do not, they will lose out on some of the cost of living measures that will be announced, maybe in the Budget.

Q12 Gavin Robinson: Good morning to you both. To follow on from Robin’s point about thresholds, about 20-odd years ago, when I was starting to help in political office, a lady came to me and we talked to her about pension credit. At that stage, £6,000 was the maximum that you were allowed as savings, and she had about £7,000. I have never forgotten her looking me in the eye and saying, “But, son, £4,000 of that is for Wilton’s,” which was the local funeral director. That showed the sort of savings and preparations she had made—so that she wasn’t burdening anyone else. At that stage we were able to talk to her not about divesting savings, but about maybe taking a prepayment plan, so that she could invest in the funeral but also bring herself within the eligibility criteria for pension credit. Whether it is Advice NI or even the Make the Call service, do they give practical and tangible advice that would allow somebody to come within the criteria for pension credit?

Kevin Higgins: Thank you for that question. There are a couple of really important aspects. It was £6,000 and my understanding is that it is now £10,000, but the point still applies. That lady could come in with £11,000 and say exactly the same thing, so it’s a fair point. In terms of thresholds, if we could shift it from £6,000 to £10,000, which was some years ago, there is a debate to be had around shifting it from £10,000 to £15,000 and so on, or up to £16,000. So there is a point well made there as well.

On another point, often in advice centres people come through the door, we start the calculation, and we find, as you did, Gavin, that the person has no entitlement to pension credit. For whatever reason, they are slightly under. What you have to do is not leave it there and not just park that issue. You look at the circumstances of the individual. Very often you will explore other health problems or disabilities and so on. Then it becomes a circuitous route. If you can get an entitlement to attendance allowance, not only is that a bit of additional money, but attendance allowance unlocks additional premiums within the pension credit calculation, and that will then allow you to get a successful pension credit application.

Your point is well made. You know this in constituency offices and advice centres. The case has an initial face value, but your work only starts at that point. Then it is about exploring and doing a holistic piece of work, exploring every avenue, and very often, as in the example that I gave you, you can get to a place where you can unlock the additional income that that household is entitled to. There is an issue on the savings—I agree with you, and I also agree that very often there is more than meets the eye to doing this pension credit uptake work. It is about not accepting what comes forward at face value, and unlocking the entitlement that will come after you do some hard, hard yards.

Siobhan Casey: Every £500 of savings is equivalent to £1 of income. If you are out by £2,000 or £3,000, that is £6 of income. It is about beginning to help people understand the nuances of this and the fact that even if they are not going to get the maximum amount, it will open up other opportunities, as Kevin says, for other benefits.

Q13 Claire Hanna: Siobhan, you mentioned briefly that private pension build-up is lower in Northern Ireland. Do you have any high-level data on that?

Siobhan Casey: No, not here.

Q14 Claire Hanna: It rings true, but I wasn’t sure if there was data. How effective is pension credit at tackling poverty? Do you think that the level of pension credit is adequate where it is currently set?

Siobhan Casey: You could always be asking for more, Claire. The challenge is getting people to receive what is there now, and then there are onward negotiations on that. In terms of recommendations, at the minute we are calculating the number of people applying. There was a report two weeks ago on planning for an ageing society. As a Department we need to look past publicity and application numbers and actually look at the successful awards and additional income. With this demographic increasing year on year by 7,000 or whatever, how are we going to do the proactive thing?

Also, at the stage of digitalisation, we have to engage people who are missing out on key support. At the minute the Department—Kevin will know about this because we have been working with the Department—are going in now, targeting specifically the 23,000 to 30,000 older people who they believe are missing out. They write a letter and send it to their homes. That type of targeting is not scattergun; it is direct, and that is really good. We have worked with them on the outreach letters, and will work with them on the promotion of it.

A person receiving a letter at home might still be a bit concerned. It is about Advice NI, Age NI and the commissioner’s office getting together and saying, “Look out for this. This is important to you.” That is a good proactive approach that has been adopted by the Department. The point I made earlier about the independent advice sector is also important. There needs to be support for those organisations, for accessible claim handling and capacity. People would prefer to come to Advice NI or Age NI or their local resource centre to get support. We have to be seen in the collaborative and collective approach.

The other two recommendations are that other public bodies need to be signposting as well; it is about working right across. I know they will do that. The Housing Executive and others will all be working to try to identify people; good work is being done but, because of that ageing demographic coming up, it has to get better and be more targeted. I know the Department is doing more about aggregation of proper data and analysis, which will be good.

Q15 Claire Hanna: Are those eligibility criteria and the income and savings thresholds at the right level? With the cost of living, do you think those are set at the right place to bring into the remit all those who need support?

Kevin Higgins: It is a fair point. During your whole working life, you are always encouraged to save for your retirement and be responsible. To some extent, you should not be penalised for that. There is a judgment call to be made there on whether the level of savings is a bit too low and punitive. As we discussed earlier, if you raised that threshold from £10,000 to £15,000 or whatever, there is a balance to be struck in providing support to those people. It is not an awful lot of money. Older people like to have a bit put by to leave to future generations or to take care of themselves later on. There is definitely a debate now; I would support those levels increasing.

I have a couple of comments on your point, Claire, around levels. To repeat, the new state pension is £241, and the minimum guarantee of pension credit is £238. Structurally straightaway, if there are no other circumstances, that person will not be entitled to pension credit because the new state pension is those couple of pounds over. There is a systemic and structural issue there.

A bit of thought is required in terms of trends and so on. In May 2021, there were 63,450 pension credit claimants; in May 2026, there are 60,030. That has gone from roughly 63,500 to 60,000. I take Siobhan’s point that recent months have bucked the trend. There has been a slight increase in pension credit claims, but it is a downward trajectory. It is understandable, given the increased amounts in the new state pension, and the increased state retirement age. I think shortly we may get to the point where we are below 60,000 in pension credit claims for the first time ever.

There is more to the statistics; it takes a little bit of nuance. There could be a slight increase now, but the overall trend could be down. That makes it even more important that for every single household that should be getting even a little bit of pension credit, for the reasons we outlined, there should be more effort. The Department needs to put in more effort—we all do—to ensure that those households are getting every pound they are entitled to.

Q16 Gavin Robinson: That helpfully leads us into the next question. Kevin, you were talking about the Department needing to do more. As you know, this Committee is Westminster-based and clearly interested in Northern Ireland, but there is obviously a wider Whitehall discussion to be had. Could you help us understand the breakdown in responsibility for the funding, promotion and delivery of pension credit between the Department for Communities in Northern Ireland, which is a devolved Department, and the Department for Work and Pensions in Westminster?

Kevin Higgins: Others can correct me if I am wrong on this, but I believe that as social security is devolved to Northern Ireland, complete responsibility lies with the Department for Communities and the Minister in that Department. To me, it is very straightforward in that respect. We do not have to look over to Westminster and the DWP; we can take responsibility ourselves in Northern Ireland to do what we have to do, which in this case is to increase pension credit uptake.

There are systems and so on, and there is the issue of parity, but the fundamental point is that social security is devolved, and therefore the responsibility lies with the Department and the Minister.

Q17 Gavin Robinson: To unpack that a little bit, it is devolved, but we operate the same system on exactly the same terms with the same criteria and legislation. The money is coming directly from Westminster, and the systems used are Westminster systems. To deviate from any of that, there is a cost. At the moment, while it is the Department’s responsibility, it is an English or Westminster system that is being deployed in Northern Ireland, is it not?

Kevin Higgins: That is exactly right. That is why we would operate on this principle of parity, so that we can achieve the efficiencies and savings by using the same systems, and we do not build our own system here. Yes, we are devolved and independent, but the principle of parity would carry across for the vast amount of time.

One example—and you will know this well, Gavin—is the introduction of welfare reforms and universal credit. There was an intense debate about that because of the potential differential impact in Northern Ireland, particularly around the introduction of PIP. Twice as many people per head of population claimed working-age DLA at the time, so there was concern about the impact of welfare reform. We did introduce all of that, but you will remember that the Evason working group was set up, and then we put our own unique mitigations in place.

For example, our benefit cap mitigation was put in place following the Evason report recommendations. That benefit cap mitigation is worth its weight in gold now, because the Government abolished the two-child limit. So for families with children in Northern Ireland, we have the two-child cap abolished, and we have the benefit cap mitigated—it is fantastic. But you are right: we had to build an IT system to deliver our unique mitigation system, and we have to fund it. The Executive took the decision to fund it out of the block grant.

I digress a little bit there. In terms of the pension credit, yes, we tend to plough the same furrow, but the responsibility is local. In terms of uptake, we can do a lot around awareness raising and uptake campaigns, giving a little bit of funding to places like Age NI, Advice NI and the advice sector to maximise the effectiveness of uptake campaigns.

Q18 Gavin Robinson: Thanks, Kevin. Before I bring you in, Siobhan, let me just put it this way. There are three things: funding, promotion and delivery. Siobhan Casey: Kevin mentioned unique mitigations. I spoke earlier on about the fact that there are 660,000 leaflets going out from the Department for Work and Pensions here to promote this. Q21 Gavin Robinson: Kevin, I presume you are pretty NI-based, whereas Siobhan is now engaging with people across the United Kingdom, so I will move the question on just for you, if that is okay. We talked about trust, and Simon asked about whether “credit” is the right phrase, because that implies a level of debt or some sort of burden. Kevin Higgins: We would find that, and it is to be expected. Older people will not know the intricacies of the benefits system, and the starting point for many is this fear of doing something wrong and not knowing what they do not know. Obviously, there will be a fear factor, so to me the fear factor is very important, as is the complexity of the system, as I said.

When people come into advice services and we unlock entitlement, the severe disability premium additional amount on pension credit is actually £86 a week. I don’t know about that.” There is that reticence.
