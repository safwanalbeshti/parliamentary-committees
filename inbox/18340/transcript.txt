Phil Garrigan: I joined the fire and rescue service 37 years ago, in 1990.

Chair: You look well on it.

Phil Garrigan: Thank you very much. I have a good understanding of the changes. To put it into perspective, it is significantly different now than it was when I joined, and that is directly attributable to climate change. I will give you some examples to put it into context. In 2018, as the chief fire officer for Merseyside Fire and Rescue Service, we had responsibility for the co-ordination of national assets in response to large-scale events that overwhelm a particular fire and rescue service. We were bringing resources from across the country to deal with fires in Saddleworth Moor and Winter Hill. That was probably the first time we had started to see the challenges of wildfires that faced the fire and rescue service more broadly.

Fast forward quickly to 2022, when we had a significant number of incidents and the hottest temperatures on record. We had 14 major incidents to clear, and fire and rescue services came under significant pressure during that period—and I mean significant pressure. We were co-ordinating the response. Being able to respond to those incidents was placing demands on the whole of the UK, because the declaration of a major incident means that the resources available at that local area were overwhelmed.

Then you move on to 2025, which had the highest number of wildfires on the record. That was surpassed in 2026, when the fire and rescue service had to respond to over 1,300 wildfires. That included one particular day—and you will recall that day in August—when there were nine major incidents declared, and on the top of that there was a train derailment in and around the same time.

I can speak from an absolute place of knowledge. The fire and rescue service was asking for resources to support its operations and we were struggling to be able to meet the demand placed on us. From a national point of view, we were overwhelmed with the requests that came in. It was previously described as unprecedented, but from 2022 to 2026, that is not unprecedented. This is now familiar territory that we are straying into.

To re-emphasise the point around funding and resources, it is right and fitting that we talk about the wildfire aerial capabilities and specialist teams, and undoubtedly that has been supported—that is why I mentioned green shoots—under the current Government, but when you look at some of the fundamentals, at that particular point in time we needed firefighters and people. In some way, we have to increase the workforce capacity, either through recruitment or through elements of coming up with an alternative way of surging with the demand placed on us, recognising that the incidents we are talking about fluctuate at times and we need to be able to fluctuate with them. There is definitely a capacity challenge for the UK fire and rescue service in response to climate change.

Similarly, we can collectively work together to reduce the impact and the number of wildfires. Chair: Thank you very much. Dr Kochhar, we have heard about councils and firefighting services; to what extent do you think our national health service is adequately funded, staffed and resourced, specifically with regard to being prepared for the effects of climate change and climate adaptation and resilience?

Dr Kochhar: We believe that the NHS is not yet adequately funded, resourced or staffed in terms of preparation, especially for the response to extreme heat. Much of the NHS estate remains highly vulnerable to overheating. The UK Health Alliance on Climate Change report found that 90% of our hospital buildings in England are vulnerable to overheating. That is a significant amount. The consequences for patients and staff are enormous.

Our reports come from our membership. I can share with you several surveys we have done. For example, a survey by the Doctors’ Association UK in June 2026 found that 86% of respondents said that colleagues had felt unwell because of the heat and 68% said heat had affected patient care and safety. There were significant problems, including the highest ever A&E queues. Admissions were running at 92% bed occupancy, which naturally affects productivity and resilience and how staff cope as well. Personally, I felt wearing PPE so difficult.

This is not only about the heat affecting the individual; it is about recovery, infection and the hydration of patients. It affects the storage of vaccines and antibiotics. We had one GP working in sexual health telling us that the air conditioning and the fridge both went out of order and they did not know whether the vaccines and the antibiotics worked. The next day they figured out that they were fine, but that adds a dimension to the problem. We know that many operations were cancelled.

Chair: We are all aware of the historic winter pressures; we are now being told we need to start getting used to the idea of summer pressures because of the heat. That is about the general demand. You can imagine suffering with a fever or giving birth and being on a ward with a 38° or 40° temperature at a time when you are very vulnerable in your own health. It must be likely that those temperatures killed people who would have stayed alive if they could have been treated in more ambient temperatures.

Dr Kochhar: You are correct, and there is evidence to say that. Baroness Brown presented some evidence earlier as well. There is definitely evidence from various factors showing that the temperature going up even a little bit would be extremely difficult.

Chair: Anecdotally, I have heard—as will others—of constituents who have discharged themselves from hospital because they are so hot, saying, “I would rather suffer at home than be here.” Obviously we do not know the impact of that. What should the NHS be doing immediately to ensure that people are not treated in dangerous temperatures that are likely to exacerbate their health concerns? Dr Kochhar: Naturally, we talk about resources. Before we get there, you made a point about anecdotal patient suffering. A GP messaged us who was also a patient, as he was having a lot of trouble with hydration and the maintenance of electrolytes. He bought this piece of kit that cost £1,000 because he could afford it. Resource needs to be appropriately directed and appropriately focused on improving our estates. The Government have said they will give us some resource, which is far from adequate. How that resource will be prioritised is key, as even the newer buildings are not heat efficient at the moment. Some of the newer buildings are worse than the older buildings. How will they get it? Will they have to make an application? There has to be clarity on how the resource will be given to us to improve the NHS. There is a huge shortfall. NHS England said that the shortfall in estates was worth about £17.7 billion and there is nowhere near that amount of funding coming.

Dr Kochhar: They probably do have an understanding, but it is very low on their list of priorities.

Dr Kochhar: Exactly. They need to be pushed and this has to come from the Government.

Councillor Fodor, as you have already alluded to, local authorities are under a huge amount of pressure regarding their time, their people and their budgets, and there is LGR, SEND and social care. Councillor Fodor: The Local Government Association does not itself have a position that there should be a statutory duty. There is not a consensus on that. However, the majority of members probably think there is a case for it. If that were the case, there would be a new burden that would then have to be funded. You would start to get a more common baseline of what gets done across the country.

Especially coming at a time of local government reorganisation, there will be a fair amount of churn in terms of staff, skills and responsibilities. It is important to embed the right arrangements into all new authorities, as well as existing continuing authorities, to make sure there is the capacity, training, professional networks, exchange of experiences and the budgets, both capital and revenue. Just as the health service has to start planning for heat as well as for winter, councils have had to start planning for heat, on top of planning for floods and so on.

One service needs its improvements alongside the other service needing its investments as well. You have a lot of implications, and there are councils that probably have not started thinking about what this means in terms of future planning, funding bids and the pressures they are going to face, on top of their current pressures in terms of having very limited resources to balance their budgets, as they legally have to do.

John Whitby: Councillor Fodor, in terms of our changing climate, how resilient are public facilities such as local transport, schools and care homes?

Councillor Fodor: I am sure they would all make a very strong case, including care homes, where they are publicly run, transport systems, education and so on. The one-off investment gives us time, but that is all it gives us, and we would need to sustain it into the longer term.

Cat Eccles: Last year MHCLG gave increased funding settlements to fire authorities and multi-year settlements, which is helpful, but we are seeing that against a backdrop of 14 years of austerity under the previous Government. Phil Garrigan: A significant amount of the positive investment previously linked into things like the GDP deflator. In the 2026 period we lost 50 properties—50 homes—and that has a devastating impact on families, on memories, on schoolwork, and all of those things. It is interesting, because reference was made to the emergency alert, which was made in and around the time where we had lost 50 homes. If asked about whether the emergency alert was legitimate, I was going to say one thing, which is that you need to speak to the 50 people who lost their homes about the devastating impact on them, and to the nine firefighters that were hospitalised in response to that, putting themselves between the fire front and the home.

What do I think will happen if we do not get ahead of that? I think the 50 will increase significantly and, at worst, we may see loss of life. We need to ensure that we are equipping fire and rescue services—and other partners, understandably, and do all the preventive work—with the resources they need to operate effectively and safely within that environment.

Chris Hinchliff: Dr Kochhar, we have heard today that the climate we are facing now is the least extreme we will experience over the remainder of our lifetimes. That means that hospitals and GP practices are facing enormous vulnerabilities from climate change—not just from extreme heat but from flooding, power outages, water shortages and transport disruption. Do you feel that NHS staff have the capacity to prepare adequately for those vulnerabilities? Particularly in the case of emergency incidents arising from climate change, what does it take for our NHS to keep providing healthcare to our population?

Dr Kochhar: We have to recognise that heatwaves are no longer exceptional events. They are going to happen; we have already heard such expertise before.

The health service is running at capacity at the moment. There is very little spare capacity and we are cut bare to the bone at the moment. This will only stretch it further, and the ability to deliver is going to be non-existent, because the staff as well are suffering from the effects on health. For example, who does heat affect? It affects vulnerable groups who are already quite sick, and they are going to be sicker. We know from evidence that mental health issues go through the roof, and admissions because of mental health issues climb massively. We just don’t have the capacity to deliver this. Unless we prepare for it and there is some sensible thinking about how we are going to deal with this, with some spare capacity, it is going to be extremely difficult.

Chris Hinchliff: To be clear, we all know about the huge pressures the NHS is under anyway, regardless of what is happening with climate change. Staff across the NHS just cannot really think about this currently, can they?

Dr Kochhar: No, they cannot. As I said before, it is not very high on the list of priorities for people to rectify. We will deal with that when we get to it, and be reactive at that stage, rather than think about it in a preventive fashion. How can we get there? How can we invest in coolant mechanisms that are energy efficient so that we still meet our net zero targets, and still do sensible stuff? That is the thinking we have to do and that is the resource we have to provide.

Chris Hinchliff: If we start crossing some of the very concerning tipping points that exist around climate breakdown, do the BMA have concerns that we will get to a point where access to healthcare in places and in certain circumstances just will not be possible anymore?

Dr Kochhar: That is one concern. Secondly, the confidence of staff to deliver that healthcare is going to plummet. It is so hard, because you are seeing sicker patients all the time then. If the temperature is 40°C, doctors will go into work, they will do what they can, but it is harder.

Chris Hinchliff: That brings me on to my final question for you. The Government talk about £6.75 billion over nine years to deal with critical estate risks across the NHS, but we heard from the previous panel that only around £30 million is there to deal with extreme heat and trying to provide cooling. You have already described that as far from adequate, and I think we can all understand why. In your view, what will happen if that is the resource made available in the short to medium term, in terms of not only the impact on patients but staff recruitment and retention across the NHS?

Dr Kochhar: We currently have problems, and they will be exacerbated. You mentioned £6.7 billion; NHS England says £3.7 billion is needed acutely now for the high risk. We are already talking about more than 50% going to repair structures that are so much in need. Take, for example, the neighbourhood health centres proposed in the new health plan: these are going to be buildings, so the same will apply to those structures. The thinking at this stage should be about how to make things lasting, and how we are going to improve things for patients as well as staff.

Chris Hinchliff: Is the BMA concerned about what it means from the staff perspective if you ask people to turn up to work in hospitals in 40° temperatures, trying to keep people healthy day in, day out, when we are facing summer after summer of heat like that? Are you concerned about what that will do to the workforce?

Dr Kochhar: It is very hard. It would lead to staff burnout. We already know doctors are burning out, but it would lead to massive issues with the delivery of care. They would still go in, but it would lead to problems.

Martin Rhodes: Dr Kochhar, you have talked about the impact on healthcare workers. How can we protect them adequately in extreme heat and unsafe working conditions while maintaining patient care?

Dr Kochhar: There are some short-term things that we could do and there are long-term things that we can do. Obviously, the short-term things are to invest appropriately in coolant mechanisms and so on and so forth. Healthcare is not only about meeting a patient. I have to have access to the correct IT, my intensive care unit should be up and running and the theatres should be up and running. These are all areas. If the temperature goes up, we know that healing is retarded, wounds do not heal and infections are massive. We doctors want to make patients better and it gives us great difficulty when we cannot get them better, and then it spirals out of control very soon.

Martin Rhodes: What are your thoughts about a maximum workplace temperature? What would be the potential benefits or drawbacks of that in the health sector?

Dr Kochhar: Interestingly, the BMA doesn’t have a policy as such on what the temperature should be. As I said earlier, whatever the temperature, we will go into work and we will try to do the best we can.

Martin Rhodes: You do not have a position on what the temperature should be, but what sort of factors would need to be weighed up in terms of determining that? I am not asking you to set a figure; I am asking you just to say what sort of things we would need to think about.

Dr Kochhar: We believe that workplaces should have a responsibility to ensure working standards, which means that the environmental situation and the temperature stays below a certain limit and that we could perform and deliver healthcare to the best of our abilities.

Martin Rhodes: We are moving to a situation—people have already mentioned this and I want to push you a bit further on it—where we are likely to have similar summer pressure to winter pressure. What are the implications for healthcare staff? You have touched on this, but I want to pin down the impact on staff health and wellbeing.

Dr Kochhar: We believe that summer pressures should be treated as a replica of winter pressures. They are two different sets and they need different resilience approaches and different infrastructure measures. For staff in particular, providing them with a safe environment to work in, where they can treat patients, is the most appropriate thing we could do.

Martin Rhodes: I have a general question for the panel. I think you have all referred in various ways to the impact of climate change on vulnerable communities. What policy developments would be most effective in tackling the unequal impacts of climate change?

Dr Kochhar: The first one leads on from my councillor friend next to me, who spoke very clearly about reaching out to vulnerable patients in the community right at the beginning to make sure they have appropriate coolant mechanisms. Prevention is better than cure. Not getting them into hospitals is much more sensible, and that is what we should be doing. We heard earlier that the outcomes in care homes were extremely poor—there were extra deaths and so on—so it would be appropriate to reach out to these situations with appropriate coolant mechanisms to stop the patients getting so unwell that they come in.

Phil Garrigan: I would come at it slightly differently. I suppose sometimes the people and the communities in which they live understand what the solutions are better than organisations that think they have an understanding of it. My comment is around enabling people to address their needs directly but—don’t get me wrong—with some form of handrails in regard to what they should be thinking about and how we should mitigate against that risk.

There are probably some things that we have not discussed. We talk about our roles in responding to incidents, but it is also about the work that happens as a result of communities responding. During the wildfires, farmers mobilised and cut in firebreaks and supported fire and rescue service operations. There is something about how we can get the community and the citizen to support each other as part and parcel of that kind of intervention, from both a preventive point of view and a caring responsibility perspective, looking in on a neighbour. I know that is not particularly strategic and it is not particularly creative, but some of those things really work.

It is about mobilisation and it is about citizen responsibility, because I do not think we can do everything for everyone. We can do our best and we can communicate effectively, inform and campaign, but equally we have to enable people to look after themselves to a greater or lesser degree.

Councillor Fodor: It is a very important question. Local authorities will generally know a lot of the most vulnerable people and neighbourhoods and different types of resident who are vulnerable for a whole range of different reasons, whether it is due to health, age, having a child at home or having a disability that means they need support, adaptations and equipment to live comfortably. First, it is important to support community initiatives.

Secondly, we should co-ordinate with many different agencies and bodies. Data sharing can become quite a significant obstacle, due to data protection issues. If you bring together the different datasets, you can identify far more of the people and the different categories who will need help, because they are on a priority services register or they are known to be getting home care or a whole range of other issues. It is so important to bring that data and work together, and to anticipate the issues and prepare for them. That is where we need to invest in the preparedness and development work that will allow us to be better prepared when there are future incidents.

There are people who are very frightened that they will be trapped during flooding, that they cannot live safely at home in overheated homes, that the equipment they rely on for adaptations will not function, whether in heat or in power cuts due to extreme weather events, and that their home constantly overheats and they cannot get out. Communities have created core spaces, with support from local authorities, but evaluating those core spaces identified that some were not fully accessible—they might not have a toilet suitable for disabled people—and that they might not actually be cool all of the time.

There will be other needs during flood occasions, where possibly different places are needed as safe spaces, provided people can get to them. There might need to be support from the local authority or the community organisations we work with to get people to where they can be cool, whether it is a public building, a community building, a faith building or whatever. There is a whole range of different issues because there are so many scenarios of what will happen under extreme weather—severe weather, flood, drought, heat and so on.

However, local authorities will know their place, they will know most of the organisations that can play their part, and they need to have that convening role. They need support to do that working together across agencies, across different services and so on. It is something that has started, clearly because of a whole series of incidents like the pandemic and the heatwave, and because places are at risk of flood at different times.

Martin Rhodes: Thank you. Mr Garrigan, do you want to come again?

Phil Garrigan: Yes. Funnily enough, I am also going to pick up on the experience of covid. As a collective group of organisations, through the local resilience forum and that co-ordination, we were able to identify very vulnerable individuals. We were dropping off prescriptions and medication and targeting the specific individuals we knew were particularly vulnerable and susceptible. Under extreme conditions, under certain triggers, I think that is an appropriate response. Whether that is a major incident declaration in a local area or something bigger and broader than that, I think there is the facility to be able to direct collective resources towards very vulnerable individuals.

During covid, we were able to overcome some of the data protection concerns and make sure that the person we were delivering the prescription to was someone who vitally needed it. If you can get around it through the pandemic, I am sure we could get around it in regard to extreme heat so that we are able to ensure that we are supporting that particular individual.

Martin Rhodes: Would it be helpful for all the different agencies if situations such as extreme heat or flooding—whatever it is—were identified as times when you could take action to share data in a way that you would not normally, in order to be able to respond to vulnerable groups?

Phil Garrigan: Yes.
