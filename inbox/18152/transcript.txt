Q2 The Chair: Thank you very much. Will, I understand you have to leave at 5 pm, so we will try to keep our questions pithy and if you could also keep your answers relatively concise that would be helpful, so that we can get through as much as possible by then.

Perhaps I could just start with an overview about the threat picture. We have had several strategies in recent years. I am interested to hear your views on how you see the threat picture. Also, in the light of the recent space strategy that was published in early September, what is your take on its objectives and commitments in addressing those threats? Will, can I start with you?

Will Marshall: Maybe I can start with a tiny bit of background first on what we do, so that you have some context. Planet launches and operates fleets of earth imaging satellites. We have launched about 700 satellites, second only to SpaceX in the number of satellites launched, and we manage these large, proliferated fleets, which do earth imaging. They have a range of uses, from sustainability—we help to track deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon every day, and help Scotland to monitor its environmental regulations—through to security, where we are the main source of data for Ukraine today, for example, enabling them to monitor threats coming from Russia, plan their drone flights and all sorts of things such as that. We have a team on the ground there. I recently came back from there. Other examples include NATO monitoring and finding hybrid Russian warfare activities in the Baltics.

Space data and satellites are of course critical to national security and their resilience is key, so obviously this panel is an important matter. The way we approach resilience, just to give a sense of how we tackle it, is anchored on three things. One is proliferated architectures. Prior to Planet, it used to be that you would have a few satellites, but we were the first to build a satellite constellation of over 100. We maintain over 200 in orbit, and they are resilient. At the beginning of the Ukraine war, the Viasat system got jammed very quickly, but Starlink was operable. Some of the individual earth imaging satellites got dazzled, but our system of hundreds of satellites was resilient and has continued to operate every time, every day, throughout the war.

Our second piece is working closely with national Governments on specific threats to our fleet. We work with US Cyber Command and get intelligence from it about what threats could be happening, especially state threats.

The third level is being able to rapidly reconstitute satellites. We have rapid launches. We were meant to have another launch today but it just got postponed due to weather in the Cape. Generally, we are launching about four or five times per year; typically, around 50 new satellites per year.

Iterating those capabilities, we can build about 40 satellites per week. It is the most prolific system, alongside SpaceX’s Starlink, in being able to build satellites very quickly, about 40 per week, such that we can reconstitute those satellites very quickly in the event of something more catastrophic.

That is how we tackle it. We also try to be good actors in the broader field. Some of my work at NASA was on orbital space debris and we were the first to do open standards of publishing satellite positions so that we could co-ordinate and ensure that there were no collisions between satellites by keeping the satellites really low so that they do not create long-term space debris.

Why am I saying all these things? Primarily, because when the UK faces questions of space resilience, it needs to move into these kinds of directions: proliferating fleets. One of the ways to do that is to do hybrid capabilities—a mix of tapping commercial systems and building sovereign systems. That can be done very quickly, as we have done with Ukraine. For example, Ukraine asked us to build and operate some satellites directly for its sovereign ownership, as well as tapping our data feeds, and we were able to put its first satellite into space two months after the contract. We just did something very similar for Sweden. So now you can get sovereignty—control of the satellites—and speed, whereas you used to have to choose one or the other. I believe the UK could tap these sorts of ways of tracking and having more resilient systems by leaning into proliferated architectures and that that is very affordable these days, as Ukraine has proved.

Essentially, we are encountering a situation in which our space systems are becoming more vulnerable, there are more of them, they are becoming more open and more people are learning about space because they are doing things in space. Where is the weak point within your organisation? Nobody really knows that, which is why it is such a tricky point to tackle. The mysterious nature of the beginning of cyber attacks and the way in which they could impact space systems is a worry.

The Chair: We could talk about this the rest of the afternoon, I am sure, but we will move on to Julia.

Dr Julia Balm: I will echo a lot of what has been said already. I will start with the broad threat landscape picture and then go into the NSS and how that tackles it. Generally, the greatest vulnerability we are facing at the moment is that our increased dependence on space systems is not necessarily matched with enough investment or urgency and that we do not necessarily have enough response options at this given time. The pinpoint for that is both in day-to-day services and when it comes to defence. I will unpack that later.

Statistically, 18% of the UK’s GDP depends on satellite services. We know that one lost day of space systems would cost £1.4 billion. The National Preparedness Commission estimated that space underpins approximately £450 billion of wider economic activity. We know that we depend heavily on space and we are only growing in that.

We have approximately 12,000 operational satellites in orbit. The estimate is that that will increase to approximately 60,000 by 2030. We are talking about proliferation both on a horizontal and vertical level. But when we are discussing vulnerability, we are looking at the loss of those space systems. As was mentioned earlier, how do we measure those cascading effects across not only the space sector but all the sectors that space impacts? A disruption to a satellite service can propagate and affect several sectors simultaneously. The key questions we are trying to poke at now are: are we designing space systems to withstand disruption for either a short period of time or a period of time that is incredibly relevant—say, when are under attack—or for a longer period of time? Do we have enough contingencies to also allow for continued access to that space service, whether provided by ourselves or by our allies? That is the threat picture that I would paint.

Moving on to the space strategy and how we have addressed that, I would begin by saying that there is a real question of accountability. When we look at space and how a new domain sits across multiple departments, it is really hard to place that responsibility and who is accountable for, say, when a service goes down or is lost. Obviously, we have DBIST, the MoD and even the National Armaments Director Group all trying to work together on the defence elements, but then we are talking about the cascading effects across other departments that are not necessarily waving a space flag or even a defence protect-and-defend flag or mission. That dispersed responsibility can be a strength, but in some ways it is something we have to focus on for cohesivity. In the national space strategy, there is that “one government” approach that is being pitched. Following through and seeing that being implemented would be great, but it is incredibly difficult for a domain such as space, which has the challenges of classification issues, to be able to share that information in a timely manner—we say at “the speed of relevance”—within the UK, but also to our allies, in order to be effective at reconstituting.

Jessie Hamill-Stewart: We are already seeing attacks on military ground infrastructure. You will be aware of the RAF Fairford incident and other intrusions that we are seeing into ground defence infrastructure in the UK. Granted it is not space yet, but it might be space next.

It put Ukraine on the back foot just before Russia invaded.

Will Marshall: Pre-emptively.

Jessie Hamill-Stewart: Yes, pre-emptively, and with a political motivation. What is even more worrying about that case is that it ended up having wide implications as well. If you are not already aware of them, it ended up impacting thousands of European satellite internet broadband users as well, including critical infrastructure.

Cyber attacks such as that demonstrate that not only is space infrastructure vulnerable but, as the UK becomes more and more involved in these military conflicts, we are already seeing evidence of the UK’s defence and military being targeted in this way—physical access as well as cyber attacks. Why do we worry about physical access when it comes to space? We worry because the ground infrastructure is that central controlling and monitoring aspect of the entire constellation and the other dependent services. The importance of the ground infrastructure of space systems cannot be underestimated. It also happens to be the most physically accessible. If you know where it is, you know where to find it and how to potentially try to target it.

Other things the UK is doing at the moment potentially demonstrate vulnerabilities that could be exploited. One of those that is very well known is the disruption of NATS. If you were trying to get a flight in the last few weeks, you will have been affected because of the air traffic control architecture being disrupted by a tiny—minute—error, which resulted in many flights being disrupted. Somebody told me that Heathrow Airport was chock-a-block with passengers. This is disruption, and cyber attacks on space are especially concerning because when it comes to space, as was mentioned, it has all those dependent industries that could be affected. Airports could also be affected by disruption to space because aeroplanes need GPS to take off and land accurately. But it is most worrying because cyber attacks, especially to space, can demonstrate an approach by a nation state—an adversarial nation state—to try to undermine trust in things working well.

Space is extremely important. We cannot afford for it to be disrupted just to demonstrate what people can do. One of the reasons for that is all the dependent sectors. The finance sector needs the timing that is provided by space services, for example. We are at a critical point where we are extremely dependent on space but are also demonstrating these key vulnerabilities in military architecture, which could then be transferrable to the space domain, which worries me quite a lot.

Tanmanjeet Singh Dhesi: Dr Balm, given what we have just heard, what sort of hostile threats in space should we be most concerned about, both sub-threshold and in the event of conflict?

Dr Julia Balm: It is incredibly important to look at the effects of low-cost agitation, such as spoofing and jamming, and how that can have an outsized effect in the space domain. Certain actors can employ agitation tactics more easily than others. I point to the fact that there are nuclear weapon states and non-nuclear weapon states in space and our space infrastructure is inherently linked to our nuclear infrastructure as well, with C2. The ability to escalate and de-escalate is quite distinct.

I point also to a couple of threats that I am currently watching from the last week or so. On 15 October, Russia’s satellite Kosmos 2612 adjusted its orbital inclination to follow an ICEYE satellite, ICEYE-X36, at approximately 26 metres away from it, which if you know the space domain is incredibly close. This manoeuvring into or across low earth orbit into a coplanar orbit is something that we do not usually see because it is extremely expensive to use fuel on such a manoeuvre. But this has happened recently and it could be a signal of some intention from Russia, or it could amount to something related to a kinetic attack or even an electronic attack.

On Sunday, just yesterday, there was an attempted arson of a SpaceX Starlink base in Lithuania, which mimicked what we saw a couple of weeks ago in Bulgaria and Poland. We talked about space-based infrastructure being under attack, but it is really important to look at the links alongside ground-based segments, where it might be that those low-cost agitation tactics of burning a building that contains those receivers are a way to gain an outsized impact.

Will Marshall: Again, you would have to look at all the systems that the UK relies on and which are most vulnerable to this. Tanmanjeet Singh Dhesi: That would be very interesting. Please do send that information through. Lastly, may I come back to you, Ms Hamill-Stewart, on how much risk comes from potential cyber attacks aimed at satellite infrastructure?

Jessie Hamill-Stewart: A lot. You can look at the potential implications. There have been a number of attacks, which I included in a research paper I wrote quite a few years ago, and there were attacks that were intercepted where attackers were making their way through ground infrastructure to the important admin management section.

There are many ways in which cyber risks can escalate. If you are worried about satellites in particular, let us take it out of the defence context, and think about a university spin-off or individual organisation that maybe has a CubeSat. There are just a few students. It is a small company. It does not have loads of governance to manage its cyber security. That is something that you really want to worry about, because then they could, in a really worst-case scenario, crash it or collide it with another satellite. The cyber risks might sound like they are virtual and a bit science fiction, but there are two points on that. First, they can be cyber physical, and cyber physical threats have been a well-known topic area for many decades. Stuxnet, used in an attack on a nuclear plant many years ago, is one example. But there are also physical implications, such as gradually changing the controls or coming in and understanding how the infrastructure works, which gives you the information to then come back and conduct a stronger attack later.

At the moment, attackers, especially nation state-level attackers, probably have the capabilities to do more disruption than they are doing right now. The other thing I would mention here is that hundreds of cyber attacks on space infrastructure are being reported through platforms such as the CSIS, about which I can provide more information at a later time. This worries us, because we need it.

Q4 Lord Godson: Much like the peace talks, you can stop the clocks so we can beat the deadline. We have no idea how to control that, and there are narrow risks to do with cyber threats that we are seeing today—the ability of AI systems to build bioweapons—and we have got to stop them. But we have actually got a more generic control risk that people are just not understanding in the political debate. Everything about AI is about, “Are we going to lose jobs? Are there threats to our cyber security?”, and things like that, but we are facing a much bigger moment. We are facing an existential moment of whether or not we are going to give over control. Alan Turing said that we will inevitably lose control, especially when they start co-ordinating, which is exactly the moment we are at. That was in 1952.

I just say this because space, like every other domain, is going to be attacked by these systems. I would expect that we will see more Hugging Faces that make Hugging Face look like a walk in the park. They could attack our space infrastructure, but they could attack any infrastructure if we do not get a handle on managing AI risk. This Parliament and this country can do stuff about that. The UK’s AI Security Institute can be part of a verification system. We, with our satellite data, can be tracking all of those data centres, to know where to put those on-ground inspections. It is possible to verify AI treaties.

We need clear guardrails, and we need to then actually slow down the pace of development if we are going to be wise with this technology. At the present clip, there is no way we are going to be wise, and we will see open-source technologies doing hacks soon that will shock the world, based on Chinese systems. Those could attack space infrastructure; they could attack any infrastructure. That should be priorities Nos. 1, 2 and 3 for this Parliament until we get it fixed. At the minute there is some sense that we cannot slow down, but it is absolutely necessary to do that. It is the only way to be wise, and the Overton window is changing on that very rapidly. It would have been inconceivable to think about any sort of controls on AI just six months ago in the United States, but that has changed very rapidly. You wait. A few weeks or months from now, we are going to see events that will change the Overton window again, such that it becomes possible to really talk about proper controls on AI.

I agree with Jessie that that is the primary vector you need to be thinking about. Forget nation states; Russia and China are going to be small fry compared with cyber threats because of AI capabilities far more powerful than anything we have dealt with before. That is the trumping consideration for this and every other committee in this House.

Q6 Helen Maguire: I am now going to move on to the UK’s favourite topic: weather—but space weather. The National Risk Register has said that the risk of severe space weather is between 5% and 25%, and that there would be a significant impact. I wondered if you agreed with that assessment and if you could perhaps describe to us what that impact would look like.

Dr Julia Balm: There is some context I can provide. The sun is active for periods of 11 years at a time, and we are currently situated right in the middle of that 11-year cycle. That will be ending by the end of the decade. Because it is so active, it means it is really unpredictable, so the timeline that we get for knowing that there is going to be a geomagnetic storm or radio interference can be from some data released weeks, hours or even 30 minutes before. When it comes to radio frequency interference, we do not really know the impact until it happens.

The event that you are citing would be similar to the Carrington event, which was in 1859 and was the most intense geomagnetic storm on record. We have not faced anything similar to that. Obviously, we did not have a satellite in orbit in 1859, so the effects would be really serious today. The problem now, as I mentioned, is that we are in this active solar flare cycle, and we struggle to be able to attribute whether there is a natural threat or a manmade threat. Say, for example, there is interference in a satellite which impacts atmosphere and atmospheric drag. Can we attribute that necessarily to something that was a hostile activity with hostile intent, or was that something natural? We are seeing that up until the end of 2030.

Helen Maguire: I guess what I wanted to understand a little bit further is the difference between what might happen from space weather and something hostile. Dr Julia Balm: I can just give something brief and then I will pass it on. Essentially, it allows for a degree of ambiguity, actually, because it is a similar thing to what happened with Starlink, which was already mentioned by Will, when the loss of service happened in Ukraine. There is a degree of plausible deniability to say, “Oh, well, this is something that we could not predict and had no control over. It was actually a natural impact”. It is hard to verify that. It is like those locks that you put on your baggage when you go to travel, and it dings with a little pop-up when the TSA has opened the lock. We do not have anything on a satellite currently that announces that you have been intentionally jammed or directly attacked, because it is impossible really to dictate whether or not that was a natural impact. I do not know if anybody else wants to speak.

Will Marshall: I would only say that these things are far from academic. During this solar maximum, part of the challenge was that the academic literature in this area did not predict how big it was going to be. It ended up being more than twice as active as the normal 11-year period. They have this 11-year period of activities, but this one went up almost twice as much and no one really predicted that. People were operating their satellites too low because it excites the upper atmosphere and increases drag. I have colleagues that lost two-thirds of their satellites. Two satellite companies lost two-thirds of their satellites in the first year because their propulsion system was not able to keep them above it fast enough, so they got pulled down. It is great that satellites are operating really low because it means they are naturally clearing from a space debris standpoint. It also increases resolution for a given telescope or what have you. A lot of earth imaging systems operate quite low. If you do not predict it, you can really lose fleets of satellites very quickly. None of that was intentional—except if you ascribe free will to the sun. It had a big effect.

The country should come in and say, “We will help you with your R&D and share some knowhow on how to do that, so you can integrate that into your systems so that they are less vulnerable at a system level to those radiation events”.
