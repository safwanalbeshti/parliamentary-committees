Examination of witness

Irene Graham.

The Chair: Welcome to today’s meeting, which is the fourth oral session as part of the committee’s inquiry into regulatory sandboxes. Thank you, Ms Graham, for attending. The session is open to the public. It is broadcast live and is subsequently accessible via the parliamentary website. A verbatim transcript will be taken of the evidence and will be put on the parliamentary website. Ms Graham, could you start by introducing yourself for the record and then we will proceed to questions?

Irene Graham: Good morning. I am the CEO of the ScaleUp Institute, which looks across the UK at all sectors about how we grow and scale our UK companies.

The Chair: Could we start off by setting the scene? Could you explain to the committee what the ScaleUp Institute is, what it does and how it interacts with regulatory sandboxes?

Irene Graham: Thank you very much. The ScaleUp Institute was set up by the private sector on the back of the 2014 government report into why the UK was third in the world at start-ups but 13th in the world at scaling up, as published by the OECD. The private sector felt that this was such an important issue to tackle, from the Coutu report that was published in 2014, that the institute was formed to put a laser lens into how we address the issues facing our scale-up community in tackling access to markets, access to talent, access to finance, access to infrastructure to grow and scale.

That has been our focus since 2015 and we work across the whole of the country at local level on how we build scale-up ecosystems fit for purpose for our scale-ups, with national governments, local governments, the private sector and the education sector on how we seek to scale businesses and address the issues at hand.

The Chair: Can you explain how the scale-up sector interacts with regulatory sandboxes? Are they important to scale-ups or do they come at an earlier stage of the development of fintechs?

Irene Graham: It comes at both and it is a very important aspect of this access to markets. We have highly innovative companies that scale early, and at that start-up stage they want to scale. A sandbox provides the environment where, in fintech and financial services, you can test products using data that is not readily accessible and at points with live customer data. It is where one can test out a product or a service and work through the innovation, the regulatory impact and the opportunity. Access to markets is the number one issue for our scale-ups at the moment, whether they are early stage or later stage and have got into full blown scale-up and approaching unicorn status. Having the ability to test services, products and ideas is very helpful.

The Chair: How important is it in the whole range of things that are important to businesses that are scaling up?

Irene Graham: Access to markets is a very important part. There is no silver bullet. This is one of the things that helps the innovation and speed to market, but in other areas of scale-up you need access to the end customer, so the service has to be bought and adopted. You need access to the capital, the gross finance, and you need access to the right people. We have a range of levers that we need to get right to enable scaling-up.

Lord Sharkey: Could I ask about the usefulness of the sandboxes? You set out a moment ago quite a long list of the barriers for scaling-up, most of which seem to start with the word “access”. However, when it comes to growth, when are sandboxes useful and when are other forms of regulatory engagement simply more appropriate, faster or cheaper?

Irene Graham: The sandboxes are very appropriate when you are testing out a new service or a new product, whether that is for a new and emerging company service or even an established business that wants to test out a new service and understand the opportunity and the regulatory dynamics around it. That means that in a sandbox environment, using closed datasets but datasets that are not necessarily readily available in a live process, you can try, test and learn as part of it. That then allows you to hone the product for going to market and to understand the regulatory dynamics of that. That is why sandboxes are used by the early-stage company, to established fintechs and banks, because it allows you to be able to do that and even test out partnerships between smaller scaling companies with larger players.

Lord Sharkey: With measurement, assessment and outcomes, there does not appear to have been any CVA-type activity since 2017 when the FCA looked at outcomes. Why is this? Why is there a difficulty, if there is, in assessing the performance of each of these sandboxes?

Irene Graham: There are a couple of things. First, we have been assessing it and keeping reviews of it and we can subsequently provide that information to you. Over a period we have seen good results from it. We have also seen evolution of it into other opportunities, whether it is with the digital sandbox or now—

Lord Sharkey: Sorry to interrupt, but it would be very helpful if you could give concrete examples of these things.

Irene Graham: You can look at a range of companies that have been through it—Zeal, Salary Finance—but there are a range that have been through the sandbox that have resulted in funding or partnerships that have occurred with it. Certainly we can give you those examples. Like anything in a start-up or early test stage, things are successful and some will not move forward, but that is part and parcel of testing and innovating. That is why the sandbox becomes incredibly important in looking at the range of outcomes that come from it. We can certainly provide further evidence on the raising of money, the partnerships that have been formed and the scale of some of the companies that have been through it and where they are today.

Lord Sharkey: And the causal link between the use of a sandbox and the outcomes. I am also curious about how innovation pathways fit into all this. Are there alternatives to sandboxes?

Irene Graham: The sandbox is a very essential place, in a regulated environment, to test safely with data that is live and for customer data as well. That it is undertaken in a regulatory environment, particularly when in financial services you are also looking at a regulatory environment that you have to meet. That is why sandboxes are very important. Of course you can have innovation pathways working in tandem. It is not an either/or, but we do need both and certainly in a regulated environment it is a very helpful dynamic to have. That is why what the Government are doing now on the health and energy side, as well in the broader sandbox environment, is important.

Lord Hollick: Good morning. You may have heard in the earlier session that there was considerable scepticism from the start-up companies that we met as to the value of the sandbox. They described it in a number of different ways, but essentially the cost of doing it was duplicating further costs that they would do through authorisation. These were essentially costs that their company had to bear at a very early stage of its existence. Their view was that some provisional authorisation would be appropriate and would help them. None of them referred to the fact that the sandbox would in any way help them do their funding. How do those observations sit with you?

Irene Graham: The observations on costs are all relevant to the different businesses that will be going through it, and certainly I can understand the feedback there. We can see correlation between testing a product, launching the product and then raising money. What we have seen in evidence that we have reviewed, which we will share with you, is that, for businesses that have been able to go through the sandbox, test what they are doing and get validation from that, that has helped raise further funds. That has been seen as a kitemark element to their fundraising. Therefore, I think that there is alignment to that. Clearly you are looking at a whole range of different companies, different sizes, different products. Like anything in the start-up world, some will be more successful than others. However, we certainly have seen the follow-through effect from being in the sandbox to build the product to then acquire the investment.

Lord Hollick: Do you think that provisional authorisation would help early stage companies to raise money?

Irene Graham: Provisional authorisation and what the FCA now has as a scale-up unit is a very positive step. The scale-up unit is very positive because that is also designed to help cut through the regulatory dynamics for those that are scaling up in the financial services industry. That is in tandem with what might be the evolution of ability to look at pre-authorisation, or at least a quicker pathway through to authorisation. It was always the intent to make it a quicker pathway if pre-authorisation is not one of the areas that can be focused on. It is a combination of the quicker pathway and now having a dedicated unit to scale-ups, which should help that journey through regulation.

Lord Turnbull: The institute’s report identifies 44,000 firms in the UK that are relevant to your work. These are not exclusive to fintech firms. What proportion of the 44,000 would you describe as being fintech?

Irene Graham: There are probably about 2,500 in the bucket of fintechs around the country, and growing at the moment. That is about the number. Certainly the fintech side has grown the most unicorns over time.

Lord Turnbull: Of the 2,500, how many are in or have graduated from sandboxes?

Irene Graham: I need to get the up-to-date latest figures, but there have been 200 or so businesses that have been through the sandbox. A range of them have been very successful and raised money from it. We can give you that evidence in detail, but about 200 have been through at this stage, as at the latest numbers we have.

Lord Turnbull: Of those 200, how many are products being developed by larger players and how many are small companies where the whole innovation is its purpose?

Irene Graham: The sandbox was set up for two reasons—for established businesses to look at innovation, and those that are in the early stage. Some of our fintechs are now pretty established, so they are using it themselves. It is about a 50:50 balance, if not more skewed certainly to the earlier stage. We will give you that latest figure. We can see a fairly balanced process taking shape.

Lord Turnbull: Is it your impression that the smaller start-ups, once they get into a sandbox, have a clear idea of where they go from there? At what point do they graduate or, if they are succeeding, do they just continue in the sandbox?

Irene Graham: There are a couple of things there. First, we need to recognise that there are many hundreds that apply against certain criteria and a range that is taken through. One of the changes the sandbox has made now is to not just be focused on themed cohorts, but be a continuum and open all the time. That is important for accessibility to the sandbox. That is a point that we should note as a change since the evolution of the sandbox. The FCA has been taking learnings and looking to improve accessibility to it.

Some businesses will go through one period, come back and continue and then come out the other side. Some have to test and learn what they need and they will take that back into the market. Therefore, this is very firm-specific in its usage, but you are not precluded from using it multiple times or following on.

Lord Turnbull: Are there cases of business products in the past 10 years that have graduated and become household names that have gone through the sandbox process?

Irene Graham: Yes. Zilch is one that I think is household, and Salary Finance. There are evolving products and services that a range of businesses and individuals will use. There is certainly a range in the finance world that are established names now that are being utilised regularly by consumers and business. Zilch is one of those, as an example, and Salary Finance is another, and we can provide other names to you as well.

Lord Turnbull: How much of the work of the institute is devoted to people who come to you and say, “I think of myself as a business that can be scaling up, but my application has been turned down. Can you help me”? Relative to the number of people accepted into the sandboxes, how many people are disappointed by their experience of not getting into it when they wanted to? Thirdly, did the people who did not get into it find other ways of getting to their destination?

Irene Graham: We deal with several thousand scaling businesses every year across every sector and area of the country, around a range of the issues that I have identified, whether it is access to capital and funding, which is one of the big things that we are intrinsically involved in supporting, or whether it is going global or getting the right talent in. When we hear about access to markets, we are very involved in the procurement side and some of the regulatory side. If you want specifics on the help being given to financial services businesses on the regulatory side, we are not the trade association for financial services. I think that in a subsequent session you have Innovate Finance and UK Finance. They should be looking at it from the trade association part, and you have heard from some already. We are very much focused on the macro dynamic and how we help regulators, the Bank of England, and the regulator—the FCA—evolve what is occurring from the several thousand businesses we work with and many of those in the financial services.

Lord Turnbull: Where would disappointed applicants go? Would they come to you and would you be able to say from the experience of all the other people you have dealt with, “Have you tried this? Would it be better if you did it that way?” Is that part of your service?

Irene Graham: That is a very good point about how we join up the whole country with their support services to scaling firms. We have been working across the country and the Government with Scale UK, and Innovate UK has their velocity service. At local levels we have some account management happening across the country, in Scotland, the north-east, Coventry and Warwickshire. We are trying to get much more of a local point of contact for these firms and making sure, to your point, that there is a referral on. If they are not right for the sandbox, where else do they go? Perhaps to an accelerator. You will have seen that NatWest, Barclays and other banks have set up accelerators across the UK where that is also an opportunity for the fintech community to work within those accelerators if they are not quite ready for the sandbox. There is that virtuous circle that is being evolved in the UK and many more referral processes that are taking place. That is mapped across the UK overall.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: I would like to continue the same general line of questioning. When sandboxes were first proposed, at least in the financial services area, I understood them to be for quite new ideas. However, you are telling me that accelerators now come before sandboxes. I had thought that it might be the other way round. At what stage do you go to a sandbox?

Irene Graham: I am not saying they come before or after. I was responding to the different options available to a financial services business. They can happen in tandem. You could be going into the regulatory sandbox first. There is no requirement to have one before the other. I am saying that we now have a much richer range of opportunities for financial services and fintechs to work with, of which one is the sandbox. It is not, “Go here first, then there”. It is an opportunity that is much richer now for businesses that want to scale and bring new services to market.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: Thank you. Have you any idea what the success rate is in how many apply to get into a sandbox and what percentage get in? Have you any information?

Irene Graham: We do have those numbers. To respond accurately, I can follow that up in writing, because I want to make sure we are accurate in what we have seen as the pattern.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: That would be helpful. Earlier you mentioned that going through a sandbox could give a business a kitemark and that that would help with its fundraising. That means that the sandbox confers an advantage on participating firms. What does that do to the non-participating firms, whether by choice or because they have been excluded?

Irene Graham: I would look at this in the holistic range of things. For this sector you have the sandbox, for other sectors you have Innovate UK, and there are grants for other sectors. You might have early-stage angel funding for financial services. An investor will look at a range of signals as to whether to invest: whether they already have angel funding, whether there is a level of testing of the product and support to what the marketplace could be for the product and that fits. This is an example of one of the kitemarks that can be provided. It is not by any means the only one, and ultimately a funder will take a view on the business itself.

There may be other ways that the financial services business will test its products. You may have a customer that you want, and some of the sandbox is about partnering with larger financial institutions. When you can anchor those institutions as clients, which you may have done directly with the institution, you may not need to be involved in the sandbox. Therefore, there are different ways in which kitemarks are available to financial services and different ways in which one can prove validity to a funder. This is just an example of one.

Baroness Bowles of Berkhamsted: Do you think that the sandboxes are sufficiently transparent so that what is learnt and understood through them is then shared with the wider market?

Irene Graham: The evolution of the sandbox and the way it is now open access has been good and is very important. It is fairly nascent days in one sense for the evolution of what the sandbox is doing. Publications have been made and we do our own assessment of the sandbox. There is always more information that can be put out there, and hopefully that will be forthcoming in future reports.

Lord Eatwell: I would like to go back to the origins of these institutions. They were designed to reduce regulatory risk so that you do not pour a lot of money into an innovation and then it is turned down by the regulators. If you could introduce two new measures in this country to reduce regulatory risk, what would they be?

Irene Graham: I think that the sandbox is an important element. Frankly, from a scale-up perspective, the FCA scale-up unit is a very important initiative that has been born as we seek to cut through matters. When you look at it holistically, many of our financial services scale-ups want to go global and they want to roll out their products. Therefore, ultimately, regulatory passporting is a very helpful measure by which you can speed access to international markets. That requires the international environment to support that and is an area that could be further reviewed. I am sorry, this is not FS, but, for health, regulatory passporting between multiple regulatory bodies is very helpful internally in the UK. That is equally true of the sandbox being able to support that in financial services.

Lord Lilley: I am trying to reconcile the apparent success benefit your scale-up members get from sandboxes, apparently in speeding up authorisation, with the evidence that we have just had from other companies who said that it effectively duplicates authorisation. You have to go through a long process to get on the sandbox, you go through the sandbox, and then you have to apply for authorisation. Could it be because your company is almost by definition not at the start-up phase, they have got through that and they are at a later stage introducing a second wave of innovation or something and getting approval for that?

Irene Graham: I start from a point that says that it is a choice of a business to go into a sandbox or not and to apply for it or not. Hopefully, everybody is aware of that and open to what that process will be and the cost will be. It is, like any element of this, important to recognise that it is a choice and a choice that has been made. I understand that some players may find that there is duplication. I certainly hope that through the scale-up units there will be ways in which the FCA will take learnings in that regard. However, I do not think that that is true of everyone going through the sandbox. We have to remember certain things will pass through and other things will probably fail, at the choice of business as well. It is a more nuanced dynamic than what can sometimes be portrayed.

Lord Hill of Oareford: You are clearly very positive about sandboxes. Do you think that there is any way they can be improved at all?

Irene Graham: I am positive because I come from a financial services industry that wanted to open up the financial services industry to a much broader grouping. Open banking was a critical part of that, open finance that is being looked at now—and sandboxes are another critical part of that—and choices to consumers and business. Improvements are being made as we speak, whether in the opportunity to test within the environment AI or the opportunity that has evolved on the digital side. I would like to see how, with the global environment that is part of this as well, we can speed up market access to some of our very important scaling fintechs into other markets. There is clearly the need for the transparency, which everyone is seeking to do, but also market access internationally and how that can be joined up will be very interesting going forward. We should note that some international companies have used the sandbox here, and hopefully that leads to fostering also of other businesses in the UK coming from overseas to be financial services based here.

Lord Hill of Oareford: Do you think that any other jurisdictions do sandboxes better than us?

Irene Graham: Many have learnt from us. Singapore has adopted a range of our standards and is further evolving it. We have been the leader in the financial services sandbox environment. Others have learnt from that, and smaller jurisdictions and countries sometimes can adopt things quicker.

Lord Griffiths of Fforestfach: The US does not have sandboxes, but scaling-up in the US seems very successful. We heard about Singapore today, and it seems to me that the culture between the regulators and the clients in Singapore is very much better than here. Here the culture with the regulator is clearly a lack of trust between them. What are your comments on these facts? They look to me like facts.

Irene Graham: One of the reasons that the US is far quicker in its scale-up journey or has larger numbers, or its velocity, is down to growth capital and the size of capital that it can deploy into the marketplace to support companies going through scale-up journey and building global businesses. There is that depth of capital that is very different to here. One of the things we are trying to unlock is the institutional capital available to these firms.

I have lived in Singapore and it is a much smaller marketplace than the UK, so adoption and agility can be more flexible. As I say, the appointment of the regulatory scale-up unit in the FCA is a very positive step forward that should see more alignment in the relationships with our scaling financial services firms. It is a combination of elements for the UK, and the FCA scale-up unit is a very important complementary part of the sandbox.

Lord Griffiths of Fforestfach: One final question. You have a lot of experience of regulation, having been with Standard Chartered and so on, and in the world of banking. How do you feel about the world that you are now in with scale-ups compared to the Bank of England as the regulator? Are there any contrasts that you would make and lessons that might be learnt?

Irene Graham: Thank you. I should have said on the last point that the other thing we see happening more in the US is what we call corporate venturing or corporate collaboration and acquisition. Those elements play into how you can build a collaboration environment for our financial services opportunities. We need to recognise that in the Bank of England, the FCA, the prudential regulator, our larger banks and the banks coming through. One of the things that I learnt at Standard Chartered was what one might call now intrapreneurship, the ability to be entrepreneurial inside a very large organisation. We have led the way in open banking, and we should recognise that and not ignore it. That was a combination of regulatory, industry, fintech and government. We have led the way in sandbox.

We need to build in our regulators—and I do see it occurring—what I might call intrapreneurship. That means that you get units within the larger organisation, as you have with the sandbox and now with the scale unit, that can be far more leaning in towards the entrepreneurial innovation and help speed that through the larger remits that many of these organisations have. Therefore, that entrepreneurship is important. I see that in the regulatory environment today and encourage that. I also think that the opening up of data is very important and one of the things that we have led the way on.

Lord Davies of Brixton: It seems that there are two routes into a regulatory sandbox. There is where the innovator seeks judgment, but there is also where the regulator is setting up a panel and there they pick who takes part. There was discussion on that in the previous session. Does that mean that in some ways that works against innovation—that some people who have bright ideas are excluded from the process? Is this a serious problem?

Irene Graham: When you look at innovation in its various forms, you often have challenges laid out that bring in players who can seek to be the experts who might want to take on that challenge, and hackathons. There are many routes through which you can create innovation, whether that is the applicant applying itself and the business applying itself, or a challenge put out there to the market, which a range of players can seek to be engaged with. There is benefit in both, and having both and having multiple-access environments is a useful thing.

Lord Davies of Brixton: We are talking here about the FCA deciding who goes into a panel. Is there a danger that it is trying to pick winners, a job for which it is not suited?

Irene Graham: There is a very thorough assessment process. It is an open application. Businesses put their application in. Ultimately a choice will be made of who comes into a cohort and who does not. It does not mean that you cannot come back in and seek to use the sandbox in other ways or apply again for a new challenge. I do not think that that is very different from any open challenge and a selection process where everybody has the same application process. Ultimately a decision will be made on the merits of the application and what the applicant has laid out as their stall as to why they should be the ones included.

Baroness Donaghy: In your annual report, you indicate that support for scale-ups needed to be better co-ordinated, with more proactive referrals, use of data to fast-track firms to relevant resources, and that your organisation is providing a concierge service. Your mood music during your contributions has been quite favourable to the FCA’s activities. You have talked a couple of times about the unit that it has set up especially for scale-ups. You have also said that it has improved accessibility to sandboxes. Would you elaborate more on the favourable side of what the FCA is trying to do to help the organisations that you are acting as the concierge for?

Irene Graham: On what the FCA is seeking to do, it used to be that the sandbox was open only to cohorts of businesses but now it has open access all the time. That is already a barrier removed and that is available. To your point, what we talk about is account management and relationship management of these firms. That is where the scale unit means that you have an active account manager, a relationship manager for a range of our financial services firm scale-ups and who are seeking to scale. That is a dedicated unit and that is what we call account management of that unit.

With the concierge service, we are talking about how local government and government and private sector play that account management role. For example, you will see now that many of the major banks have innovation banking and high-growth teams that are supporting our particular scale-ups across sectors into funding and market opportunities. We are seeing the evolution of account management for our scale-up businesses, which is no different to account-managing global corporates, but how do we make sure that that is available to our scale-ups or the established banks? That scale-up unit in the FCA means that they have direct access quickly to resources in the regulator to support the scale-up journey.

Baroness Donaghy: What about the issue of the amount of time it takes? You have indicated that it would be helpful if it was fast-tracked. What would you say was a reasonable amount of time? The firms you are dealing with are still comparatively small, are they not?

Irene Graham: No, they are small and large when you look at the scale. It is very specific. It goes from small to mid-cap to unicorns, so we are talking about a whole gamut of scale-ups. The business is growing at 20% or more employment turnover, so that is going through that range. We need to get the right services at each of the different stages of the scale-up journey. We look at that whole community of scale-ups and how they are supported overall in the areas of challenge and need.

When we talk in the ScaleUp Institute about fast-tracking, we mean the ability, if there is a proven service that has gone through a testing—it could even be with Innovate UK and other sectors. Remember that we are talking about all the sectors here. If you have proven the viability of a product, how do we then fast-track it across the system for it to be available to consumers, or business in this case, or available on the National Health Service if it is in the health space? When we talk about fast-tracking, it is how you fast-track through into procurement and commercialisation processes and how regulation can facilitate that. It is about entry into market access. The NHS is an example of that when you look at the broader sectors of, for example, life sciences. For financial services it is how you can bring it to market, how it can be further embedded if it is a collaborative service with larger institutions, how we highlight those that are doing well in that sandbox and how we connect up with where the service that they have meets the marketplace to commercialise it.

Baroness Donaghy: One of the fintech witnesses said that they had hoped that there would be a link between the exercise carried out within the sandbox and the subsequent formal application, and that it was frustrating that this did not seem to happen. I may have misunderstood you, but you indicated there that there is a link, in your experience.

Irene Graham: What I am saying is that taking the learning from the particular example given and the fact the FCA has this year established the scale-up unit, I hope that that link has now improved. When we talk about fast-tracking, that is the type of link we want to see.

Lord Smith of Kelvin: I asked earlier witnesses about competition. I was worried about small businesses developing a brilliant idea, going through the sandbox procedure and then that things might leak out from that and competitors say, “That is a great idea; let’s batter them in the marketplace”. Can I ask you the exact opposite question? Are sandboxes sufficiently transparent? Are lessons shared for the benefit of the wider market?

Irene Graham: I think, Lord Kelvin, that there are views on whether it is openly shared in the market, and there could be improvement there. We have to recognise that in the sandbox environment you are using at times, as agreed in a regulatory framework, live data or consumer data. It is important that there is the relevance of what is aggregated and reported out and what is perhaps competitively sensitive and therefore is not as widely shared or individually sensitive. That is the balance between how you in aggregate take out the lessons learnt and put insights out in the market, while recognising that some of this will be specific to the company and competitive or to any of the data that might be being used.

Lord Smith of Kelvin: Can I just say, by the way, that Lord Kelvin was a brilliant man, the River Kelvin is a wee river that runs through Glasgow, and if you are a Smith you have to say “of” somewhere.

Irene Graham: I am sorry.

Lord Smith of Kelvin: What would you change about the system that we have with sandboxes?

Irene Graham: I think that we need to bed down the system that we have and it is important that we retain what we have today. There have already been developments in that system and I have highlighted some of those: the digital aspects; the AI aspect that is being worked with NVIDIA now, which is a new angle to it; and the fact the scale unit will be and is being and has been created in the FCA. We are continually evolving it and that continues to be a journey that we are on. Most importantly, we should retain it, seek to evolve it, as is being done, and make sure that the linkage between testing, authorisation and commercialisation into market is as smooth as possible.

The Chair: Thank you, Ms Graham. That has been a very interesting session. You very generously said at various points that you would provide further information and evidence of how the benefits have worked out in practice for those who have been through sandboxes. I think I am right in saying that there has not been a unicorn created via a sandbox yet, but it would be helpful, when you do pull together those that have benefited successfully, to have something that demonstrates the scale of their success, even if they have not quite got to unicorn status.

Irene Graham: They may not yet. There might be unicorns that are now using it, though, that have come through from other reasons of how they have scaled and are now able to use it. We will give you that evidence.

The Chair: Excellent. Thank you very much for your time.
