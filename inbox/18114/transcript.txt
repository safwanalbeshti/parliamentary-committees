Q399 Chair: In the draft Heathrow expansion NPS, the Government set out four tests that must be met before expansion can proceed. Are these the right tests from the perspective of communities that are or would be affected by expansion? Is the threshold clear and set at the right level for determining whether each test has been met?

Deborah Petty: I reread the HENPS yesterday and have to say it was a thoroughly depressing read from a community perspective. It is the only potential protection that communities have from a permanent change to how they live and what they experience in terms of noise, particulates and pollution. I feel the HENPS falls woefully short. It is quite a partial document. The language is quite woolly, as other people have said, which gives room for manoeuvre.

We are very concerned that communities are potentially going to get thrown under the bus because of the national growth infrastructure designation. At every turn in the document, the Secretary of State could just give it the green light anyway if it is in the overriding public interest.

There are six quick points I would like to make. First, it is completely toothless. There are few hard and fast targets and absolutely no sanctions or penalties for failing to meet them. Secondly, it needs to have stronger independent assessments and oversight. When I first read it I thought, “Gosh, the promoters are being allowed to both report on and mark their own homework too much.” When I reread it yesterday I thought, “Blimey, they’re actually setting their own homework.” The parameters with how things are described, “Take reasonable steps,” “As much as reasonably practicable”, “try to,” essentially allow the promoters to say what they can do and get measured against that, and that is not acceptable.

There are very few specific targets. There is one on mode share, the 5,000 apprenticeships and the three-year subsidy for five new domestic routes. If the main purpose is growth and jobs, there are no metrics on those. It has a very fragmented and confusing oversight structure and there needs to be a much better delivery body that communities should be represented on.

As far as emissions—combining air quality and climate for speed—other more expert people have outlined that there are few protections on the particulates.

Chair: We have heard from them.

Deborah Petty: PM2s and UFPs in particular are not covered, but just because they are not measured does not mean the harm is not there. In fact you could argue that everybody around Heathrow is going to be subjected to a long-term medical experiment that they have not signed up to because we do not sufficiently know what the harms of ultrafine particles are at the moment.

The unaccounted carbon cost for us is a scandal. From a community and local business point of view—all the people paying ULEZ and congestion charges and sharing the pain of transitioning to green vehicles—for aviation to be given a green light to just say, “Knock yourselves out,” and undo all that good work at the stroke of a pen for minimal if any GDP gain is really not acceptable.

Moving on to noise, there is much to be said, so I hope we can return to that.

Chair: We will.

Deborah Petty: The big thing that is missing from the HENPS is noise change metrics. There is absolutely nothing that limits the amount of noise change that people can be subject to and nothing about the intensity with which routes can be flown or the number of noise events. In the 2014 events of the trial on the concentrated flight path, the change was what brought people out waving placards. That is what makes people really sit up and take notice.

The communities deserve that the best data is used in the HENPS. At the moment the ANAS and ANNE studies have been delayed and therefore excluded. If they are taken into account, the inconvenient truth is that the airport has a much larger impact on noise. Rather than 400,000 people it is 4.65 million. In freedom of information requests to the DFT, 1.9 million are set to see their noise increase by 25% to 100% or more. Some 400,000 people could potentially see their noise increase by 100% or more.

Q400 Chair: What is the source of those figures?

Deborah Petty: DFT, freedom of information request. It could be from a low base of noise exposure or an already high base exposure to something catastrophic. People have a right to know what they are in for. There is no protection in the HENPS for that.

Quickly on growth, for minimal GDP growth there are not enough protections on the financial viability of it. There is nothing that says there needs to be any debt control or liquidity buffers or anything like that. If the cost is as it is now, say for the Heathrow promoter scheme, the airlines have already said they will not use it; it is too expensive. There is a genuine risk that we end up building a hugely expensive white elephant at great expense and disruption.

It says that on a couple of metrics, noise should be no worse in future than it was in 2024.

There are three problems with that. One is that in 2024 the noise was already horrendous, affecting 4.65 million people on the Government’s own appraisal. Of those about half a million were, “Highly annoyed,” which are the precise words in the appraisal. Just maintaining that level of harm really is not good enough. What the noise appraisal also reveals is that the runway in the middle—the existing northern runway—will not operate in mixed mode for safety reasons. In total there will be arrivals on the southern runway for three quarters of the time and on the new runway for three quarters of the time. That is our deduction.
