Surinder Arora: If anything, Heathrow expansion would support the other regional airports from a connectivity point of view. Q487 Jacob Collier: We have heard that point about affordability and deliverability from airlines, and you have stressed it today. What do you think are the biggest risks to your scheme, and what are the biggest advantages compared with the other proposal?

We do not go out there and dictate, “This is the length of the runway we are going to do. If you start building and you are not going to deliver until at least early 2040, billions will go in—we worked out that the cost for those 11 years is more than £10 billion. That is not even spoken about. Although people say, “Oh, it’s no cost to the taxpayer,” the actual payer is the taxpayer. People who use the airport are taxpayers—most of them are—and they are paying for it. Hence we have come up with a scheme that meets the Government’s timescale, is affordable and is deliverable.

We do not want to ban narrow-body aircraft. We want the connectivity to regional and other airports around Europe. Forty per cent of the business mix today is wide-body aircraft.

The aircrafts are getting more efficient, even from an environmental point of view and a noise point of view. If you look at the numbers, the noise levels have gone down over the past decade. When we worked on the scheme pre covid, our customers—the airlines—said the optimum size was 3.2 km, and yet four or five years later, they said that actually the optimum size today is 2.8 km, so what will happen in one, two or three decades? Carlton Brown: The other thing is that, with a smaller scheme, you have less land take, less impacts and less environmental impacts more generally. In summary, it is cheaper, it is quicker and it has less impact, so it delivers growth quicker.
