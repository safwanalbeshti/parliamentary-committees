Chair: That is noted. I will begin by asking about the global economic picture. What is your assessment of how the current economic conditions in the UK affect export-orientated industries such as Scotch whisky?

Will Haworth: It is difficult. If you are a UK exporting business, the current climate is not particularly forgiving. You have a global trade policy landscape that is becoming more unilateral, fragmented and transactional. You have a range of conflicts, in which the UK has little to no influence in terms of determining the outcome, that are impacting supply chains, energy prices and the prices of goods.

For a country such as the UK, where roughly two-thirds of GDP comes via international trade, changes in this environment are incredibly significant. For a sector such as Scotch, which is even more export-oriented, restrictions such as tariffs and technical barriers to trade behind the border are incredibly damaging. They can have long-lasting effects, long beyond the length of the tariffs themselves. The US is a very good case study of that. Businesses like that really need a reliable, consistent environment, which they are not getting at the moment.

Dr Sampson: When I am looking at the global economy at the moment, the two biggest shocks we need to keep in mind are, on the one hand, the high energy prices, and, on the other, one bright spot for the global economy recently, which is the boom in all trade that is in some way related to AI—all the hardware that is needed for the AI buildout. The consequence of those two shocks is that we have a bifurcated global economy. Countries that are either energy suppliers or suppliers of AI hardware are growing relatively strongly because there is strong demand for what they are producing. Other countries that are energy importers and are exposed to the high energy prices and do not supply AI hardware are struggling. Unfortunately, the UK falls more into that latter category of countries that are growing relatively slowly and, as Will said, facing a very challenging economic environment at the moment.

Professor Khorana: I will give some numbers here to provide context. In my opinion, the global outlook is very challenging. It is a very uneven kind of landscape that countries are operating in. I say this because, when we look at the numbers, we see that global GDP is around 3% and expected to be 3.4% next year. Inflation rates are quite high comparatively, at around +4%. Having said this, world trade has grown. As you rightly mentioned, there has been a boom in AI and energy exports.

Given that Scotch whisky is an export-oriented product, which is hugely exposed to the fluctuations globally, and given that the trade landscape is fragmented and global supply chains are under stress, the Scotch whisky sector has not done as well as it should have. If we put this into the UK context, we see that the GDP of the UK is 1.2% and expected to go down to 1% next year—these are all IMF estimates that I am giving. If we start looking at the business investment outlook, we see that its growth is around 0% at the moment and expected to be -0.5%. This clearly tells us that the business climate they are navigating is hugely challenging and can have a hugely detrimental impact on a sector that is hugely linked to exports.

Scotch whisky as a sector is facing demand and supply-side challenges, as you have rightly identified. I would go back and say, in addition to that, there are demand and supply-side challenges because the pattern of consumption is also changing. Given that economies are slowing down, the disposable income levels of consumers is also lower. There has been a global shift in the consumption pattern, plus high energy costs, trade fragmentation, geopolitical conflict and increasing transport costs. They are all making the landscape extremely difficult to operate in.

Chair: If we look forward, what are the coming risks that we might want to look out for? What might be coming up in the future that we should be looking out for?

Professor Khorana: The most important risk we should be looking out for is to ensure that businesses continue to invest. The risk is that businesses may not invest as much, given that the sentiment is quite low generally within businesses.

Risks that we can see down the line include high energy costs. In the UK, energy costs are 63% higher compared with the average median. If energy costs continue to rise, and given that Scotch whisky is quite an energy-intensive sector, there are going to be risks to competitiveness for the industry.

Chair: Dr Sampson, are there any other risks you think are coming?

Dr Sampson: Because the Scotch whisky industry is so dependent on global demand, anything that leads to a downturn in the global economy is a big risk factor for the industry. There are two potential downside risks that I would point to there. One would be any escalation of the conflicts between Russia and Ukraine or in the middle east. That would be an obvious risk. The other one would be if the current AI boom is a bubble that pops at some stage and leads to a global downturn. In both those cases you would see declines in global demand, and that would reduce Scotch whisky exports.

Will Haworth: There is an enormous question about where global trade and economic policy go in the next five to 10 years. US tariffs and the approach that President Trump has taken on US trade and tariff policy take a lot of headlines, and rightly so, but you do not need to look very far away from the UK to see other countries and jurisdictions really putting up other types of barriers. For example, the EU’s Made in Europe initiative is a brazen and very open attempt to bring back supply, bring manufacturing back into the EU and make public support contingent on manufacturing within the EU.

All countries and jurisdictions are fundamentally looking for ways to build up their economies and reduce exposure to risks such as the China shock. We are likely to see in the future an increasing proliferation of measures like this that look to protect domestic manufacturers. It is not deglobalisation—that word gets thrown around quite a lot. I think that we are going to see a shortening of trade relationships between more closely aligned geopolitical blocs, rather than the large-scale globalisation that has existed, arguably, since the end of the cold war.

Dave Doogan: On that point, Mr Haworth, what is the impetus behind that? Is it about creating economic growth and employment and reducing some of the social cost challenges within jurisdictions such as the EU, or is it about reshoring manufacturing capacity and having manufacturing capacity at a level that would allow that manufacturing capacity to pivot to something else in extremis, such as defence?

Will Haworth: It is all the things you just mentioned, if I am totally honest. The west in particular has, for a number of years, relied on cheap inputs, commodities and goods, predominantly from China but also from other global economies. For many years, that was a fantastic thing. Consumers and businesses had access to cheap goods and inputs that they could then turn into expensive, high-end products.

That global dynamic is now shifting. China is the obvious case study. Look at the way the Chinese automotive sector is now keeping track with, if not outpacing, most western automotive sectors. Large multinational companies that have, for decades, relied on those cheap inputs to make a better product are no longer able to do that. Jobs are at risk. The cost of those products is now higher than those of competitors. The political response, but also the economic response, is often to ask, “How can I stop that happening? How can I bring that impetus back into my home jurisdiction to create the jobs and investment that we need?”

Kenneth Stevenson: You mentioned energy costs. The whisky firms are not stupid, so I am assuming that they would have seen this coming or would have seen a gradual progression and a rise in prices. They must have moved towards being an electric economy at some point. What is holding them back from being an electric economy and maybe having a wind turbine or solar panels? Is it connections from NESO, or have they just not moved quickly enough with what has happened with the energy sector?

Will Haworth: Is that directed to me or anybody in particular?

Kenneth Stevenson: It is to everyone. You brought it up and mentioned it. I thought, “Whisky companies are large corporations. They must see what is happening.” Obviously, the energy prices are hurting them. I know companies that have moved to have their own wind turbines or solar panels to help their energy supply. Do you know why they have not done that, or maybe they have but just not been quick enough? Do they have problems with NESO or with connections from the grid? That would obviously help to cut their costs.

Chair: That question might be one that our second panel could very usefully explore.

Kenneth Stevenson: Yes, it could be.

Will Haworth: I would not be able to answer with huge amount of accuracy. The UK’s energy problems are down to a range of factors, including international factors such as the conflicts in the middle east and Ukraine. There are also domestic factors. The speed of grid connections is a persistent issue across all sectors in the UK. I would not be able to discuss in detail why the Scotch sector has not cottoned on.

Douglas Lumsden: Professor Khorana, you mentioned that energy costs are 63% higher in the UK. Why is that? Why are our energy prices so much higher than those in the rest of the world?

Professor Khorana: I am not an energy expert. As I understand and as I have been reading, the infrastructure seems to be quite old. There are issues with the grid. Scotch whisky consumes a lot of energy in distilling, as well as in glass manufacturing, for example. Given that a lot of energy is being consumed, prices have gone up as a result of the shock in the middle east, the lack of infrastructure upgrades, and grid inefficiencies. All this has contributed to higher energy prices. The Government have a role to play here.

Douglas Lumsden: I am trying to work out why we are an outlier almost, with prices so much higher than in the rest of the world, but maybe that is a question for another panel, Chair.

Chair: Yes, possibly.

John Cooper: The picture you are painting is a difficult one. Globally, there are lots of shocks and storms. Scotch whisky feels a little bit sometimes like a cork in a storm-tossed sea. It is not really in charge of events. Having said all that, it is a unique product. It can only be produced in Scotland. Global demand in many places is dipping, but India is an outlier. The Indian demand is absolutely enormous.

Professor, you touched on the fact that you thought that this is a moment for investment. That might be counterintuitive to a lot of people. People might think that, when facing shocks like this, it is best to bring the horns in and not spend money. You think the opposite: the companies should be doubling down as best they can.

Professor Khorana: If you look at the patterns of how trade has evolved and between different types of Scotch whisky, you will see that trade with India has increased hugely. It is not in single malt. If you break it up, you see that exports in the single malt category have not really increased as much. If I recall correctly, the increase has actually happened in bulk-blended malt and bottle-blended malt.

In India, we see a very interesting dynamic of growth not in the premium brands but in volume. In terms of value, trade has not increased as much. The same holds for Turkey. If you look at America, you find that, in terms of value, trade has dipped. It is a very interesting dynamic we are seeing.

John Cooper: It is very dynamic; “volatile” would be the word I would use. What do you think Scotch whisky should do about that? Should it be looking at areas such as India and trying to grow the higher end of the market? Is there something in that?

Professor Khorana: Absolutely, yes. This is a point when a lot of emphasis has to be placed on doing the right kind of promotion, especially for high-value blends. In India, the demand is for the lower value, so a lot of promotion has to be done. We know that, if there is no investment, the returns are going to be very limited. The more you invest, the more you get.

John Cooper: You would argue that that investment has to come in the marketing at that higher end.

Professor Khorana: Yes. The Government have a very important role to play here. Access to finance should be enabled for businesses in the Scotch whisky sector. If you look at the British Chambers of Commerce survey that has been done, we find that smaller Scotch whiskies, especially MSMEs that are involved in the lower end of the supply chain in Scotch whisky production, are the ones that have been hugely impacted as a result of this downturn.

John Cooper: This is a common story in Britain. We have lots of start-ups, but, when we go to scale-ups, we struggle. It is access to finance that we bump against all the time. So many companies talk about a valley of death, where they reach a point where they are just big enough and they need capital to grow further, but in fact they cannot access that and dip down at that point. Do you think Scotch is caught in that?

Professor Khorana: Yes, it is.

John Cooper: Mr Haworth, you talked about Europe. We have just been talking there about the potential in India and that is possible because of the new deal that has been done with India. Is there a concern that closer relations with the EU and the new SPS—sanitary and phytosanitary—deal that is coming in might impact on trade deals around the world?

We are in things such as the CPTPP. I keep saying this: we have to come up with a better name than CPTPP. It is easy enough with AI to come up with something that is easier for me to pronounce. That CPTPP deal is a bloc of countries around the Pacific rim. There has never really been a trade deal like that. There has never really been a bloc like that. That offers tremendous opportunities there. Is that likely to be impacted if we were to do more deals with the EU?

Will Haworth: That is slightly a “how long is a piece of string” question. The SPS deal that is currently envisaged as part of the upcoming UK-EU summit is not super-extensive. It does not take us back to what the UK’s relationship was when it was inside the EU immediately. It commits the UK to what is called dynamic alignment, which means that, over time, the UK agrees and commits to maintain like standards with EU standards to allow the frictionless movement of those goods across the border. It does not necessarily mean that you have to adapt those EU regulations word for word.

It creates obvious issues. The one that always comes up is US dissatisfaction with UK food standards in relation to things such as chlorine-treated chicken and hormone-treated beef. Those products have not been allowed in the UK or EU for decades. I do not expect that the UK Government are going to walk into a closer relationship with Europe at the expense of what are obvious growth opportunities in other markets. India is a fantastic example. There are lots of others in south-east Asia. There are also lots of others in South America. The Mercosur customs union is another one that the UK is looking at and that the EU itself has just signed a free trade agreement with, which is in force as of this year.

There is a risk. I do not think that, in the immediate to near term, it is an open risk that will immediately prohibit the UK from expanding its trade policy beyond the EU though.

John Cooper: Dr Sampson, what are your thoughts on that? That sounds like we could have it all. Scotch is heavily export-reliant. Can we manage to export to the EU and align with them while also talking to America, south-east Asia and India?

Dr Sampson: My reading of the SPS deal that is under negotiation with the EU is that it is not going to have direct impact on the Scotch industry. Scotch is, for the most part, outside the scope of what is being negotiated there. It might benefit on the margins from some reductions in the frictions at the border, so there might be a slight boost to Scotch trade with the EU because of that.

The bigger question, which is what Will was discussing, is if we go beyond the SPS deal and there is a bigger movement back towards closer relations with the EU. How that would affect our trade relations with the rest of the world? The key question there is whether we end up at some point re-entering a customs union with the European Union. If we do, at that point we will become part of the EU’s common external trade policy, which means we give up our own independent trade deals and inherit all the trade deals the EU has.

There are going to be trade-offs there. We were talking about India. Both the UK and the EU have a trade deal with India, so it probably does not make a huge difference there, though I think the EU deal reduces tariffs down to 40% more quickly than the UK deal. Likewise, the EU currently has a deal with Mercosur, which the UK does not. It is a complicated trade-off, and it is not entirely clear that inheriting the EU’s deals necessarily would leave the Scotch industry worse off. It depends; it is going to differ market by market.

John Cooper: There are many unknown unknowns then.

Dr Sampson: Yes.

Dave Doogan: Professor Khorana, you highlighted earlier some of the significant changes in consumer behaviour with Scotch whisky. Could you elaborate a little on them, in so far as you can see the future any better than the rest of us can? Can you elaborate on what that horizon looks like and what the Scotch whisky industry is trying to reconcile about what may or may not happen? I was particularly interested to hear about, and would like you to expand on, the value end of the product—the high value but lower volume of single malt, versus the more substantive product of blended Scotch. How might that pan out over the coming decade, say—subject to global events, of course—which is what we are driving at?

Professor Khorana: I have been noticing that there has been a general change and shift in consumer pattern when it comes to consumption of Scotch whisky. It may be triggered as a result of the campaigns that are run about consumption of alcohol. I really do not know what the underlying factor is. Is it as a result of disposable income? I guess that it is a combination of these factors.

If you look at how the drinks sector is performing, you will see that there is an increase in the sales of no-alcohol or zero-alcohol drinks at the moment. We also see a shift from drinking neat whisky to using whisky in cocktails. I hope I have been able to give you an answer about the changing pattern of consumer habits. It has essentially been driven by this.

Dave Doogan: Dr Sampson, when it comes to the international market for Scotch, Scotch is always exported into a number of really important markets, such as Japan, India and the United States, which have a domestic industry market, but they are not Scotch, so that is a different thing. They are not single malt, so that is a different thing. Interestingly, there is going to be English single malt coming on the international market quite soon. How much of a threat to Scotch is that? How confident are you in the international market’s ability to differentiate between Scotch single malt and English single malt, which will both have a UK provenance? How big a threat would that be to the Scotch whisky industry?

Dr Sampson: That is a really important question going forward. Scotch is a very strong brand that is known around the world, and that helps consumers to differentiate the products that the Scotch whisky industry is offering from whiskies coming from other countries, including whiskies from England. It is in a strong position in terms of the brand equity and the recognition that is there.

At the same time, if we look over the last decade, Scotch’s share of global whisky trade has been falling—only slightly but it has declined. To put numbers on that, it is down from 54% in 2016 to 51% in 2025. Global markets have substituted away from what is coming from the Scotch whisky industry over the past decade to a small extent. That indicates that there is that competition there.

Consumers do not necessarily see Scotch as being one product and other types of whiskies as separate products. They are considering, “Should I buy Scotch, bourbon or Japanese whisky?” The Scotch whisky industry is clearly the market leader there, but it has been losing its sales shares, and that is a concern. If that trend continues, that is clearly a concern for the industry. It is something that particularly the marketing and branding teams, in conjunction with the UK Government, will need to focus on.

Dave Doogan: Mr Haworth, given what Dr Sampson has just said and what we have heard already this morning, what are the priorities for the industry beyond just binary growth? We understand the growth imperative. In terms of accessing markets, product positioning and the desirability that surrounds premium Scotch in particular, and the effect that that has on volume Scotch by association, what is the future looking like? What should the industry be pursuing in terms of that global growth in quite uncertain economic times?

Will Haworth: We know, and we discussed earlier, that the Scotch sector relies on export and global trade for 80% to 90% of its performance. In a very blunt way, the more access that Scotch and Scotch distillers have to international markets, the better position they are in. If we look at the UK’s trade policy at the moment, it has signed a number of free trade agreements post its EU exit. India is a great example. CPTPP we have mentioned. The Gulf Co-operation Council is another one that was signed this year and is yet to enter into force. There are other markets that the UK is considering, such as Indonesia, the Philippines, the UAE on a bilateral basis and Uruguay. That is in a recent consultation that the Government released, seeking input from businesses.

Those are positive things. It is good that the UK is looking to continue to expand its trade policy and market access for businesses, including the Scotch sector. There are a number of different drawbacks or difficulties with that approach. The first is that it is not just the question of, in absolute terms, what the UK’s market access is to these markets. It is also what it is relative to its competitors. For all those markets that I have just listed—bar, I think, Uruguay—the EU either already has a free trade agreement or is pursuing a free trade agreement. On Mercosur, where the EU already has a free trade agreement, the UK is in no position to negotiate actively at the moment.

Already there is a risk that the UK is falling behind its competitors with regards to market access to big growth markets, particularly in South America and the APAC region. In my view, the Scotch sector should be pushing for the UK to be moving ahead as quickly as possible on this, securing terms that are, at a minimum, a replica and match the terms that competitors such as the EU have achieved. Beyond the tariffs, which are very important, agreements that address other things that are technical barriers to trade, so health certification and labelling requirements, often fly under the radar when we are having a discussion about global trade. They can be as important, if not more important, than the tariffs themselves.

Dave Doogan: Very briefly, because I know others want to come in on this, none of us can see into the future and none of us knew what was going to happen years ago. I am very interested to hear about progress you said the EU has made in terms of repositioning itself in the global market in the past decade, with events. The UK has, of course, struck trade deals since Brexit as well. On the net position specific to Scotch whisky, if the UK was still in the EU, would it be in a better international trade position than it is now, having come out of the EU and struck its own trade deals in various different jurisdictions? Is the net position positive or negative on trade alone?

Will Haworth: You would have to look at the numbers for that. On the surface, it currently appears so. It appears that, if the UK had remained in the EU, it would be in a better net position vis-à-vis access to the global markets that we have been speaking about for the Scotch sector.

Mr MacDonald: Dr Sampson, you said something about Scotch as a percentage of the world market dropping from 54% to 51%. There are distilleries in Scotland that sell millions of litres of raw spirit to overseas whisky companies, which they then mature themselves. Do you think that that is something that is damaging to the Scotch whisky industry?

Dr Sampson: That is a tough question to provide a definitive answer to. It is certainly important for the continued ability of the Scotch industry to sell premium products at premium prices that there is a clear distinction between Scotch that is considered premium Scotch and bulk Scotch. The whisky companies are aware of the importance of maintaining this distinction and not cannibalising their own sales by providing cheaper bulk exports that could substitute for the premium products, but it clearly is a consideration that needs to be taken into account. You want consumers to view premium Scotch as a separate product from bulk Scotch or Scotch that is exported and turned into other whisky products overseas. They have done a reasonably good job of that so far, but it is something that you need to be aware of on an ongoing basis.

Mr MacDonald: We are talking about economic opportunities for the Scotch whisky industry. Mr Haworth, what countries offer the biggest opportunity over, say, a five or 10-year horizon, for the Scotch whisky industry if you were running one of the major companies?

Will Haworth: With company colleagues of mine, we have done some quite deep analysis on this. The one that stands out by some distance is the UAE. That is possibly surprising, given the cultural sensitivities towards alcohol there. The international dimension of its inhabitants and its place as a business hub in the region means that demand for alcohol, and premium alcohol such as Scotch, is very high. Our modelling would estimate that in an FTA with the UAE, where there is currently a 50% tariff on Scotch, if that tariff were to be removed immediately, over a period up to roughly 2040 you are looking at possibly growth of over £1 billion for the UK sector in revenue, and hundreds of jobs.

Mercosur, which is the South America customs union bloc that I mentioned, is another significant market. It is slightly different in terms of its consumer base and the tastes over there. That is another market where we would estimate there to be an opportunity of hundreds of millions of pounds, if not closing on £1 billion.

There is a difficult question here. Indonesia, for example, is an interesting case study because official statistics would show a very low level of alcohol consumption for cultural reasons. Actually, it is quite well known that the rate of illicit alcohol consumption is extremely high. Therefore, the unofficial statistics, the unofficial numbers and therefore the unofficial growth opportunity are a lot higher as well. If I had to pick out a small number of markets, those would probably be the three at present.

Professor Khorana: The other growth markets, but not in high-value Scotch, are India and Turkey, because there we have seen a substantial growth over the years. We know that the UK has a trade deal with India. Interestingly enough, we know that tariffs were as high as 150%. They have gone down to 75% after the implementation of the trade deal with India and they will be reduced, phased over the next few years, to 40%. We should not rejoice that a reduction in tariffs per se will lead to an increase in market access. We have to look at country-specific factors that have an impact on the growth potential of the Scotch whisky industry.

To put this into context, if you look at India there are a combination of factors that continue to have an impact on Scotch whisky exports to India. First, there are regulations from the central Government. The Scotch whisky industry has to adhere to those. In addition to that, there are also state-level levies and excise duties that exist, and they vary between different states. That adds on an additional layer of burden. In addition to that, there are pricing and distribution costs that have an impact. Finally, there are the licensing regulations.

It is a host of other factors that are sitting behind the border that have an impact. These cannot be addressed through the FTA. They have to be done at a bilateral level. This is where the Government have to play a very important role in making sure that the implementation of the trade deal is done in a manner that supports the Scotch whisky industry.

Mr MacDonald: There is a second question as part of this. Alcohol duty, especially in the UK, is very high for Scotch. Do you have any economic evidence to support the fact that, if it was cut, it would actually result in a higher tax take?

Professor Khorana: I am afraid that I have not done any number-crunching on that so far.

Dr Sampson: No, I do not have any numbers on that, sorry.

Chair: Mr Haworth, you were talking about countries where it would be important for whisky to become more established or to trade more. It was the UAE, the South American bloc and what was the third one?

Will Haworth: It was Indonesia.

Kirsteen Sullivan: Continuing with tariffs, I wonder whether we can talk about the experience of US tariffs. The tariffs came in in 2019, were suspended in 2021, came back again as part of the liberation day tariffs in 2025 and then came off again in 2026. There is clearly a lot of uncertainty and change, which must make it quite difficult for companies when they are looking at investment decisions. I wondered whether you could give me a bit of insight into what you think we can learn from the experience of those US tariffs and how that uncertainty can create additional challenges for exporters.

Will Haworth: The US trade posture, as you say, has been incredibly uncertain. It makes it very difficult for investors in any sector. The Scotch sector is actually one that has not got away with it, but has probably been slightly better treated. President Trump removed Scotch tariffs as a result of the state visit. He was very clear that that was a favour to the King, rather than any liking for UK trade policy, but it is, for whatever reason, a net benefit.

There are a number of important reasons, and I will start with the context of US trade policy. I do not think that we should expect US trade policy to change significantly from what it is now. I do not think that the midterm results significantly change US trade policy. They certainly do not change the President’s ability to exercise authority over the imposition of tariffs. From a broader political perspective, there is no real bipartisan consensus within the US for a return to global open markets. If you look at the actions of President Biden—arguably very different from President Trump in much of his politics—he actually maintained a number of Trump first-term era tariffs, mostly on Chinese goods. Those 2019 tariffs that you were talking about there came in under him as well.

If you are a Scotch exporter—or any exporter, frankly—looking at the US, the baseline expectation has to be that market access will come at a higher cost for the near future. The lesson to be learned from that—and this is where the EU has pivoted very quickly in some regards—is that, if that is going to be a more difficult market for you to access, how do you diversify market access opportunities for your businesses away from that? That is where this stream of free trade agreements, some of which admittedly have been many years in the making, has come from. For the UK, as I said earlier, looking at a proactive trade policy in response to a US that is going to be harder and more expensive to access, maintains and gives you that flexibility and your businesses the opportunity to access markets to offset the higher cost and difficulty of accessing the US.

Dr Sampson: I fully agree with the assessment that we should not expect to see major changes in US trade policy, either as a result of the midterms or if there is a Democratic Administration in 2028. There is a broad bipartisan consensus in Washington at the moment in favour of more protectionist measures than the US has traditionally imposed. It is right to expect harder access to US markets going forward.

The other thing we can learn from that tariff experience with the US is that, looking back, the 2019 tariffs hit Scotch as collateral damage as part of the Boeing-Airbus dispute. The point to note there is that, when countries get into a trade dispute and impose retaliatory tariffs, they tend to place them on industries that have a high degree of salience, whether that is cultural salience or political salience. For better or worse, that is a good characterisation of the Scotch industry. A risk factor for the Scotch industry going forward will be that, in the event the UK has a trade dispute with other countries, placing retaliatory tariffs on the Scotch industry is a fairly likely outcome. In the event that there is a UK-India trade dispute or, perhaps more likely, a UK-China trade dispute in coming years, the Scotch industry needs to be prepared for losing market access at least temporarily in that case.

Will Haworth: May I quickly add one point on to Thomas’s? The UK-China risk is very real. If you look at the way that the UK is posturing towards the EU, part of that is now becoming conditional on the UK aligning more closely to the EU’s economic security agenda, which is becoming more restrictive towards China. There is a very real possibility in the near term—and I mean in the next 12 to 18 months—that the UK move towards EU results in it having to impose or match tariffs that the EU has imposed on certain Chinese goods.

Electric vehicles are a very good example and there is a very real case study of this. When the EU introduced those tariffs in 2023 or 2024, one sector that the Chinese Government retaliated against was the French cognac and brandy sector. They did that because the French Government were one of the strongest proponents of a tougher economic stance on China and, as Thomas said, they are politically, economically and culturally salient sectors that they knew would hit voters hard and would hit the local population in a difficult way. The UK-China dynamic is potentially a big risk for the Scotch sector in the coming years.

Kirsteen Sullivan: That is a really interesting point you raise. If this kind of horizon scanning is going on, what can companies do to insulate themselves from the very worst of these impacts?

Will Haworth: To some extent for Scotch, as we said, it is a bit unavoidable. It is caught as collateral in larger trade disputes that do not have anything to do with the alcohol sector or the Scotch sector. I would go back to my previous point, recognising that the risk of access or the cost of accessing certain markets, for example the US and China, is going to increase. The push for diversification of the UK’s access to other markets to offset those higher costs I believe should be a priority for sectors such as Scotch to ensure that, if these higher costs come in, they are able to pivot quickly to other markets and look at expanding other markets, rather than having to address higher costs and lose revenue in the long run.

Kirsteen Sullivan: Professor Khorana, what are the longer-term consequences of tariffs for businesses and their decisions about investment in trade?

Professor Khorana: I really wanted to pick up on this point, so thank you for asking the question. We know that the ongoing tariff landscape is creating, or has created, a lot of uncertainty for businesses, no matter whether it is Scotch whisky or any other business sector. We know that, in general, whenever there is uncertainty, business sentiment takes a hit. That has precisely been the case with Scotch whisky, as it has been with other sectors.

What is very interesting about the Scotch whisky sector is that it plans very well. It plans in advance, because the maturity period for Scotch whisky is roughly, on average, around three years or more—it can be five or 10 years. The planning begins much earlier. Given that there are tariffs, the threat of tariffs, this ongoing global uncertainty and generally low business sentiment, it does not look very good when you look at how it will pan out in the next few years, because planning for the next three to five, seven or 10 years has to be done by businesses today.

We know that Scotch whisky gets a lot of the US casks for Scotch whisky maturing. If you look at the numbers and how they have behaved, you see that the number of casks that have come from the US has declined. This is telling me something: that uncertainty is having a very negative impact on the business sentiment in the Scotch whisky sector.

Maureen Burke: How long does it take for exports to recover after tariffs are removed, particularly when wider economic conditions remain difficult?

Professor Khorana: We know that, once tariffs are imposed, it can cascade through different channels. It can be passed on to the exporter, the importer, the consumers or the distributors. In this case, for Scotch whisky pricing, the tariffs that were imposed by America were passed on primarily to the consumers, which had a very negative impact. In response to your question of how long it takes, it is a long-term horizon. It is not a button you can switch on and switch off. It takes time because the market reacts to the changes in the global landscape. You are looking at this medium to long term, not in the short term.

Dr Sampson: Building on that, another reason why the reactions can often be quite sluggish is that, if you think about the investment decisions that producers are making, they are making those decisions not based necessarily on what the tariff is today, but on their expectation about future tariffs. As we have already talked about, there is a lot of uncertainty in the trade policy environment generally at the moment, and particularly over US trade policy. Even if today or in July the US tariff was removed on Scotch whisky, to the extent that there is a risk that that is a temporary reduction—and we have seen a lot of volatility in US trade policy over the past year—the potential of higher tariffs will also be influencing producers’ decisions. You get a different response than you would if there was certainty that the tariff was gone forever.

Will Haworth: I agree with both those inputs. The only thing I would add is that one of the responses that companies exposed to higher tariffs can make is that they can change production methods or supply chains to try to reduce costs. For Scotch, that is not really an option, given the limitations of its geographic production and production methods. For that reason, pass-through to consumers is one of the only viable options, which then creates issues in terms of pricing and consumer tastes. That has impacts in the long term as well.

Maureen Burke: What do you think the UK Government can do to protect exports such as Scotch whisky from future changes in international trade policy?

Will Haworth: One answer to that question is the market access thing. The more access that the UK has for its businesses and the more diverse that pool is, the better protected businesses are from changes that are unilaterally made by Governments, in the US or China, for example, to increase costs.

There is also greater support that the UK could give domestically. That goes into the question of the UK fiscal position and opens up a whole can of worms that you could talk about for hours. There are ways that the UK could provide greater export certainty through financing and more business promotion as well. The Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade, as it now is, has a very good and very well-established function, beyond trade policy, of trade promotion. That is enormously helpful, particularly for smaller companies and smaller distillers in the Scotch sector. I would argue that greater funding and a greater focus on that, in addition to the trade policy issue, is a significant net benefit in the long run.

Dr Sampson: I have two quick points. One is the benefits in this case of diversification of export markets. In the event that there is a shock in one market, the more diversified you are across different destinations, the less exposed you are to that shock. At the moment, the US and the EU are the biggest markets, and together they are close to 50% of Scotch exports. If there are opportunities to diversify and increase exports to new markets—some of the countries we talked about earlier—that would make the industry less exposed to a shock to US or EU market access.

The second thing builds on the point Will made about the export promotion function of the UK Government. I agree that that is very important and can be very valuable for the industry. We have slightly underinvested in understanding which types of export promotion methods work best. There is a tendency to undertake export promotion, but not necessarily to evaluate the success of those export promotion methods. A high-return investment would be more evaluation of what type of promotion of the industry is most successful.

Professor Khorana: I completely agree with what both of you have said. I would add one point, however. The UK has come up with its industrial policy. If we look at the contributions Scotch whisky makes to the economy in terms of jobs, employment, exports and value added contribution, in my opinion, Scotch whisky has not been highlighted and flagged as an important sector in the industrial policy, which it rightly deserves. There are eight sectors, and none of them talks about Scotch whisky.

That is an aspect and an area the Government can focus on, revisit and see what can be done to integrate the really premium brand of Scotch whisky into the fabric of the industrial policy. Maybe it could be through supporting their industries, through supporting them through lower energy costs or through supporting them through targeted UKEF measures. That is what I would like to add.

Dave Doogan: Dr Sampson and Mr Haworth, you both said that you do not foresee a particular U-turn on tariffs as a result either of the midterms or of a change in Administration in 2028. It is not about tariffs or no tariffs, is it? It is about the type of tariffs you apply and the way that you apply them. It is safe to say that some of the US tariffs are strategic. They have backfired spectacularly in US agriculture.

To the point on Scotch whisky, bourbon casks only get used once, except by the Scotch whisky industry, which then takes them in. Has that been factored into the thinking anywhere? Is that a lever that the industry can pull to say, “It is probably best not to have tariffs on Scotch whisky because of the effect that that will have on your domestic bourbon industry”? I know that you cannot make any Administration make a decision, but were those points made previously? Is that just naive and it is way above that and all about posturing, as you say, on a totemic brand and industry?

Dr Sampson: I do not know whether those specific points were made in negotiations. We know that the UK was successful in getting the tariff on Scotch whisky removed. To some extent there, the status or the slightly iconic nature of the Scotch whisky industry has probably been helpful in getting President Trump to agree to remove the tariffs.

More broadly, it is very difficult to influence US trade policy, particularly as an overseas group, because there are so many different interest groups that have a stake in how US trade policy is made. If there is a Democratic President after 2028, there probably will at least be some kind of re-evaluation of what US trade policy is going to look like going forward. There may be scope at that point to influence changes on the margin. Our baseline expectation should very much be that we are not going back to a pre-2016 world. We have entered an era of, certainly from the US perspective, permanently higher tariffs. We want to be starting from that point when we think about what might be possible.

Douglas Lumsden: Dr Sampson, I want to go back on something you said earlier about export promotion and that there is no evaluation done on what really works. I am confused by that. What is taking place just now? Is it almost a scattergun approach by the Government and hopefully some things will work?

Dr Sampson: I do not want to say that there is no evaluation taking place, but there is scope for a more targeted attempt to understand the costs and benefits of different evaluation strategies, rather running a marketing campaign and then commissioning someone to write a report on how successful it was. Imagine you are introducing a new medical drug. That is not how you would evaluate the effectiveness of the drug. The gold standard of evidence evaluation in the pharmaceutical industry is that you run some kind of randomised trial where some patients get access to the drug and others do not. That gives you a very clean way to understand the effects of the drug.

Those kinds of methods are increasingly being used in policy evaluation, and there is scope to build them into how we evaluate our export promotion strategies. For example, what could one do there? Suppose you are considering a number of markets in which you could run an export promotion strategy for Scotch whisky. Perhaps you can randomise which markets you actually pick to be the target for that strategy. That will give you a cleaner way of understanding how successful the programme is.

Douglas Lumsden: Who would be in control of that? Would it be the Government or the Scotch Whisky Association?

Dr Sampson: I guess that that would depend on whether it was a Government promotion strategy or one run by the whisky industry, or if they were working together. I am probably not best placed to speak to the exact details of how that works.

Douglas Lumsden: There may be a higher growth for the single malt market going forward, as opposed to the blended market.

Professor Khorana: That could come as a result of that, but definitely India is a market where there is growing demand in terms of value and volume for the Scotch whisky sector.

Douglas Lumsden: I guess that it is maintaining the promotional team to sell Scotch whisky around the world. We want to make sure that that takes place.

Dr Sampson: Yes, absolutely.

Kenneth Stevenson: Taking everything into consideration that we have discussed today, what should the UK Government prioritise to help the industry grow?

Dr Sampson: For me, it is the market access point. There is very little the UK Government can do about changes in overall global demand, but they can help the Scotch industry increase its share of that demand.
