Those are people who have been recognised as needing protection, and will now face 20 years as the default, and 30 years in case of penalties. As Anita has highlighted, there are some concessions, which we are happy about, but they do not reflect the responses to the consultation. I will also quickly mention where this started. The case for earned settlement was built on care workers. The Home Secretary pointed to alleged fraud of the care visa and the £10 billion cost involved. Most of that group is now protected, and rightly so, but the system remains in place for everyone else, and the core model is unchanged. It brings issues of equality at its core, because the scheme is measured almost entirely through earnings, which are not neutral, per se. They carry the gender pay gap, part-time and caring work, disability and racial inequalities that we see in the labour market, so those will just be reflected in the new scheme. As Anita has highlighted, there are also massive issues around children.

The scale of this is quite significant. According to Home Office figures published on Monday, half a million people will now have to settle later, and that number is a floor rather than a ceiling, because quite a lot of people have not been captured in it. I also highlight that the system will be very costly and complex. The Government promised something that would be a bit easier to navigate, but instead we are ending up with routes for three, five, 10, 20 and 30 years, with a lot of tests and things that need to be assessed along the way. There will be more cost for applicants, but also for the Home Office. We have not seen an impact assessment yet, so the first thing for the Committee is to push the Government to have one before the rules are laid later this year.

Dame Nia Griffith: As you understand it, what objectives are the Government trying to achieve with the changes to settlement? To what extent will the proposals achieve those objectives?

Sabrina Galella: We understand that the Government set out about four aims with these reforms. They really talked about settlement, saying that it should be earned, not automatic, and that it should reward contribution. Now, we see that they are talking about fiscal contribution, and saying that it should promote integration, reward the compliance of people who play by the rules, and, in effect, deter regular entry, which sits with the wider goal of reducing net migration. As Andrea has highlighted, the Government say that they want to attract the brightest and the best, but they also include public confidence in the system.

With all these aims, the evidence that we have so far points to a very different direction. First, settlement is not automatic. The Home Secretary says that settlement is granted near automatically. However, when we look at the response, we can see that is actually not the case. It says today that people have to apply after five years, subject to meeting the relevant route-specific requirements. For example, a skilled worker must still earn over £41,000, or the going rate for the job if that is higher. A partner also needs a specific family income, and everyone must pass the “Life in the UK” test and meet the English requirement and clear suitability checks on criminality, NHS debt and character. Then, there are expensive fees to apply, and people also need to show continuous residence. This idea that settlement is automatic is not based on facts.

In terms of the savings that the Home Secretary and the Government talked about, they would come mostly from delaying when lower paid workers can claim benefits. The Home Affairs Committee called this a “short-term” gain because it just delays people having access to benefits. That Committee also accepts that people leaving could cost more than this change saves. Then there are the additional costs that are not counted in this. Those include local authorities having to pick up the bill, especially for increases in demand on children services or risk for homelessness services.

On integration, where the House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee looked at longer routes, it simply said that they risk undermining integration because there is no security in terms of status. It therefore becomes harder for people to be secure in their housing and employment and in building roots. It also becomes more difficult for children to access education because they do not have access to home fees. It leaves people in this precarious migration status and it works against integration.

There is also a point about moving the goalposts. Except for a few exemptions in the proposals, there is no general transitional protection. There are people who came here and built their lives on the promise that in five years they will have secure settled status, but now they are going to face waiting 10, 20 or more years. As I have said, we are talking about over half a million people. When it looked at the proposals, the House of Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee said that changing the goalposts and applying those changes retrospectively is “manifestly unfair”.

Again, it is also going to be incredibly expensive and quite complex for everybody to navigate, both for applicants but also for the Home Office. There are also going to be more applications to process. There are going to be more opportunities for errors to happen, so more people might fall out of status, and more people might be refused—again, because there is just a higher percentage of errors, along with having all these extensive checks. Refugees will have to go through at least nine renewals. At every point there might be an error made. There may be a delay or a missed appointment. They all carry very heavy penalties for people.

We do not think that the Government’s stated aims at the start of this process are going to be met. We also think that, because they started to tackle what the Government thought were abuses or misuse of the care worker routes, those people are rightly protected. There is no need to apply the same system to everybody else, because settlement is not automatic and we already have quite difficult thresholds for people to meet before they have earned settlement.

Andrea Als: Just to add a sentence, I think that the Government’s aim was around compliance and ensuring that there was immigration compliance for all, but they have essentially looked at the JAMs I was speaking about—the compliant people—so that does not quite make sense.

Dame Nia Griffith: Do we have any idea how many people are likely to experience a wait of 10 years or more for settlement under the proposals, or whether particular groups of people will be more impacted by the changes?

Now, they will be thinking, “I need to extend my timeline by another five years.” I know that the Government are looking at a “best years” approach to allow people to take maternity leave, but those sorts of things are the real, live questions in women’s minds at the moment.

It feels like the proposals will have an unnecessary and disproportionate impact on women, because they are a very compliant group. That is not to say that groups regarded as non-compliant need to be come down on in a harder way; it is just to say that this group, numbering between 25,000 and 50,000, is being impacted in a way that does not make sense. There should be transitional arrangements.

Dr Siddiqui: Migrant women and children are disproportionately impacted because they are more likely to be in lower-paid jobs, they are three times more likely to experience domestic abuse and a lot of them are not really protected. Organisations like ourselves—the Southall Black Sisters—are based in the charity sector. We are a “by and for” service, so we work with and for black, minoritised and migrant women, and a lot of our workers are also migrants. The charity sector generally is going to be very badly impacted. I think it is really important that charities are exempted or the thresholds to be completely reduced.

Dame Nia Griffith: Could I push on the point of retrospectivity? What do you feel are the implications of that? You have talked about global reputation, for example. The pathways to settlement that people are currently on are being changed. Does anyone want to make any further comment on the issue of retrospectivity?

Anita Hurrell: I can say something about retrospectivity in relation to two groups. As Andrea said, many thousands of people on skilled worker visas, including their families, are not seeing any concessions or row-back on the policies. They are going to be on a 10-year route and it is going to be very difficult for them. We must also remember that this week’s news comes on top of a year and a half of uncertainty since the May 2025 White Paper. People have been living with absolutely profound levels of fear. They don’t know what to do. They don’t know whether to have babies; they don’t know whether to have IVF. They don’t know whether to get a mortgage.

Thanks to organisations like Justice for Carers, many people in the care sector now have a bit more certainty, but this is affecting hundreds of thousands of people across the country and in our communities who basically have had their life plans upended, including those who relied on the 10-year lawful residence route. Retrospective effect has really created chaos in a lot of people’s life plans.

For us, that has had quite a serious impact on children. Lots of people think about their children when they migrate; they think about their children’s education and what language their children will grow up speaking. We know that there is a lot of concern about potential disruption to children’s education. That is on the skilled worker route.

The biggest retrospective bombshell that we got this week, and Sabrina can speak to this, is that in footnote 14—in a footnote—to the Government consultation, we suddenly have core protection and a 20 or 30-year route to settlement applied retrospectively to refugees, including children in refugee families.

There are some estimates of the numbers in the Government's own paper, but the Refugee Council, I think, believes that potentially 200,000 people are impacted through this footnote—huge numbers of people, including those who already have refugee status. These are people who claimed asylum and got refugee status. Maybe they knew that things were changing in the asylum system, but they had been told explicitly, in black and white, that if they claimed asylum before 2 March 2026, they were on the old five-year pathway—the one that we all know—and now they are being told, via a footnote, that they could retrospectively be moved on to core protection and these incredibly long routes.

Retrospectivity does huge damage to trust. It has really wreaked havoc over the past year to year and a half. The less the proposals are retrospective, the better. Lots of people can talk more eloquently than me about legitimate expectation and potential legal challenges, but we have huge concerns on that point.

Richard Quigley: You have touched on this, Anita, so this question is for Andrea and Sabrina. What do you think will be the main impacts of a 20 or 30-year settlement route for refugees?

Sabrina Galella: Let me give a very quick whistle-stop tour. As Anita has highlighted, quite a lot of changes have happened this year. Until March this year, a refugee would receive five years’ leave and then could apply to settle. Now, there are different tracks according to what has been published on Monday.

There is the resettlement route, which has just reopened. Refugees who come through a resettlement scheme can settle after five years. This is welcome news, but those are very limited in number, and resettlement rules apply only to refugees who are outside their country of residence. They are processed through the refugee agency of the United Nations, so people do not get to apply individually, and they do not have a choice. They are really quite limited in terms of scope and number.

Then we have this new idea of core protection, so refugees who claim asylum—all of them—now get 30 months’ leave at a time. It used to be five years, and that need for protection now needs to be reassessed at every renewal. So every 30 months, people have to check whether there is still a need for them to be protected in this country, and now they can settle only after 20 years. Again, we are talking about recognised refugees.

Then there is this announced protection, work and study route. We do not know how it will work in practice, but refugees who get into sustained work, education and training can move on to this route, which would lead to settlement after 10 years. There is a bit of misunderstanding over the wording on what happens—whether the clock restarts at zero when they switch—so we are going to wait until we have a bit more clarity on that.

What is the impact? On top of genuine refugees having to now wait 20 years to settle, there is irregular entry, so anyone who arrived via a dangerous journey, such as by small boat, has 10 years added. That makes 30 years on core protection almost the standard, because the majority of people do have to come via dangerous journeys, as highlighted in the opening statement. There is no way to apply for asylum from abroad, so people have to come here and ask for asylum at the border. Women experiencing gender-based violence or victims of trafficking may have no control over their mode of travel to the UK. They may have travelled by irregular means out of desperation or coercion, yet they will be punished for this.

In terms of having a criminal record of any kind and having no Government debt, it is worth mentioning what is happening with the wider Immigration and Asylum Bill going through Parliament at the moment, specifically part 4, which creates a charge—a tax—for people who sought sanctuary. Now, they will have to repay the cost of accommodation when they receive support. I am going to say, again, that people seeking asylum are banned from working, at least for the initial year. It sounds like they will have to pay the cost of being put in state-funded accommodation through no fault of their own. In addition, sometimes the lack of legal aid compounded by very poor Home Office decision making makes their claims stay longer in the pipeline.

If people have Government debt, they will not be able to settle until the debt it is cleared, so refugees face a burden on top of a burden. Then there is the point about public funds. Anyone who has used public funds—that applies to all groups of people, not just refugees—in the five years before applying has to wait 20 years for settlement. As Anita has highlighted, refugees who claimed before 2 March are now moved to core protection. That is a U-turn, and it is another thing that people were not expecting. Just in these last three days, we at ILPA have been getting so many questions from people because there is so much uncertainty and fear. People were not expecting this. As Anita said, to see this in a footnote is quite disappointing and worrying.

Earned settlement has to be read in conjunction with the Bill, the wider changes that are going through Parliament, and the changes that have been made to family reunion, which also has been paused since September 2025, meaning that refugees cannot bring their families over unless they apply under appendix FM, which is quite an expensive and complicated process, and the majority of them will not meet the threshold. This simply means that we have a punitive system that is going to punish people for seeking sanctuary and having to take dangerous journeys because they have no other choice.

It will also prolong the time they are in integration limbo. They will not be able to find employment or access housing in the same way as other settled people. It will be harder for their children to settle and have access to the same opportunities. In many cases, it will increase dependence on exploitation. It might also split families, especially for young people whose families have not settled. In general, it will also push people away from seeking help. Over the last year, we have already seen evidence from our members that quite a lot of people are not accessing vital support simply because they fear having to pay a penalty for using public funds. That was before. Now that this has been announced, we know that quite a lot of people who are in need of public funds, which are designed for people who are in need, will not use them, so there will be more refugees facing destitution and homelessness and being put in harm’s way. Overall, it is incredibly punitive for refugees and also not what people in the system were expecting to see in the announcement on Monday.

Anita Hurrell: I will add three quick points on what this means for refugee children in families on 20 or 30-year routes. First, we have long maintained that children need permanence and a future horizon. Refugee children ask a lot about their past but think a lot about their future. We know that the Government recognise that young people who have come over as refugees often want to continue in education. They put in huge amounts of effort—really inspiring degrees of effort, when you think about what some of those children and young people have been through. We are talking about the need for permanence and the need for the recovery and rehabilitation of children who have experienced human rights abuses, family disaster, bereavement, long journeys to the UK and exploitation. Children need permanence and a future horizon—they need to be able to progress in education. We think these long routes will hinder that and hinder their development.

Secondly, the Government want refugees to contribute, but those long routes make that really difficult. When someone has a short-term visa, getting a job is harder. It is more onerous for the employer because they are employing someone with a precarious immigration status, so they have to keep checking the date. Children are going to grow up in households with lower incomes and less employment stability as a result, so there will be big implications there. It is going to make it harder for refugee families to do what the Government want them to do, because they are going through this cycle every 30 months.

The third point relates to identity—this is about the whole of earned settlement, but it relates to refugee children as well. There is some recognition of children and young people in the response that we got this week, but to put refugee families on 20 and 30-year routes really does not reckon with the importance of childhood and teenagerhood in forming children’s identity, and what it does and says to children when their families are put on those long routes. I recommend that the Committee refers to the research of We Belong, a youth organisation working on those issues, which in a different context has spoken about state-sponsored unwantedness and lots of issues to do with children and young people’s identity, and their participation in education and the labour market.

We have a big problem with NEETs and alienation and radicalisation. What does it mean to tell children and young people that they do not belong by putting their families on those long routes? Young people in those kinds of situations think, “Why do I have to go through all this bureaucracy?” Felling like you belong has a way of changing how you see yourself. On that identity dimension, we want to highlight the work of organisations such as We Belong, and what this means for how children and young people grow up seeing themselves.

Chair: Can I ask a quick question for my own understanding? You talk about the lack of security and permanence for children on the refugee routes. How does that interact with the seven-year private life route and how do those two things interact? I have not quite got that in my head, so could you explain?

Anita Hurrell: Yes. To be honest, that question was going around my head this morning, so I spoke to some colleagues about it. I think it is sort of unclear. We are really pleased that children’s need to get status in their own right has been recognised with the protection of private life rules, but whether children in refugee families will be able to avail themselves of those routes is still unclear.

At the moment, as the rules stand, if you have had an inadmissible asylum claim, you will not be able to apply under the private life rules as a child or a young person. I can write to the Committee with more details, and Sabrina might want to come in, but we are not really sure at the moment. We hope that those rules will provide something for most children growing up in the UK, but we need to talk to the Home Office more about some of the details.

Chair: This is really helpful. Just a quick follow-up: would those private life rules be for the child but not the parents, or would they include the parents? If you have a nine-year-old child who has been here since they were two, how does that work?

Anita Hurrell: The rules as set out at the moment are that a child born in the UK who reaches the age of seven can get ILR immediately. If you have been in the UK for seven years as a child, or if you are aged 18 to 24 and you have spent half of your life here, you can settle in five years. There are provisions that give either immediate ILR for UK-born children or a five-year route for non-resident children and young people.

What the implications are for parents is another big question—Sabrina might want to come in on this. There are provisions for the parents of British children, and that is one of the other big questions in the paper. For some of those children, if they get ILR at seven, they might get citizenship at age 10, where they are UK-born. What does that mean for parents? That goes to one of the main concerns that we have: if you provide some rules for children to get on to routes to settlement that are maybe a bit shorter, does that mean much if even a British child has a mother—a single mum—on a 20 or 30-year route, and their benefits access is determined by their mum’s immigration status? These are really live questions. I am sorry if I cannot answer them all definitively, but those are the questions that are up in the air at the moment.

Chair: That is very helpful.

Dr Siddiqui: A lot of the women we assist are migrant women who have come to this country not necessarily as asylum seekers but maybe on partner visas. Often, if they are not able to claim under the spousal visa for settlement under the domestic abuse route, the only alternative is to use the asylum route, particularly article 8, which is the right to a family and private life.

Both of those are being threatened at the moment, so those women will have to wait longer to be able to get asylum and refugee status. They will not be able to pay for their bills or their accommodation, because a lot of the time they are economically dependent on violent partners, so they do not have the resources themselves. They cannot claim public funds, they are more likely to be destitute—both mother and children—they are more likely to be rough sleeping, they are more likely to be homeless, and they will be in limbo for much longer. They are more vulnerable to exploitation and harassment from their partners, who could be stalking them and harassing them even if they have left them. They are vulnerable to returning to an abusive partner.

We also understand that article 8 may be narrowed down. A lot of times, women are the ones who are the main carers, but they will possibly not be able to use article 8 as much in the future to be able to stay in the country. There is a whole range of implications around asylum and migrant women generally facing gender-based violence.

Alex Brewer: I want to address the impact on women and people with a disability. We have a lot of questions, so can you keep answers as succinct as possible? Everything you are saying is absolutely fascinating. I want to start with whether the Government’s decision to allow households to combine their income to meet the £25,142 threshold adequately addresses the concerns that women with caring responsibilities might be less able to meet settlement requirements.

Andrea Als: It addresses it to some extent, but we still have the regional issues; that is really problematic. In addition to that, it is welcome that they are taking on household income, definitely. Women who face honour-based abuse, which many agencies do not recognise as domestic abuse, may also not be covered. There is a concern that not all forms of abuse will be included, and that different types of visas are not included.

They would fall under the test around criminalisation and may not get settlement as a consequence.

There is definitely potential to make positive progress there. We would love to work further with the Committee, the Home Office and the Department for Education on that.

Chair: Absolutely. Finally, the Immigration and Asylum Bill contains measures relating to modern slavery. Do you think any of those measures will have an adverse impact on children? Is there a case for extending access to the independent child trafficking guardians to care leavers who might have been trafficked, for example, until they reach the age of 25?

Anita Hurrell: I think the answer is yes and yes. We can certainly send further written information to the Committee on these, including citing the evidence that both Barnardo's and ECPAT UK have put forward during the passage of the Bill so far. There are some positives in the Bill for children who are victims of modern slavery, but the most significant concern is that several provisions are inconsistent with what safeguarding professionals know about how children disclose abuse. I can point to three, but maybe we will follow up in writing.

The credibility provisions are causing huge concern. With children who are victims of modern slavery, the provisions require decision makers to start from a place of questioning trust and credibility rather than considering it. We are really worried about the way it affects how children are believed or disbelieved. We would suggest that maybe the Bill unintentionally imports assumptions about who is a credible victim, and they are not really consistent with safeguarding practice or indeed with the violence against women and girls agenda that we have talked about already. We have concerns about credibility and specialist support, and about temporary protection to stay leave. I might follow up in writing with the Committee, if that is okay.

On the potential to extend the duration of an independent child trafficking guardian, pilots and studies have shown the benefit, when evaluating what these guardians have done, of having this kind of trusted independent adult who can navigate complex systems. I know there is important evidence from Barnardo’s, which operates the scheme, and from others working in the anti-trafficking sector that those needs do not disappear when a young person turns 18. In fact, the transition to adulthood can be extremely risky for young people around homelessness, immigration status, exploitation, mental health, potential disengagement from services, and potentially going missing from services. We would be fully supportive of that proposal.
