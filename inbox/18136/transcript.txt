Q221 Chair: Welcome to this joint session of the Health and Social Care and Education Select Committees. This is the fourth session of our inquiry into young people and children’s mental health services. It is a delight to have with us today Professor Peter Fonagy and Professor Gillian Baird of the now infamous Fonagy review. It has been a week since it has come out, and it has been very widely reported.

Can I start by asking a very simple question? How did you come to be the one to be asked to do this review? What is your background, so that those who may be less familiar with it understand the expertise that you bring to bear on this area?

Professor Fonagy: Thank you for asking, and thank you for having us here. We are really pleased to be able to present to you. By background, I am a clinical psychologist. I have been working in child mental health for four decades, which is quite a long time. Since 2011, I have been senior clinical adviser to NHS England in the child mental health policy team. I have really enjoyed it. That is probably the reason why I was asked—I was familiar with the system and had clinical experience that seemed appropriate to the task.

Professor Baird: I was asked to be co-vice chair with Professor Sir Simon Wessely, who is not with us today but whom I suspect many of you know. By way of background, I am a paediatrician. I specialised in paediatric neurodisability and was a consultant running a large service at Guy’s and St Thomas’ for rather more years than Peter has been. I was also much involved in NICE and various guidelines and research into areas relevant to this review.

Q222 Chair: People can read the over-600-page report too, if they want to, but, Professor Fonagy, could you very briefly outline the top recommendations that you made? While you do that, could you underline the recommendations that you think are the low-hanging fruit, and which ones you think might be more challenging to implement?

Professor Fonagy: The low-hanging fruit will be a little bit challenging, but I will try. The review proposes a radically different model of managing children’s mental health. It is a shift from a diagnosis-led system, where people have to wait to get a diagnosis in order to get support, to a system where, as soon as they present with a need, that need gets addressed and they are then prioritised for a diagnosis if needed, but in the hope that in many instances it will not be. This means that they would receive support earlier in relation to what they actually need, because, surprisingly, diagnosis does not tell you what people need. After you have a diagnosis, it still needs to be figured out what it is that you require. That seemed to us illogical. I emphasise that diagnostic assessment and specialist services remain essential in this system. We are not trying to downgrade the service. It needs to be protected and strengthened.

We called for a number of other changes, which are perhaps ambitious. A stronger focus on prevention, a public health approach to wellbeing, and tackling the social and environmental drivers that we identified for the increasing prevalence of common mental disorders, particularly in children and young people but across all ages, seemed to us to be important.

We also identified that the current system was incompatible with delivering effective prevention. The system that we are proposing is far more aligned to a system where targeted prevention is aligned with offering support at the point when people present with need. We argued that we need to create conditions for people to stay well rather than relying predominantly on specialist services.

There are just a couple more things that are really essential to our logic. This has been recommended because of the very high level of co-occurrence of neurodevelopmental disorders and common mental disorders.

Q223 Chair: What do you mean specifically by that? Just break it down for a non-clinician like me.

Professor Fonagy: For a non-clinician, it is that a person who is diagnosed with ADHD or autism is likely, in the majority of cases, to also have common mental disorders of anxiety, depression or self-harm, or an eating disorder. That means that, in order to deal effectively with both common mental disorders that carry with them traits of autism and other neurodevelopmental problems such as ADHD, it is essential that the assessments occur as part of a single system. That does not mean it is the same assessment or a single assessment, but it does mean that it is an integrated assessment.

At the moment, people get put on one of three pathways, as I am sure you know. That is an inefficient system. That sometimes requires people to be assessed a number of times and wait on a number of waiting lists before they acquire a diagnosis.

There are other things that we recommended that we will perhaps come around to being able to talk about.

Chair: Thank you for that. I have a constituent who has been on a CAMHS waiting list for an autism diagnosis for seven years. They are now 15. The time has passed to help them in their schooling. I am sure every MP here and many listening will relate to that.

Q224 Peter Swallow: One of the experiences we all have as local MPs is exactly as Layla describes there, hearing from families who are very distressed that their young people are not getting the support that they need.

Another thing that we will all be familiar with is others telling us that it is all down to the snowflake generation, and that the mental health crisis we are seeing in our young people is because they just are not resilient enough. Your report rejects that idea. Could you perhaps just explain why you reached that conclusion and the evidence that lies behind that?

Professor Fonagy: You asked two questions. I will answer the second one and maybe turn to Professor Baird to answer the first. The “snowflake generation” is an insult to young people. I challenge all of you here to cope with the pressures that young people are under and to sustain the quality of childhood and young adulthood that you all were privileged to experience.

You did not have two major categories of pressures. One is increased stress. I can go into detail in relation to this, but increased stress is financial; that is well known. It is about insecure work, unaffordable rents and so on. Also, there is increased stress in terms of social media and social comparisons that are incessant, and educational pressures. We have evidence for that. Again, I can go into that, but it seems that young people are less well protected and less well supported. That is the second category of issues.

It is recorded that they have a lower sense of belonging to their schools. They do not feel that they belong, but it is not just schools. There has been a 73% reduction in youth services that are funded by local authorities since 2011. They do not have other young people to go to.

Finally, in terms of the lack of protection, I want to mention what we found particularly important, which is the opportunity for recovery, which is unstructured play. That is not just about youth clubs, but just being able to sleep. If you have social media that keeps you awake, stops you from sleeping and reduces face-to-face interactions, you will have less capacity to cope.

When you and I were young, we had friends. We went to see friends when we were in trouble. If that is taken away and replaced by mobile phones, they have greater stress and less support. I would reject the idea that it is a snowflake generation.

Professor Baird: The other question was particularly about waiting lists.

Peter Swallow: That was more of a flavour to get into the question. We will come back to that.

Chair: We will be coming back to waiting lists. Fear not.

Q225 Peter Swallow: I do not want to steal anyone else’s thunder on that question. Really, I just wanted to follow up on a very helpful answer about this idea of a snowflake generation being an insulting narrative. Why do you think it has gained such traction despite the lack of evidence for it? How do we combat it?

Professor Fonagy: I am going outside the report slightly and now expressing my personal views. This is not so much about me, because I am on the other side of this, but looking at you, most of you are of a generation that was brought up believing that your future would be better than your parents’. The current generation are convinced, and rightly so, that they will not have the opportunities that their parents had.

That sense of hopelessness has been demonstrated. This is an empirical fact. That is part of their beliefs, but it is also part of our beliefs about them. It is an enormously powerful influence, but it also makes us feel somewhat guilty in relation to them. Therefore, it is much easier for us to say they are a snowflake generation than for us to say, “Actually, we have really messed it up for them, and it is our fault.”

If you think back, covid was the first time that I can remember when our generation, the adult generation, brought in a way of adjusting to a major health crisis that advantaged adults and disadvantaged children and young people. Mostly, the priority is given to the new generation, but for whatever reason—I am not here to blame anyone—we decided that our health was more important than theirs.

Q226 Peter Swallow: That is a profoundly challenging answer. It is challenging to all of us here. I just briefly reflect that, with AI coming down the track as well, it is not at risk of getting easier.

The other prevailing narrative that we hear a lot is that the rise in need reflects better diagnosis and identification of mental ill health by practitioners. That is perhaps slightly less of a pernicious myth, but nevertheless it is one that you also rejected in your report. Could you again just say a little about how you came to that conclusion?

Professor Baird: The first thing to say is that there is no evidence of overdiagnosis. We did not find evidence. To some extent, the studies have not been done. Nobody has taken a very large number of people with one diagnosis and had another group of people then examine them to see whether they would come to the same conclusion. That would be the obvious way to answer this question. Despite a huge amount of anecdote, report and opinion, there was no evidence, and this is a highly evidence-based review.

We did find evidence of misdiagnosis, where somebody would have a diagnosis of one condition and another condition was not recognised that might better explain their difficulties. This comes back to the co-occurrence, which is a central feature of the review.

The obvious examples are with eating disorders; only more recently are people recognising that many of those affected have autism as well, which has an influence on the management of the condition. You could be diagnosed with autism when somebody else might think that your major problem was ADHD or anxiety or another mental health problem. The way in which services are so often organised as single-diagnosis pathways means that you go for an assessment where the answer is, “Yes you do have this,” or “No, you do not,” and then you may have to rejoin a completely different pathway for an integrated assessment of what the main problems might be.

The other thing is that, when we looked at the number of people with a common mental disorder such as anxiety or depression, you might think the first thing would be that perhaps people have much milder problems these days. That is not what the evidence found. The evidence found that the level of impairment was as high or sometimes higher. The symptoms were causing greater impairment, so it is not a milder problem.

There are various other possible explanations for why the number of people with a diagnosis of ADHD or autism might be rising. I am happy to go into those, but there may be some other questions about those. There is a broader understanding of some of these difficulties. There have been diagnostic criteria changes. Of course, while the underlying traits, features and symptoms of neurodevelopmental conditions are heavily genetically influenced, the impairment that people experience is a result of the interaction of genetic predisposition with environment.

Q227 Caroline Voaden: I would now like to talk a little bit about the drivers of mental distress that you refer to in the report. You talked about several things including emotional pressure, social media, economic uncertainty and reduced social connectedness. I notice, Professor Fonagy, that you have already talked about social media. You refer quite a lot to sleep in the report. We know that sleep is really important to good health. Being on phones late at night is a real barrier to good sleep.

Is it possible for you to quantify the extent to which each of these pressures is driving demand, where you think the main focus should be, and where the priority should be in the Government’s response?

Professor Fonagy: That is a brilliant question. As with all brilliant questions, I cannot answer it. There is an honest reason why I cannot answer it, which is that they are interacting. If influences interact, it is very hard to see which came first: the egg or the chicken.

There are three things that I can say with confidence. One is that, as I have already said, they are not making it up, but the language has changed. Awareness of mental disorder has changed so that it has become a psychological language. The same level of distress that a person might feel today is more likely to drive them towards getting mental health support than it used to. That may seem like a negative, but it is actually a positive because we can help these people.

In terms of the drivers, there is a lot of negative content on social media and I do not want to minimise that. I do not want to be an apologist for social media, but it displaces things that are protective rather than simply causing harm.

I am mentioning that because of the second part of your question, about what the Government can do. By banning social media broadly, we cannot achieve quite as much, because the same distress that drives people towards social media and to seek answers from social media will still be there even if you ban it, except with less support.

It is really important that the good things that it displaces can be encouraged and supported. For example, if social media displaces physical exercise, we can encourage it. If social media displaces face-to-face interactions, we can create situations where they are enhanced. If it causes problems with sleep, we can have good ways of dealing with sleep problems. You cannot force people to go to sleep, but you can put on an alarm clock and make them wake up, which is a very simple measure of dealing with sleep disorder that we have used for a number of years.

I have one final thing, which touches on the earlier point that I made. One thing that we could all do in relation to discouraging children from overusing social media is to use our own social media less. The number of people who are engaged with their cell phones rather than face-to-face interactions over meals is appalling. Of course, it sets a model for young people because they are there to learn from us. Human beings learn by looking at adults and seeing what they do. If we set an appalling model, can we be surprised that they do appalling things?

Q228 Caroline Voaden: There is quite a movement now for delaying the purchase of smartphones for children going into secondary school, and not having one at 11, but pushing it back. Do you think that just not having a device until young people are older would perhaps make them more resilient?

Professor Fonagy: Young people are very resilient, remarkably so, but at the same time I want to emphasise that they are also shrewd. Any attempt at some kind of Government injunction to deprive them of something that they feel that they need is less likely to be successful than we hope. We can do a great deal to change social norms and expectations on them, and to emphasise, as I said, activities that are displaced by social media.

I do a lot of advising parents about how they should bring up their children, and there is one piece of advice that I give above all: stop criticising your child and notice when they do something that you want them to do; reinforce and encourage that. We need to be less spiteful and less depriving in our approach to young people, and far more encouraging. That is a general shift that your advice to Government could probably take forward.

Q229 Caroline Voaden: Last, I have a private Members’ Bill about outdoor education.

Professor Fonagy: Thank you for that.

Caroline Voaden: I believe that getting children outside is a very cheap way of improving children’s mental health, sense of belonging, wellbeing and all of that. Could you tell us how important you think it is for children to be outside more and to be in nature?

Professor Fonagy: The reduction of green space correlates positively with the increase in mental health issues for young people and the decrease in wellbeing. I am sure you are very familiar with those statistics.

Obviously, being outside is an opportunity. This is irrelevant, but my father, in order to enhance my resilience, made me wear short trousers throughout winter. That was in Hungary, in a continental climate. I do not think it made me more resilient. It is not about being exposed; it is the opportunities that it brings with it, which is communication with other young people.

From age 8 through to age 11 or 12, there is a shift in the interests of young people from their parents as the primary influence to other young people being the primary influence. If we deprive them of having that opportunity, we deprive them of the major way that their genes have enabled them to integrate with modern culture. You are doing God’s work.

Q234 Gregory Stafford: Thank you very much. Moving on to some of your recommendations, in recommendation 5, you essentially looked at a complete overhaul of the commissioning, funding, oversight of the system, et cetera. What changes do you propose to improve the ADHD and autism diagnostic and treatment pathways? Specifically, which change do you think would have the greatest impact on improving the waiting times?

Professor Baird: The most important is to integrate the mental health and neurodevelopmental disorder assessment so that, if you have problems and you come in for assessment, you will be having an assessment of all the possible contributory factors. If you have one problem, you are so much more likely to have another. To miss that then has a huge impact on the level and appropriateness of support that you are likely to achieve.

That is going to be one of the most important things that we can do, rather than these separate pathways. A huge amount of duplication is currently going on. People have an assessment in one place, then in another. There is a lot of duplication. Integration, both of assessment and then of services following that, is one of the most important recommendations that will help.

Q235 Gregory Stafford: In terms of that balance, are you suggesting that changing how the support is accessed and delivered is a greater priority, for example, than just increasing capacity overall? They are both important, but that is your view.

Professor Baird: Yes, absolutely. If all you did was to increase capacity, you would not do a very good job at all. The system as it stands at the moment is not the right one for meeting people’s needs early and then providing appropriate and comprehensive assessment.

Q236 Gregory Stafford: What do the Government or the NHS need to do to provide this “no wrong door” access? What are the practical steps?

Professor Fonagy: There are several. The first one that should be emphasised is changing the sequence from a sequence that is, “Wait, assess, diagnose, support,” to something that is, “Recognise need, then understand need, then begin support, then establish diagnosis where it changes, and then review and adapt as needed.”

This is a radical change that has been implemented in a number of places. As we cite in our review, we try to seem original, but we are not. This has been discovered. It only works though, as is also documented in the review, if it is sufficiently well supported. To have a needs-based system without adequate support creates a problem rather than solving one.

We are asking for two things. One is further resources to support that needs-based system. The second is a reorganisation that is more efficient than anything that is in place now. The combination of the two will ultimately lead to a reduction in cost because of earlier intervention.

At the moment, we have a system where you gatekeep in order to be able not to treat. With children and young people particularly, if you wait long enough, the treatments that we have are not helpful. It is setting thresholds at a level where you can no longer intervene effectively. It is a particularly mindless thing to do. It is understandable, but all the evidence is that moving support earlier makes the treatments that we have far more effective. If you wait even for two weeks, the likelihood of a condition getting worse is something like 20%; if you wait for six months, it is 75%. Reducing waiting lists is really a key part of what we are recommending.
