Q65 Chair: Welcome to the final evidence session of our inquiry into taxation of the hospitality and tourism sectors in Northern Ireland. We are really pleased to be taking our evidence today from St Columb’s Hall in Derry/Londonderry. This is the first time that the Committee has taken public evidence in Northern Ireland since March 2024, and the first time outside Stormont since June 2018, so this is quite the achievement.

We are joined by Dr Joanne Stuart, chief exec of Northern Ireland Tourism Alliance, and Aidan Lyons, director of indirect tax at Grant Thornton Ireland. Thank you ever so much for joining us. How would you assess the financial health of the hospitality sector in Northern Ireland?

Dr Stuart: Thank you very much for the invitation to speak to you today. As you said, I represent the Northern Ireland Tourism Alliance, which represents the whole of the tourism ecosystem in Northern Ireland. We are the voice of tourism in Northern Ireland, and that covers everything from transport to tour guiding, accommodation, attractions and so on.

In July, we had the 2025 figures for tourism, which showed that tourism—the number of visits and trips—is growing. However, the associated spend and the associated days or nights that people are staying have not increased at the same level. We are doing well, but we have more work to do with regard to increasing the spend and giving visitors more reason to stay in Northern Ireland.

The latest Tourism NI industry survey ran to assess the first six months of this year. The majority of companies are doing the same as, if not better than, last year, but from a challenge perspective, we are looking at the value. An increase in visitors does not necessarily mean an increase in revenues, which goes through to the bottom line.

We have a number of challenges, particularly in the hospitality industry. I know that representatives from Hospitality Ulster have spoken to you. The challenges are things like business rates, the inflation costs in food and the increase in salaries and wages, which was a particular challenge for all businesses in the tourism industry. Primarily, we are made up of small and medium-sized enterprises, and the increase in the NIC threshold and the rate of NIC were huge costs to be assumed by the industry.

As a result, that did not leave companies with enough reserves to get into the investment that is needed, or to put forward competitive pricing, because of the costs that have to be assumed. I have prepared a more detailed brief for the Committee on the back of this, and I have given a full breakdown of the differentials between Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland across all the different operating costs. That is stark, with regard not just to VAT but to payroll, pensions and so on.

Q66 Chair: You have gone through basically what the problems are—the VAT, employer NI contributions, increases to the national minimum wage, businesses’ energy costs—but to what extent do you think difficulties are caused by the VAT rate, as opposed to the other factors?

Dr Stuart: VAT is one factor, but there are also a number of costs when you look at this from my perspective—I look at the whole trip. Our case is based on looking at it from the consumer perspective—the visitor, business viability and future investment. When we look at the visitor or the tour operator choosing where to go, VAT is one of the costs that potentially makes us less competitive. Then we are looking at the increase in travel costs—APD and ETA—and a number of aspects add costs on to our trip.

Chair: Which we will come to.

Dr Stuart: Therefore, because we have a higher rate of VAT, all that is coming together. We believe that we need a sustained reduction in VAT. However, that does not mean that we do not need to look at the costs that are within our control.

Chair: I will go to Aidan with the same question.

Aidan Lyons: I echo Joanne’s thanks for the invitation to appear before you today. I specialise in indirect tax, in VAT principally. The standard rate of VAT in the UK is 20%. There is a differentiation from the Republic of Ireland, where it is 13.5% and 9%—there is a difference.

It is a consumer tax, so it is the consumer who pays the tax, and it is added on to the cost or the price that they pay, whether that is staying at a hotel, going to a restaurant or whatever it may be. It can have an impact on the overall price paid. I am not an economist, although our submission included quite a lot of economic analysis and reference, so I would certainly defer to Joanne in that and in her understanding and knowledge of the sector. Potentially, a reduction in VAT might help—it can help—but there is a wider policy here. Clearly, costs are going up—utilities, wages and other costs that the sectors have to face—and they all factor into the overall position.

Q67 Chair: I suppose this is one for you, Joanne: would you say that tourism and hospitality businesses in Northern Ireland are struggling compared with similarly sized businesses in other sectors?

Dr Stuart: I could not say yes or no; I think it is different across the piece. We have some very successful tourism businesses, but smaller businesses can be struggling. That is common across the economy in Northern Ireland. The increase in NIC definitely had a major impact in tourism and hospitality. It really did halt investment and recruitment in those businesses. It was also difficult for businesses, because once you started to change at the lower end, there were increases all the way up. With the cost of pensions and so on, that made a big difference. Businesses have to think very carefully about recruiting people.

The Republic of Ireland has announced a budget, which we are working through at the moment, but certainly based on figures from before the budget came out, there was a difference of nearly 7% in the per-person cost. Those in Northern Ireland were paying 7% more than those in the Republic, and that makes a lot of difference when you are trying to recruit.

A lot of the costs that we are dealing with are similar across the economy. There are specific ones, like business rates for example, where there is support in the rest of the UK, but we do not have the same support here. Again, when we are looking at costs and where decisions can be made, they are divided between Westminster and the Northern Ireland Executive, which can cause a challenge.

Q68 Chair: On that note, Joanne, do you think that the tourism and hospitality sector requires more support from the UK Government than businesses in other sectors?

Dr Stuart: I do, because of the type of businesses we have. We are also very much people-focused and people-facing. Although we are utilising technology to make business efficiencies, we are never going to replace people; we would never want to do that. That is what creates the welcome for visitors, so we are an important sector for providing those jobs on a regional basis. As we start to see AI develop further, there will be people who need to look at different jobs and opportunities. That is where tourism and hospitality can step into the breach, but we have to make sure that our businesses are there and will have the capacity for investment.

Q69 Adam Jogee: Thank you both for coming this afternoon. It is good to be back here in Northern Ireland. Can you touch on the cost pressures facing the tourism and hospitality sectors in the United Kingdom and the Republic of Ireland? What do they look like? Give us a flavour of that comparison.

Dr Stuart: As Aidan set out, obviously there are the differences in VAT. It is more about food. Alcohol is charged at a higher VAT rate in the Republic of Ireland, so it does not include alcohol. We talked about wages and the differences. There is a slight difference in the minimum wage. I think the Republic of Ireland is more or less where we are now, but obviously national insurance and the minimum employer pension are both higher for businesses up here. It works out at roughly £1,800 more per employee in Northern Ireland.

Business energy prices in Northern Ireland are definitely different, whether that is for small or large users. I have included all my sources in the briefing paper. It is between 9% and 14% higher across those bands. Then we have inflation: overall consumer inflation in the UK is 3.1% and in the Republic of Ireland it is 3.7%. There are a number of different things.

One thing that I would say about VAT—I am sure Aidan agrees—is that it is very complicated and complex.

Aidan Lyons: Yes.

Dr Stuart: There are multiple different things, but we have tried to provide some information to simplify that. Does that answer your question?

Adam Jogee: And there is the inevitable competition that goes with that. If you are not that far away, you can go and spend your money there.

Dr Stuart: Yes, that is the thing. We will probably come on to talk about the pilot. The thing we have to remember with Northern Ireland is that we are promoted as the island of Ireland. People come to the island of Ireland and then decide whether to spend time in Northern Ireland or in southern Ireland. We have that competition right on our doorstep, and people can make decisions at the last minute. That is why it is really important that we are able to articulate the value and the reasons that people come to Northern Ireland. That is why the promotion budget for Tourism Northern Ireland is really important. It is obviously very difficult at the moment, because we do not have a budget in Northern Ireland. We are talking about VAT, which is important, but it is also part of a larger problem.

Adam Jogee: Thank you; that is a really helpful answer. Aidan?

Aidan Lyons: There is not too much that I can add to that, because Joanne has set it out eloquently. It speaks to her experience, in terms of her role. We see from our clients that, across Northern Ireland, there are cost pressures, whether it is from utility bills or NIC. I think that is true of all businesses. There are some tremendously successful businesses in Northern Ireland. Clearly, the hospitality and tourism sector does have some specific pressures. There is a difference in the taxation rates as far as VAT is concerned between the Republic and Northern Ireland.

Q71 Adam Jogee: Do you think that it is helpful to make a comparison between the two?

Aidan Lyons: In terms of—?

Adam Jogee: The experiences of businesses in the sector on either side of the border. It is not a trick question; I am just interested.

Aidan Lyons: It is an interesting question, and I am sure that Joanne can bring something to it. If you are a tourist, I think you choose where you are going because there are things you want to see; that drives why you go to a certain place. If you are coming from the US to the island of Ireland, you might want to spend some time in Northern Ireland—why wouldn’t you, if you wanted to go to the Giant’s Causeway and are specifically driven by that? We should make that as attractive as possible.

Q72 Simon Hoare: Dr Stuart, I want to ask about the Great British summer savings scheme—although it should really have been called the “Great United Kingdom summer savings scheme”. You will remember that it introduced a temporary reduction of 5% of VAT on children’s meals, tickets and family attractions. The Committee is keen to hear, in broad terms, your members’ experience of the scheme, and in particular how many businesses participated, whether you are able to tell us what that is as a percentage of eligible businesses, and to what extent those VAT savings were passed on directly to customers through lower prices. Did the scheme lead to increased footfall, sales or visitor numbers?

Dr Stuart: I have done a lot of work to find information from our members on this. NITA had weekly calls with our members as soon as it was announced. Detail was quite sparse and difficult to get, to begin with, but it caused a lot of implementation problems. There was not a very big lead-in time, and businesses were therefore having to change prices. Also, for that summer period, there are a lot of incentives that go out, particularly from attractions, so you get a lot of pre-bookings. A lot of time was spent understanding the refunds and that type of thing. So there was a cost.

I also worked with ALVA. It looked at the UK and did a survey of UK members, which includes members from Northern Ireland. The implementation and additional business costs were highlighted by everybody who responded—I think it had 20 responses to its survey. Everybody passed through the full discount to the customers. However, the evidence showed that there was very little additional business to what would be expected over the summer period. It also seemed to catch the tourism agencies on the hop, so there was not very much promotion of it. A lot of customers were not aware of it. Obviously, the attractions had passed that on so were able to inform them when they arrived, but it was very hard to quantify real additional footfall to what would be expected at that time of year. The visitor was receiving the saving, but there was no significant increase seen coming through to the business as additional revenue or profit.

Q73 Simon Hoare: In terms of striking the balance between a beneficial financial outcome to the business as a result of an uptick in footfall, versus the costs of administering and understanding the scheme, making refunds, doing the paperwork and so on, was it financially neutral or was there a negative cost?

Dr Stuart: It is sort of anecdotal based on a survey, but for the majority of people I have spoken to, it was a cost.

Q74 Simon Hoare: I do not mean this question to be as anti-Treasury and churlish as possibly it sounds. There was very good intention from the Treasury, but the detail was very sketchy at the time of the announcement and there was not enough lead-in time. Dr Stuart: The public were not really aware of it, so that was the challenge. We are certainly not saying that that is not something, but we think that if you are doing something like that, it has to be designed with business. This is part of the problem, and it was one of the problems that we faced with covid as well.

Businesses understand their business, and by working closer with business we can make the implementation easier and support it by having clear eligibility rules and sufficient lead-in time. We can then have co-ordinated promotion by the tourism agencies and the businesses, which we think would have a much bigger impact.

Q75 Simon Hoare: Although I do not want to be accused of leading the witness in any way, shape or form, I am going to suggest that your answer to this question will be yes.

I am not sure what answer I am going to get, but it links back to the Chair’s opening set of questions. Would my hunch be correct that in order to do what the Treasury wishes—to provide beneficial support to UK hospitality, but in the Northern Irish context here—an endemic, permanent, long-term approach to a differential on VAT for the hospitality sector, due to its permanence and ease of understanding and so on, would be a far greater benefit to NI hospitality than the scheme that we had this summer and indeed than any nascent successor schemes that Treasury Ministers may have in mind?

Dr Stuart: Any VAT reduction has to be sustained. There has been talk about a pilot, but you are talking about three to five years of that to really understand how things are feeding through—not just for the consumer, because that impact should be immediate, but to the businesses: are we seeing more investment and an increase in jobs and so on?

It is about engaging with business to ensure that we have ironed out any potential issues before starting, and about making sure the tourism agencies are also engaged so that everybody is lined up at the same time and we can get the best benefit from it.

Aidan Lyons: From a VAT perspective, for any VAT rate change there will be system changes that need to be implemented, and a determination has to be made whether to pass some or all of the savings on to the consumer. When you have a rate change for a short period, you are making a change to your systems at the start and then you have to change your systems back at the end of that pilot period. Clearly, there is a cost to doing that. It is probably easier for the larger corporates to deal with that; they may have more sophisticated systems and a bigger IT department and can therefore implement the sort of changes required. If you are a smaller business, it may be more challenging.

On the Great British summer savings scheme, there was a degree of complexity in what it did and did not apply to. It was very much aimed at families—children’s food and what have you—but from a VAT point of view, you need to understand which parts are in and which parts are out. I am a VAT adviser, so if the tax is complex that is great for me, because clients need more support. But you want it to be something that they can easily understand so they can implement those changes: what supplies are subject to the reduced rate and what supplies are outside?

You also need a lead-in period for this, as much as anything. For any IT change, you typically do not just ring up or email the IT department and say, “Can you make this change?”, because they have other things to do as well, so it needs to feed into their overall list of priorities.

It is about trying to understand that where there is a change, there is a time and cost commitment for the business to implement that change, and then it will have another, similar cost and time commitment at the end when it has to change it. It is very clear that if a reduced rate of VAT were to be introduced, you would want a good lead-in period. There would need to be a decent amount of time while that rate applies to understand the implications and whether it makes the changes that you want it to make. Then, at the end of the three years, five years or whatever it may be, you cannot leave businesses on a cliff edge, going, “Is it going to change? Is it going to go back? Is it not?” They need to know, and it feeds into their margin and has an impact as they plan for the future.

Q76 Chair: So it has to be long-term? Changing it just for the summer has no impact or little impact.

Aidan Lyons: I cannot talk to the economics, but I can certainly talk from a VAT perspective. If you were to make a change, you would want it to be over a longer period of time, because then you get a better idea of whether it is actually working.

Chair: It is very hard to measure the impact over such a short period of time.

Aidan Lyons: I think so. I think that is true of many things. It may be hard to determine the overall impact of a two-month or two-and-a-half-month sample period.
