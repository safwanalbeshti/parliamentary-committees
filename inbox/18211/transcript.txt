Examination of witnesses

Sarah Sackman and Daniel Flury.

Q46 The Chair: Welcome, Minister and your colleague, to this fourth evidence session of the Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee’s inquiry into support for jurors. We are delighted to have the Minister with us. I would be grateful if you could both introduce yourselves for the record.

Sarah Sackman: I am Sarah Sackman KC MP, and I am the Minister for Courts and Legal Services.

Daniel Flury: I am the operations director in His Majesty’s Courts and Tribunals Service.

Q47 Baroness Buscombe: Thank you for joining us today. What assessment have you made of the adequacy and consistency of current support for jurors who have distressing experiences as part of, or as a result of, their jury service?

Sarah Sackman: I shall preface what I am about to say by recognising that jury service is a crucial civic duty, and we are grateful to all the individuals from various backgrounds who undertake it.

Before addressing the important question that you raise about support, it is important to say that the majority of jurors have a positive experience. Many people that we see, both from our experience survey and anecdotally, tell us that they welcome the opportunity to participate in such an important civic duty, one that people take seriously. They end up learning a lot about our justice process and indeed our society. But you are right: the support that we give jurors through that experience—before they come to court, when they are summonsed at court and in the follow-up—is something that, I am glad to say, we are paying far more attention to than we ever have done in the past, when we just invited people to jury service and the aftercare and support were limited, to say the least. I want to express my gratitude to the committee for that.

The main things that we have done in terms of an assessment are twofold, and Daniel will add something if I have missed anything. First, we undertake a jury survey that asks people about their experience and provides the courts service with data around how we can improve our operations to make sure that they are consistent, and that the information and support that we are providing are consistently applied across the court estate.

Secondly, the committee will be aware that last year we commissioned Dr Hannah Fawcett and Dr Matt Brooks to examine current practice across 16 Crown Court centres to consider what good looks like—that was the title of the assessment—in terms of understanding juror needs and what support they needed. They looked at things like the facilities, such as the waiting room, as well as the deliberation and what support was offered to jurors. In particular, the assessment tested a structured end-of-trial intervention, enhanced jury officer training and whether a dedicated support line was of value. So we have a much richer picture than we have had for some time to understand the juror experience and what might be needed in terms of support.

Baroness Buscombe: Could we talk a bit about the Rivers Centre in Scotland, where we understand that for the past 30 years NHS specialist psychological support has been offered to those who have suffered, or who feel they have suffered, some kind of trauma through the process of being a juror. Is that something that you are looking at?

Daniel Flury: We are certainly aware of what is happening in Lothian, in the Rivers Centre, where there is an explicit offering of psychological support after a jury trial. Our approach in HMCTS is, as you probably know, to refer them either to their GP, who we think is best placed to make a clinical assessment, or to Samaritans or any other sort of charity. So, while we have lots of material to signpost those who have undertaken jury service, we do not make an explicit offering. That is something else that we are considering as part of our response to the Fawcett and Brooks report, but at present we have no intention of replicating what they do in the Rivers Centre.

Sarah Sackman: To build on that point, we are always looking to other jurisdictions, particularly those close to home, to identify what best practice looks like, as we should, but it is important to highlight that out of the Fawcett and Brooks study—which was fairly broad in scope, looking at 16 Crown Court centres—we are going to institute national minimum standards, which we have never had before. So that is an important step change in terms of consistent standards and support for jurors, irrespective of where they are in the system or in the country.

However, the study found that the uptake for the really specialised counselling service that is provided was pretty low. That is not to say that it is not of value to those who are sitting on a jury where there is traumatic content or where there are particular vulnerabilities, and of course that is right, but we found that in general terms the majority of trials did not evidence a demand for that specialist service. That does not mean jurors are not entitled to expect a decent waiting room, proper direction from both judge and jury officer, and proper information throughout the process; of course they should, and that is the national minimum standard. But it is important that we distinguish between where there really is a need—trials that involve particularly grim and awful evidence that is likely to trigger a need for additional support—versus the majority of trials, where the study showed there was not really the demand. We can continue to look at this, of course, and we will continue to see what the results from the Scottish work show.

Daniel Flury: To illustrate that point further, during the Lucy Letby trial, Manchester victim support intervened and provided support to jurors who sat on that trial. We had something similar with the David Fuller trial in Maidstone in Kent, where there was particularly harrowing evidence, although he eventually pleaded guilty so it was not required. So we can obtain support directly for very specific trials, and the judge may direct us to obtain specific support for certain trials, but these are the exception rather than anywhere near the norm.

Baroness Buscombe: I have two short follow-ups because others have questions to ask. First, some of us were lucky enough to go last week to the Old Bailey, and we had an interesting discussion with the recorder and those who manage the court. Of course in some ways the Old Bailey is exceptional in terms of the kinds of cases, including some very difficult cases. While we understood that the take-up might be quite low in terms of people needing more support, I would argue, if I may, that knowing the support is there is in itself a huge support—just knowing that you have that if you need to turn to it. Secondly, do you have a timeline for publishing minimum standards that you would like to see applied across the board?

Sarah Sackman: Those are all really good points. You are right that in many ways the Old Bailey is exceptional in terms of both the kinds of trials that are heard there and the nature of it as a Crown Court centre. I am not going to gloss over the fact that there are parts of our court estate that are not where we would want them to be. That is why we are making a more significant major investment in maintenance than we have for a very long time.

To answer your question, the national minimum standards will, as I said, set clear expectations for the information that jurors are provided with, including signposting practical support—you are right that they may not need support, but if they do then it will be clear where to get it—as well as signposting how jurors can raise concerns during the process. One might be feeling pretty resilient at certain points but then at other points in the trial feel really quite different, and that will be incorporated within the national minimum standards. Our ambition is to introduce those national minimum standards next year. That is the timeline. I should say that, while we have a pretty good idea of what we expect to be in them, the work of this committee is really well timed.

Q48 The Chair: On the question of what they might contain, you said that following the assessment you had a better idea of what is needed, clearly accepting that, even though it might not necessarily be for all jurors, some jurors will need it. To pick up the point that has just been made by Baroness Buscombe, the idea of jurors knowing what help is available is clearly advantageous. I have in front of me the main government document, which lists all the details about jury service. Nowhere in the list of about 12 headings is there any reference to support for jurors. It is contained in the document, which is encouraging, but, amazingly, you have to find it under the heading, “Discussing the trial”. That is where you find what help there is for jurors. It tells me: “If you find the trial distressing, you may be upset by the trial and want to speak to somebody privately. Speak to court staff; they will give you advice”—and in a minute we will go on to the issue of court staff’s ability to do that. But it also says: “For emotional support, speak to your GP to find out what support is available, and you can contact the Samaritans, although they cannot give advice”.

So you are told that you can go to your GP, who will try to find some help for you, but he or she cannot give it, and the Samaritans cannot give advice. That is the sole reference to support for jurors in the official detailed government document about jury service. They are given a separate handout, which does not say that the Samaritans cannot give advice but says that you can talk to them day or night, and that your GP can help you to find free counselling services. Do you think that is adequate?

Sarah Sackman: I will defer to Daniel over whether that represents the totality of the information that jurors are given, because I believe they are also sent information in the form of emails, which include a video link. There is also information, as I said, that is provided at court. Frankly, though, that may be one of the things that we need to take away from this exercise as we institute national minimum standards. By analogy, in the correspondence that we produce within the Ministry of Justice when people write about quite distressing subjects, the signposting that we provide is now routinely really quite clear. If one of the takeaways is that we need to improve that signposting then that is a valuable takeaway.

Daniel Flury: I certainly accept that the guidance is in need of some codification, if I can call it that. In some ways, this is what the new standards are about.

You should not have to look in other areas to find out the full facts on it—I accept that point—but the guidance or standards will cover information including the details of the video and so on: expenses, signposting, facilities available, reasonable adjustments, details of what to expect in court and support you will be provided with.

The Chair: I am grateful for that, and we will come on to each of those issues you have mentioned in a minute. What I am concerned about is whether or not you, first of all, accept that you need to do more highlighting, which I think you have said you agree with. Secondly, do you think that what is on offer that you are going to highlight is remotely adequate—signposting to a GP, who may help you find free counselling, and signposting to the Samaritans, who “cannot give advice”? Do you think that is adequate? However much you advertise it with huge posters and videos and everything, is it adequate?

Sarah Sackman: Can I address that in a couple of ways? First of all, we clearly do not think it is adequate; that is why we are instituting national minimum standards. If we thought the status quo was good enough, why bother? We do see that there is room for improvement, building on the evidence base that we obtained through this study. You will see that that will consist of a couple of different areas of work: work around internal monitoring of standards to make sure that we have consistency of practice everywhere, but also the ambition to produce a public-facing jurors’ charter, which will explain what every juror, irrespective of where they are in the country, can expect from His Majesty’s Courts and Tribunals Service, and what to do if those standards are not met. That is a really big step forward. There is a point around how that information is presented to jurors, which we all accept can be better. A takeaway should be what goes in that initial summons letter, as well as the information that is provided throughout, because you might not retain that letter.

The second part of your question relates to the adequacy of signposting people to their local GP or NHS 111, which has a mental health element, and the Samaritans. For the majority of jurors, it probably will be adequate to know that there is a recognition that sitting through a criminal trial can surface things which have a psychological impact, and the best ports of call are going to be dedicated charities like the Samaritans or the NHS. However, that is not to say—this is to come back to Daniel’s point—that targeted support is not going to be warranted where you have particularly traumatic subject matters.

The Chair: I will stop you there if I can, Minister, because some of the details will, I hope, emerge during some of the subsequent questions. What we are getting is a clear understanding that you accept that the signposting, whatever it is, is currently inadequate and needs to be beefed up.

Sarah Sackman: The phrase I would use is, “It can be better”, and we are going to make it better.

The Chair: That is very helpful. Before we move on to the next issue, I call Lord Anderson.

Sarah Sackman: I will just provide some data for context. In the study that was undertaken—the Fawcett and Brooks study—there was an end-of-trial intervention and a bespoke counselling service was made available to all of the jury trials that were covered within the scope of those 16 Crown Court centres. Of the 17,811 jurors that were captured by the trial, 10 took up the service. I absolutely acknowledge the point that, for those 10 who attended at least one session, that will have no doubt—I hope—had real value. I also absolutely acknowledge the point that Baroness Buscombe made earlier: that the fact of knowing that there was this bespoke counselling service and this dedicated helpline available may well have provided comfort, reassurance and resilience to many of those. It does paint a picture: 10 out of nearly 18,000 jurors. I will obviously be interested to see what the committee’s conclusions are but, while we may well need something more than is currently there, it needs to be targeted. It needs to be proportionate. The demand case for maintaining that counselling service, given its cost at a national level for all jurors, was not made out by that study. In a tight fiscal environment, I have to be really thoughtful about how we target our money. I do think that that context is important for the committee.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: You are saying, or perhaps you are still thinking about it, that the answer to that question is not Victim Support—that it might very occasionally be appropriate, as it seems to have been in the Lucy Letby trial, but you are not saying that that limited number of jurors could get the support they want or need from Victim Support?

Sarah Sackman: In the context of the Lucy Letby trial and the local context in Manchester, informed by the judge in that case presiding over that and ascertaining what they thought was necessary, I am saying that that the skill set that Victim Support—I understand in that case that a phone number was available—would provide would be of benefit to a set of jurors who were looking at a pretty distressing case. But I am not necessarily saying that that is the only way that you could support jurors. We are saying that the support that is currently available obviously takes different forms. What is going to be right for one person? One person might prefer to go to their GP; another might prefer to call the Samaritans. The point is to target support and signpost it clearly to people to know what is available to them. Many people might not have heard of Samaritans; we have in this room, but there will be some people who have not.

Daniel Flury: I will add a point on the Samaritans. We do offer a little more than just, “Here’s a number. Good luck”. We have what we call a specific referral pathway that court staff can use, which does get us through to an enhanced level of service in the Samaritans. It is a little more than just referring them to a telephone number or website.

The Chair: That is helpful to know. Perhaps if you could just write and give us a bit more detail about, that would be very helpful.

Q50 Lord Moraes: Thank you, Minister, for your time this morning; it is much appreciated. We have already alighted on the role of court staff, and we have been looking specifically at the training for them, which has already come up. Could I ask you specifically what training is available to court staff for supporting jurors?

Sarah Sackman: You can, and I am going to direct that one, if I may, to Daniel, because he will give you a fuller answer than me.

Daniel Flury: I hope the committee has seen what we call our training workbook for jury bailiffs, which sets out the skills and expectations and the role of jury officers. We can certainly send it to the committee if you have not seen that; it sets out what is required. The role has changed quite considerably over the years from one that was there principally to administer the oaths, deal with expenses, and usher jurors in and out of the building. I talk to jury officers now, and they play far more of what they call a social worker role. These are the sort of people we are looking for, especially in our court ushers as well as the jury officers. We are looking for people with good listening skills, empathy and the ability to defuse situations and deal with people who are upset. In HMCTS as well, we have recently rolled out a package of what we call trauma-informed training, which sets out how the consequences of trauma affect certain individuals and what you can do in response to that. It is our ushers and jury officers who really are at the front line of all of this. We last updated our training workbook in 2025. I am expecting to further update it, probably after the findings of this committee emerge, because we clearly can see that the role of the usher in particular has changed quite a lot and we want to help them adjust to the changed level of expectation.

Lord Moraes: It is becoming clear to the committee—we have been looking at this very closely—that, on investigation, the role of court staff is much more important than it would first seem. It is a highly sensitive role. It affects them, as we have just heard, but it also affects the jurors quite profoundly. Do you think the training is adequate? Are we identifying best practice—is that satisfactory at the moment?

Daniel Flury: I certainly think the training is adequate. I always think there is more to be done to improve the situation. The difficulty with a lot of this is that it is about people’s personalities—whether certain jury officers empathise with people and so on. But there is always room to identify and spread best practice a little further.

Can I just focus on two specific things we have done for ushers and jury officers in the last 12 months? First, we accept, as I have just mentioned, that the role has changed quite considerably over the last 10 or 15 years. We have recently upgraded them. They were at the lowest level of the Civil Service grades—what is called an administrative assistant. They have now been moved up to the next grade above. That change in grade and, of course, change in salary reflects the increased level of expectation. That has been an important step of recognising the role they play, but there is more to be done on spreading best practice and so on, as you say.

Q53 Baroness Berridge: Turning again to the enhanced support for jurors pilot, in its evaluation there was no comparison to existing best models of practice. We are fortunate to be in a situation now where we know how to support people, whether emergency service workers, journalists, or people in NGOs reading accounts of torture going through secondary trauma. Sarah Sackman: The evaluation and the whole study was conducted by two highly experienced academics, who took into account a large dataset and conducted a structured assessment. If there are any omissions or failure to go wider, ultimately they looked at what they looked at. We at the Ministry of Justice and HMCTS—I will bring in Daniel here if he wants to come in—are always considering what best practice looks like and looking at other jurisdictions to see what they do and what we might learn from them. Baroness Berridge: You keep making reference to “targeted and proportional”. Sarah Sackman: We do look at that. The second thing Lord Leveson talked about was efficiencies. We have to make the system more efficient. We have published our response to the Leveson review and are accepting a programme of work around efficiencies. You will have heard me talk about the video remand hearings and about a case progression officer in every Crown Court centre. We are doing short-trial courts, sometimes known as blitz courts, and you might have seen them at the Bailey on your visit. There are a host of measures that we are doing to make the system work more efficiently.

The third aspect is the most pertinent one for today. The IRCC concluded that without structural reform we would not bear down on these delays. So are we making progress? We are, in areas 1 and 2—we are making progress in so far as the Bill has come from the Commons and is now coming to you for very important scrutiny. However, we will not get the backlog in our Crown Courts down unless we look at the whole system and ask ourselves: where do cases need to be heard? The most serious ones must always be reserved for a jury trial, but cases that do not need a jury trial, and which can be heard just as fairly in a magistrates’ court, should be retained there. That is the central premise of the Courts and Tribunals Bill, and I hope the Bill will get on the statute book. When we have all three levers, I think we can get the backlog down—that is what our modelling shows.

The Chair: Can I just ask the sort of knotty question that people might ask? You might well succeed in getting the Crown Court backlog down, but are you not going to increase the backlog in the magistrates’ courts?

Sarah Sackman: You are right to ask the question, which I have asked of our officials who are doing the planning, because we do not want to divert the problem from one place to another. The way that we avoid that is twofold. The starting point is that the magistrates’ court is a more efficient jurisdiction. A triable either-way case, which could be heard in the Crown or in the magistrates’, is heard four times faster in the magistrates’. It is an efficient jurisdiction. It has a caseload, but it does not have a backlog anything like as bad as the Crown Court’s.

The second thing that we have to do, and we are doing it, is to increase the capacity of the magistrates’ court, and we are doing that in a number of ways. The committee will know about the magistrate recruitment campaign. Our objective is to have recruited an additional 7,000 in order to get up to 21,000 magistrates, which is where we were in 2014, by the end of this Parliament. We are recruiting more legal advisers and district judges. We have to expand the capacity of the magistrates’ court in order to cope with the additional demands that we are placing on it, but I am pleased to report, from the stats that we published just a few days ago, that our recruitment programme, which we have reformed and invested in—we have increased the marketing budget fourfold, and we have a team working on the uplift programme—is on track. By the time I hope the reforms kick in, the magistrates’ court will be ready to receive that additional workload. We cannot turn on the reforms unless that is ready, and we will not do that, but I am confident that we can be ready.

Q62 The Chair: What discussions have you had with HMPPS about the impact of basically more trials overall throughout the whole system taking place, and therefore the increased pressure on prisons and the Probation Service?

Sarah Sackman: As you can imagine, that has been a really important part of the discussion. I was asked earlier about an impact assessment, and what you are asking about is detailed in the impact assessment. The effect of being more efficient and getting through more trials is that you increase demand and pressure on the prison system, which, as we know, is already under huge pressure. So those impacts are complex but they have been modelled to a really high degree, and that is all set out in the impact assessment.
