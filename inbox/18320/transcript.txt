Witnesses: Priya Dogra and Geoff Cooper.

Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Select Committee. Today we are holding a one-off session on the work of Channel 4. I am pleased to introduce our panel. We have Priya Dogra, the Chief Executive of Channel 4, and Geoff Cooper, the Chair of Channel 4. Welcome to you both.

Before we begin, for those watching online, the Committee has been asked to inform viewers that members may occasionally use their mobile phones during the session. This is to communicate with the rest of the Committee, to check timings and sometimes to look up little bits of information. It is all to do with Committee work. I remind members to declare any interests before asking questions. I will start and refer members to my registered interests.

Priya, I will start with you. Channel 4 “Dispatches” played a part in exposing Russell Brand’s crimes in bringing them to public notice, but some of those crimes happened at Channel 4. We hear a lot about the bravery of the women who came forward and the journalists who brought this to the nation’s attention. Looking back, Alex Mahon had to apologise to a former Channel 4 member of staff, who was referred to as Bella by Ros Urwin in her newspaper article, in 2024, because her complaint that Russell Brand had sexually assaulted her was not properly investigated twice, once in 2009 and again in 2023 after the “Dispatches” documentary. Do you think that Channel 4 has done as much as it could to not only expose Russell Brand’s crimes but also to protect and investigate those that happened under its own roof?

Priya Dogra: Thank you, Dame Caroline, and good morning to everyone on the Committee. This is the topic that I think the industry is grappling with as a whole and I will start by saying I want to express my dismay and frustration that we end up in these cycles as a media industry. This is not what we want to be as, as an industry. We want to be known for the positive impact we have on creativity, culture, society and the economy and not these stories that have dominated the airwaves over the last week or so.

You are right, we have talked about the bravery of the women coming forward in the “Dispatches” programme and we have talked about the bravery of the women being cross-examined on the stand. You have to applaud that, as well as the perseverance of the journalists at “Dispatches” and The Sunday Times. It took them four years to have that investigation come to light. The reason is that the women go through huge personal risk in telling their stories and often women only come out when they know others are coming out because there is some safety in numbers.

That is the issue that we are fundamentally grappling with in the industry. We had a conversation yesterday convened by the Secretary of State across the industry to talk about how we prevent these incidents from happening in the first instance. I think that is what all of us are here to think about. One of the things that we talked about is how you identify patterns early.

We have a Speak Up facility that had existed in the past but was strengthened under my predecessor Alex Mahon, particularly after the “Dispatches” investigation came to light and on the basis of the complaints that Bella, a former employee, made. Her complaint was not investigated properly in 2009 and it should have been. It was not escalated properly. Alex apologised for that and that apology stands and it should not have happened. Since then we have strengthened the Speak Up facility. It is anonymous, run by a company called Safecall, where every call handler has 25 years of experience. Those complaints are escalated to me and reported to the board regularly.

Chair: Priya, can I interrupt you? You are talking about the 2009 complaint. After the “Dispatches” documentary, Bella, who by that stage was a previous member of your staff, got in touch with Channel 4 and tried to raise her complaint again. She described that internal investigation as shambolic and a whitewash. This is on the basis of a Channel 4 documentary that she tried to raise the alarm and she said she fell through the cracks. She had to keep repeating it over and over again. How confident are you that others who tried to come forward were all picked up or were there others who fell through the cracks and were never found?

Priya Dogra: This is a really important question. Since that time we have strengthened the Speak Up facility. Initially, when Bella made her complaint in 2023 it was not handled properly and it is almost unforgivable that that happened twice. Since that time, beginning under Alex and continuing, we have strengthened Speak Up to show up on every call sheet. It is available to our employees. I make it known when I talk about events, particularly the Brand conviction, that Speak Up is a facility that is available. The board asks us regularly to report the incidents that come up through Speak Up and it is prominent across all of our productions. We make it a requirement through our supplier code of behaviours.

As you know, we are registered with CIISA and we have funded it since its beginning. We talked yesterday about how CIISA can be the backstop. If people don’t want to come to Speak Up because it is a Channel 4 service—it is meant to be anonymous and neutral—CIISA can be the neutral industry backstop. I mentioned to the Secretary of State and Jen Smith yesterday that we will now make CIISA registration mandatory across all of our production companies.

Chair: We will come to CIISA in a minute. This all sounds like you are tightening up the act, but Bella was not a member of staff at the time she made her complaint and that is why she thinks she fell through the cracks. She knows of others that tried to make a complaint at the same time and their complaints were mishandled as well. How can you reassure us that your complaints system works for those who are not just serving members of staff or freelancers but those who have worked with you in the past? As you say, sometimes these stories take a long time to percolate and for people to come back with their complaint.

Priya Dogra: I am horrified to learn that there were others that I am not aware of who made complaints at that time and feel they have fallen through the cracks. If they have, I urge them to contact us through Speak Up if they want to stay anonymous. That is not acceptable; it should not be. In the instance of Bella, I am not sure we can apologise enough as an organisation for putting her through that twice. We have apologised in public in the past and in private. She met members of the prior board, the chair and the deputy chair, and we have taken real action on the back of that. An internal review was conducted, supervised by the ethics committee of the board. Some of the recommendations, including strengthening Speak Up as well as supporting CIISA, are part of the things that we have implemented. We have tried to make that facility as prominent as we can, internally and externally.

Chair: What would be the process these days if a former member of staff wanted to come back and get in touch with you? I spoke to Bella on Friday and in her case she had to go through the main switchboard and it was not properly handled. She was passed from pillar to post and literally her case fell through the gaps. She had to keep coming backwards and backwards and backwards to tell her story. It should not be that way. People should be heard at the beginning.

Priya Dogra: I completely agree with you. It should not be that way, particularly when someone is reliving their trauma by telling the story over and over. People come into the organisation in one of a couple of ways. The first, as I mentioned, is Speak Up. A lot of people also come in through our viewer enquiries line. If they come in through viewer enquiries, generally that complaint, if it is appropriate, will be directed to Speak Up, and there are others who have written to me directly or other members of my staff. All of them will be handled by directing them to the right individuals within the organisation.

Chair: One of the other issues that we saw during the Brand trial was that his behaviour was in some cases protected, in other cases enabled, and certainly witnessed by some of those around him. According to Bella, Channel 4 continued to employ a man who had allegedly witnessed the incident with Brand in 2018. How confident are you that you are not still employing people that have showed a demonstrable lack of ability and willingness to speak up on issues like this over the years?

Priya Dogra: I will address two different points there, if you do not mind. The first thing I want to address is the power imbalance that exists in our industry. Power imbalances are not new and they are not unique to our industry, but in media we create stars. That is what TV and film does and they can appear larger than life. Another feature of media is the backbone of this industry is freelancers and they generally work on short-term contracts. That exacerbates the power imbalance. One of the things that we are focused on doing is creating more resources for freelancers. We created Action for Freelancers, in collaboration with the Film and TV Charity, that enables them to understand their rights and enables more consistent hiring, more advice on hiring, mental health resources and others. In addition, we have worked with Talent Trust on some of our productions, which effectively does anonymous surveys through the course of productions so you are not waiting until the end to figure out if something happened. You can spot patterns early on.

Finally, one of the more important ways that we address the power imbalance is we will have to show as an industry that commercial interests do not override our principles on important things. We will walk away from a big show, a big personality because it does not align with our values. I had to do that on a recent show and others will have to make similar decisions in the future, but we have to show as an industry that no one is bigger than the values we want to hold.

Coming in particular to the individual that you mentioned, who Bella had named, who was further employed by Channel 4 back in the day—as the Committee knows, this precedes me, as I have been in the role for about eight months—my understanding is that there were no findings at that stage of even Russell Brand’s behaviour. There was rumour and speculation but there were no real findings. That has now changed. This person has stepped away for a period of time from the production company. At the time of the inquiry in 2022, enquiries were made. The investigation that was conducted under the ethics committee reviewed something like 31,000 documents and engaged with 88 people, including 60 interviews. It found that at that point there was no reason to assume that anybody at Channel 4 knew of the acts that Russell Brand had committed or that they were aware of the details of the specific incident. Remember, it was not properly handled back in 2009. That is where things stand right now. To my knowledge, we have not worked with the individual since.

Chair: With respect, it was not properly handled in 2009, that is self-evident, but I do not think it was properly handled in 2023 either. To what extent do you think it is necessary for Channel 4 to have an independent inquiry about this whole issue, about who, what and when, what the issues were with people being able to report instances of inappropriate behaviour so that you can draw a line under this and you can know for sure that the systems and processes are now in place that will root this out? Nothing you have told me makes me confident that you can be 100% sure that staff working on the productions commissioned by Channel 4 can feel 100% safe about raising concerns and know that they will be listened to.

Priya Dogra: I will say a couple of things in response. The first is that staff can be 100% confident that when issues are raised, we will take them seriously. We have shown that and I would be happy for our Speak Up facility to be properly audited. As I said, it has been assessed by the board. We report to the board regularly, but that is something we take very seriously, particularly—

Chair: How do you know staff are confident? Just because you have something in place does not mean that people feel confident using it. One of the issues we hear time and time again about reporting bad behaviour in the workplace is that people are afraid to speak up because they worry what it will do to their own careers. When I spoke to Bella, she told me that people said to her, “Millions of people would want your job”. It is all very well having systems and processes in place. You can say 100% that the systems are there but you cannot 100%, with your hand on your heart, say to us as a Committee that 100% of people feel confident using it.

Priya Dogra: I agree with that. I made the comment earlier that we believe our Speak Up facility is world class. It is anonymous and people should trust it. If they do not, there are other ways for people to reach out and, as we have talked about, CIISA is an important backstop in that instance. There are other ways for people to come forward. I encourage people who have things to say, particularly the people you mentioned who felt they had slipped through the cracks in 2023, to come forward and know that their complaints will be taken seriously.

Chair: You do not intend to do an inquiry about incidents that happened in 2023 and 2009? You are confident that that has been dealt with now?

Priya Dogra: The inquiry has happened and it was very comprehensive, done under the supervision of the board’s ethics committee. I think where we are focused now is—

Chair: That is not independent, is it?

Priya Dogra: The ethics committee is not independent. It is a sub-committee of the board, but the review was conducted by a member of the legal and compliance team who had joined in 2017. As I said, it was a very thorough review, which the board oversaw, as is its right to do. Where we are focused now is making sure, exactly to your point, that the facilities and resources we have in place continue to be strong in the future, we continue to improve them and we work with the industry as a whole to talk about not just the way that people come to us with complaints but how we protect welfare on a set. All of those are things that I am focused on now.

We have had Ofcom’s revised guidance, as you saw. We have had a robust set of recommendations from Lorraine Heggessey on productions going forward and we are looking at how we implement those. We have spoken to Ofcom about bringing the PSBs together under its auspices and talking about implementing some of these. You are right, I cannot be 100% confident people will use our facility but we want to people to know that, first, we take them seriously, secondly, we are looking to the future to improve and evolve our standards and, thirdly, as I said, we have shown and demonstrated that no show or no person will be bigger than the values we want to hold as a channel and as an industry.

Alison Hume: I would like to draw members’ attention and your attention to my previous career as a screenwriter and a producer. I also owned an independent production company, which is no longer trading. I am a member of the Writers’ Guild of Great Britain.

Priya, you mentioned that Channel 4 is supporting CIISA. The BBC and ITV have come out and said that they expect the companies they commission from to align with CIISA standards. Will you make sure that all of the production companies that work for you sign up to CIISA going forward?

Priya Dogra: Yes, that is the commitment I made to the Secretary of State and Jen Smith from CIISA yesterday about going forward. We have encouraged the production companies we work with to follow the CIISA standards. Going forward, we will make it mandatory for companies we work with to be members of CIISA.

Alison Hume: Thank you for that. What more can Channel 4 do? Signing up to CIISA and putting Speak Up on the call sheets is good, but we know that it is a highly competitive industry with a lot of young freelancers who came in and are not necessarily part of a union; this might be their first experience in television and there are the power imbalances, as you have mentioned. There is often bad behaviour in production companies as well, so they need to be able to come to you. Are you able to look at a scheme where there would be a point of contact in Channel 4 for that person so that you are looking out for the most vulnerable people in the industry? We have to stop the abuse in its tracks before it starts.

Priya Dogra: I completely agree with you and there are a couple of ways that we are proposing to do that. One, as I mentioned, is looking at expanding the work of Talent Trust across more productions, because it provides anonymised reporting through the course of production. Of course our commissioners are involved and they are on set at certain times, but they are not producers and are not on set all of the time. The second is that we have appointed a welfare executive, Cheryl Woodcock. One of the recommendations that Lorraine made was a clearer distinction between welfare and commissioning, such that complaints related to welfare will now first go to Cheryl instead of the commissioning editor. We are trying to separate those two things to distinguish between editorial and welfare.

I agree with you about the vulnerability of freelancers, particularly in a constrained environment. I take Dame Caroline’s comment that somebody would say, “A million people would want your job” in a constrained time for the industry and, therefore, somebody might not speak up. I take that very seriously and it is our responsibility to show that when people speak up, the repercussions are not on them, but on the person they spoke up against.

Alison Hume: Thank you for that. You said that you are working with an organisation that would be dealing with anonymised reports. That is not the point. The point I was making is that young freelancers have a point of contact with Channel 4 and it is not just them ringing up Speak Up on the call sheet.

Priya Dogra: I think that is fair. We have found that some complaints need to be anonymised but we will make Cheryl’s role more prominent if it helps with the issue that they need to reach out to someone directly within Channel 4.

Alison Hume: I want to talk about inclusive working environments. That is also part of the work of CIISA. We have very low levels of crew members with visible and invisible disabilities. Can you tell me what Channel 4 is doing to make sure that you are covering all bases for crew members who may have additional needs?

Priya Dogra: As you know, inclusion and diversity are at the very heart of Channel 4. It is reflected in our workforce and we stand out in stats within our own workforce for women, disability and LGBTQ+ staff. We have a scheme by which we support diverse indies in the first instance and smaller diverse indies. We support them through a fund. Our supplier code of conduct, as well as our code of behaviours, lays out exactly not just how companies that contract with us are expected to behave but that individuals within those companies are signing up to behave in a certain way. That includes accommodations for staff disabilities.

Alison Hume: Does that work with CIISA? Can you bring the two together? It seems like you are running two different things there.

Priya Dogra: CIISA is a group of standards. The internal supplier code of behaviour that most companies would have, not just ours—most production companies and most broadcasters would have their own standards—align with CIISA but are perhaps more detailed, more bespoke, depending on the specific needs. They are not in conflict, they are in alignment.

Chair: CIISA, although not mandatory, asks organisations to register with it to commit to three years. Is Channel 4’s commitment for three years?

Priya Dogra: At the moment we have made a one-year commitment to CIISA. The reason is not that we don’t believe in CIISA at all. As I said, we are now making CIISA mandatory across our production ecology. We wanted to engage with CIISA on some of the specifics of the year 2 and year 3 activities they want to do. We have questions about data protection and ability to compel witnesses or ability to compel companies to release certain kinds of data. Those are questions that we have engaged on with Jen Smith and Baroness Kennedy and we will continue to do so.

Chair: You said that you require the partners that you work with to align to CIISA standards as well.

Priya Dogra: I have just agreed with my staff that we will make it mandatory going forward that they are registered with CIISA. We have always encouraged them to align to the standards but we will make registration mandatory.

Chair: How does that impact your relationship with Disney?

Priya Dogra: In what sense, I am sorry?

Chair: Are Disney fully signed up to the CIISA standards?

Priya Dogra: I was talking about the production companies that we work with as opposed to distribution partners. We are talking about the kinds of environments where CIISA standards would be aligned. Disney productions are different. We have an agreement with Disney to distribute our content.

Chair: Do you know whether Disney is signed up?

Priya Dogra: I do not know whether Disney is signed up.

Liz Jarvis: Geoff, does Channel 4 use NDAs?

Geoff Cooper: I will defer to Priya on the detail of that but I know it is a topic that is across industries, not just this industry but other industries. I think that NDAs as a blanket are the wrong approach but NDAs have specific provisions that allow for them to be adopted in sensible circumstances.

Priya Dogra: I can pick up. Thank you, Geoff. We don’t use blanket NDAs. There will be confidentiality clauses in certain contracts and in staff settlement agreements when people are leaving the company. We have now implemented, over the past year or longer than that, that there are pretty large caveats to that confidentiality. We have to maintain confidentiality of company information, but if you want to report misbehaviour, misconduct, all of those are permissible because they should be. In prior contracts where those caveats were not available, we have made clear that we will not be enforcing them but, as I understand it, the law has changed anyway. We do have clauses but they are not intended to be gagging orders in any way. If somebody has seen misconduct or misbehaviour, they are open to report that.

Liz Jarvis: In June 2025 Baroness Kennedy, who is the chair of CIISA, called on Channel 4 to lodge an independent investigation into the use of confidentiality clauses in settlement agreements, following a Deadline analysis of 61 Channel 4 settlement deals signed by ex-employees between 2017 and 2021. Deadline said Channel 4 paid out £5 million to these individuals to prevent complaints escalating. They sound like NDAs to me.

Priya Dogra: I do not know the details of those settlement agreements, I am afraid, but if I understand what those agreements were, as staff leave the company they receive normal notice periods and other settlements and in return there is a settlement agreement signed. It is not a blanket NDA. You are correct in saying that prior to the last few years some of those clauses were quite broad. We have made sure that the latest round of settlement agreements that we are asking staff to sign when they leave contain standard non-disparagement clauses but they are not in place to not allow people to speak up when it is necessary.

Liz Jarvis: How many confidentiality clauses in settlement agreements have been signed since 2021? How many involved allegations of sexual harassment or misconduct?

Priya Dogra: I am afraid I will have to get back to you on that. I do not know the details.

Liz Jarvis: What about the production companies that you use; do they use NDAs?

Priya Dogra: The production companies will use contracts, again not blanket NDAs. They will use contracts between the production companies and the people that they are contracting with. We are not party to those. We are commissioners, so we are not party to the contracts between the production company and the talent. Those may or may not include those sorts of clauses. My understanding is that the law has changed. You cannot limit someone’s ability to speak up against misbehaviour and misconduct regardless of the details in a contract. Part of what we are doing through Action for Freelancers is making sure that people have the information that they cannot be held to those clauses in the contract whether they are old or new.

Liz Jarvis: Could a junior on a set raise a complaint about a star now without fear of losing their job and would they be taken seriously?

Priya Dogra: They would be taken seriously. The reason I cannot say 100% in every instance is because it would be irresponsible for me to say that. What I can do is set the standards, set the tone, make sure that the facilities are available, make sure that my staff and the production companies understand the repercussions of not complying and understand the kinds of culture that I am trying to set and we are trying to set as an industry. That does not mean that someone inadvertently may not get the treatment that they deserve. If someone is feeling that, I encourage them to come to me directly.

Liz Jarvis: One of the things that struck me watching the “Dispatches” documentary again after the sentencing of Russell Brand was that “Big Brother” was a huge moneymaker for Channel 4 back in 2005 and 2006. It generated £68 million. How much did the fact that it was generating so much money for Channel 4, which was absolutely vital to Channel 4, mean that the complaints that were made—for example, by “Big Brother’s Efourum” crew members who told more senior members of the production team at Endemol that they had concerns about Brand’s behaviour and these complaints were allegedly passed on to Channel 4—had a part to play in that it was just too much to lose for Channel 4?

Priya Dogra: I cannot speculate on the decisions that were made at the time. I was not there, I was not involved, so I cannot speculate on what people knew and how the decisions were made. What I can say is the internal review found that decisions that were made in 2009 and later were not—people were not aware, there was no hard evidence of Russell Brand’s activities that he has now been convicted for.

I don’t condone any of this behaviour, I do want to say that. You have seen some of the clips on social media this week, as I have, and I find that humour and the cult of personality around Russell Brand horrendous and distasteful. I look at the jokes as the lowest form of humour because they are made at the expense of someone else’s physical safety. I do not believe it should have been acceptable then and I certainly don’t think it is acceptable now. I say that not just as a woman but also as a CEO. We can, we need to and we will do better, but I cannot speculate on the decisions that were made at the time. I can only say the decisions I make now will be based on values.

Geoff Cooper: Can I come in and add something, please? I think the point you raise is an important one. Commercial revenues, commercial drive, personal targets, success and how that is eventually measured in any organisation, forms part of whether someone acts properly when a complaint is raised and what they do with it. Certainly in the case of the Russell Brand investigation, it seems to be implied that something went wrong there. I think the fix to that is to have a culture throughout the organisation that ensures that people operate with the right kind of values.

That is a matter of great importance to the board. One of the actions that I am taking right now and is under way is the appointment to the board of new non-executive directors who have background and experience in the media industry in production and commissioning. They are board members who have had direct face-to-face awareness of the kind of issues it can raise. We need that for other reasons as well as we evolve our content strategy but one of the advantages, when questions like this come up, is that we will have suitably experienced people.

Liz Jarvis: Are you confident that the culture will change?

Geoff Cooper: I am, because the board has prioritised it as something we want to look at. In fact, we have a Channel 4 board meeting tomorrow and our current chief people officer is due to come to the board to give her thoughts on how we build and sustain a healthy culture in Channel 4.

Can I add a couple of things? It is all very well us saying we want the organisation to have these values. The best set of values in any organisation is derived not by top-down direction but from people in the organisation saying what is healthy. You then have to invest in it to sustain it and you have to make sure it is constantly reinforced. At our remuneration committee, where we considered our longer-term approach to incentives throughout the organisation, we asked management to go away and think about how a personal multiplier or detractor that would apply to someone’s bonus could be related to the values they exhibit when they are at work.

That is not a complete answer. There will be many other aspects of architecture that we need to put in place but I just want to reassure the Committee that from the board’s perspective this is crucially important and we see culture as a key foundation to getting the right behaviours. It starts from the top. It is not something the board can just give to management and say, “Okay, off you go and deal with it”. It has to be elevated as a major strategic question.

Liz Jarvis: What about ensuring that the production companies that you work with are also maintaining that culture?

Geoff Cooper: I think we would seek to ask management’s view on how they can do that. Our primary concern is that we have that established in our organisation now and concurrently we should be looking at our impact throughout the industry.

Priya Dogra: I will add a couple of things. Our outgoing chief people officer let me know earlier this year that she wanted to leave and I have been in a recruiting process. What I prioritised in the recruiting process was exactly that, somebody who had driven cultural change in an organisation. We will announce this person next week but I am delighted with who we have chosen. This person has literally written the book on cultural change and will help implement what I want the culture of Channel 4 to be going forward. As Geoff said, the culture is not something I can mandate or dictate. I can set a tone or a set of values. The culture is how you recruit people, how you reward people, how you reward performance and what you promote, and that is what we are trying to do. It is how you do that across the institutions in the organisation.

The question about how it relates to production companies is a really good one because that is part of our footprint for how we have impact on the organisation and the ecosystem, and we take that very seriously. Our success is measured in some way by the success of the thriving production sector. These are difficult times in general and the production sector should be the envy of the world. The way in which we do that is ensuring that the values that we hold are the values that we expect the production companies to hold. We work closely with them on decisions that relate to casting. As we think about diversity and representation, we work with them on the protocols that we expect them to hold to on set so that welfare is paramount. Our commissioners will be in touch with them regularly.

We are commissioners but when you hear me speak about production companies, I generally say “we” because we do these things together. We do not just outsource a show and then show up at the end and say, “How did you do? Were there any issues on the set?” That is not how we work. That is how we reinforce our values day to day because as our people absorb them, they get incorporated into the ways in which we work with all of the companies that we partner with.

Nigel Huddleston: May I refer honourable members to my registered interests?

Priya, Channel 4, like most media companies, appears to have rigorous processes and procedures in place for reporting and investigating complaints and abuse that has happened. My concern is who are the dodgy blokes, who are the potential predators, who should be on a watchlist? What are you doing to proactively make sure that abuse does not happen in the first place? Do you have a watchlist?

Priya Dogra: This is tricky because, as you know and you are aware, there are informal watchlists floating around the industry, not just our industry but others. It is fraught with a lot of concerns, not least of which is data protection and the fact that there may be some real misconduct on this list and there may be other examples that is not. It is entirely fraught, but I can say that we do everything that we can to make sure that the people we hire hold up our standards. We make proper enquiries, we ask around. It is a small industry and you hear about how someone has behaved on set. You make the enquiries you can, you use your judgment to understand what you can trust and what you cannot, but we are not an investigating body. It is not for us to investigate and adjudicate on complaints that come through. Ultimately, it is a matter of judgment that we use regularly.

Nigel Huddleston: My instinct would be if in doubt they’re out, don’t even deal with them. I am afraid, Chair, we have come across this way too often and it is not unique to Channel 4; it is not unique to media. We have seen it in sport, politics and business. Everybody knows the wrong’uns. There is the gossip out there, the rumour mill that you do not want your daughter being in a room with that person. We have all heard it but then years later we find out that there are complaints. I think this goes beyond culture and values, important as that is. We need more rigorous processes in place in advance to identify a spot and to warn people about these potential predators to the greatest extent possible. Priya Dogra: When we cast people, whether it is members of the public or others, we do background checks and home visits, and we chat to friends and family. We try to build as robust a picture as possible. It relies to some extent on people telling the truth, which they do not always do, and we try to build guardrails around that. In the instance of actual stars, you use the antennae that you have and the connections in the industry to have a sense of how people have behaved on other productions and whether there is any noise.
