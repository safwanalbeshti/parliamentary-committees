Examination of Witnesses

Christa Rottensteiner, Vicky Tennant and Dr Patrick Burland.

Chair: Welcome to the second panel of this session. We are delighted to welcome Dr Patrick Burland from the International Organization for Migration, Christa Rottensteiner from the International Organization for Migration, and Vicky Tennant from UNHCR UK. Welcome to you all.

I will start with the first question. The government package of policies aims to deter arrivals in the UK and speed up removals of people with no legal right to be in the country. We will come on to specific aspects of the proposals in later questions, but what are your views about the package taken as a whole?

Vicky Tennant: First of all, thank you for the invitation to give evidence. As the UN Refugee Agency, we have a very specific mandated responsibility for refugee protection and solutions, including specifically in relation to the refugee convention, so we have approached the legislation very much from the perspective of its alignment with the refugee convention.

We have shared written observations both on the restoring order and control package, but also on the legislation specifically. Of course, we would absolutely agree that this needs to be looked at as a whole, and also in the context of other measures that are being pursued by the Government in this space.

I should kick off by saying that we as UNHCR very much share the Government’s concern around the dangerous journeys that we see asylum seekers, refugees and migrants making to the UK. We also appreciate the emphasis, and share the concern, about there being a need to shore up public confidence in the asylum system and to reinforce support for refugee protection.

We very much welcome the explicit commitments that the Government have made to upholding their obligations under international refugee law, and we see a number of elements of the reform agenda that are very much aligned with the objective. For example, we welcome the work that is under way and has been undertaken in addressing the backlog in first-instance decision-making. There has been a lot of progress in that regard. Improving the quality of decision-making is something that UNHCR is working closely with the Government on.

We welcome the emphasis on returns of those who are found not to be in need of international protection. We see that as very much part of the integrity of a functioning asylum system. Of course, voluntary returns are also something that is important to pursue. We welcome all the work—and this was very explicit in the restoring order and control statement—on safe, regular routes, the reopening of the UK resettlement scheme and the launch of the new sponsorship routes. We would like to really see that work intensify and be brought to scale. We would also invite that family reunification also be given priority in terms of providing safe and legal routes.

The Government have very much also leaned into working with international partners, including in France and in Europe. The UK-France pilot was one important example of that. We would like to see more investment upstream that is really addressing the reasons why refugees are moving onwards and providing real alternatives.

We have concerns around the Bill. You have asked very specifically about whether this will deter irregular arrivals. There is limited evidence, from our perspective, that some of the policy options that are being pursued are likely to have an impact on decision-making by migrants and asylum seekers moving along the routes, but many of these measures are very significant in nature, and would substantially impact the quality of the protection that is provided in the UK to those who are found to be refugees. Those are the areas, really, where we have most concern.

We will talk about this in more detail, but we are concerned about those measures that affect the quality and the durability of the protection that is given to refugees who have been recognised as refugees. The issue of repayment of asylum support costs is something that we have commented on. I have mentioned the suspension of family reunion; we have concerns about that.

We have concerns about the temporary status, with some refugees remaining on a 30-month period of leave with regular reviews, and what that means in terms of uncertainty, the ability of people in that situation to integrate in the UK and the extended wait for settlement and access to naturalisation. We know that some people will manage to shift to the more advantageous work and study routes, but we are particularly concerned about those who will not be able to.

We are worried about these points because, in some respects, we think these measures are not aligned with the letter and spirit of the convention and because they potentially undermine the Government’s own commitments and objectives on integration, self-sufficiency and contribution.

The second element that we are concerned about is the more restrictive regime that we see being pursued in relation to refugees who have arrived irregularly. The reality is that safe and regular routes for refugees are extremely limited and many refugees arrive without documentation. The element that we see becoming part of this overall policy package that is problematic really seeks to restrict access to certain rights or make duration of stay and access to naturalisation more difficult for people who have arrived irregularly. There was an amendment along those lines introduced this morning that we can come back to.

The third area that we have concerns about is the area relating to the new appeals body. We have encouraged that a number of elements of that package be reconsidered.

The Chair: Does the International Organization for Migration broadly agree? Do you have anything to add?

Christa Rottensteiner: Thank you very much for inviting me. I agree with UNHCR on the importance of having a well-managed system and the risks if one does not exist.

IOM records migrant deaths, and it is very sad that this year already 29 people have died on their way from mainland Europe to the UK. When migration is managed effectively, we can see how beneficial it is for the countries of destination and origin and the migrants themselves. IOM works very closely with the UK Government to facilitate safe and orderly migration. I agree very much with Vicky around the importance of safe and legal routes, but they need to be part of something much bigger to address the whole migration route and this issue of dangerous journeys.

IOM’s observations on the Bill have focused very much on Part 5 and the modern slavery provisions. That is because of IOM’s mandate. We support in the UK adult survivors of modern slavery and we also analyse NRM data to identify trends and make policy recommendations.

We recognise that the UK has a leading role on modern slavery and has a very well-functioning system, but we think that the provisions of the Bill that focus on modern slavery risk having very negative unintended consequences for the victims, including UK nationals, who represent the largest nationality group in the NRM.

We can get on to this in more detail, but I just wanted to say from the outset that we are concerned about Clauses 41 to 44. We think they will make it more difficult for victims to be identified and supported. We think this risks undermining not only victim protection but also all the efforts to hold traffickers to account.

We also believe that the clauses are not necessary because many of the areas are already covered in existing legislation. They will have a minimal impact on achieving the intended policy outcomes. There are also questions around legal compatibility, and we heard some of these in the previous session.

Baroness Hamwee: We have recently had confirmation from the Government of their plans for earned settlement, which are not uncontroversial. I know that there is more detail that will have to come. As I understand it, refugees will be required to wait—you have mentioned it, Vicky—30 years before they can apply for settlement.

Can you tell us how this compares with other countries’ requirements on both time period and the other aspects, one of which must be fees? Would this be compatible with our obligations under the refugee convention, including not to penalise refugees for—I will use the word that you used, Vicky—irregular entry to the UK.

Vicky Tennant: Certainly, we know that the Government took inspiration to some extent for the package of reforms from the Danish model. I do not pretend to be an expert on the Danish model, but I have certainly taken advice from colleagues who do have that expertise.

Clearly, there are a range of possibilities for refugees under the proposed earned settlement model. There may be some who will be able to move into the work and study route relatively quickly and who may not be subject to the prolonged wait for settlement linked to irregular arrival. For those people, the route may be shorter, but there will clearly be some who will not fall in that category and could end up waiting for as long as 30 years.

That would certainly make the UK an outlier. For example, Denmark did indeed introduce this temporary form of refugee status a few years ago. They still have a route to settlement, which I believe is eight years, and it can be reduced to four years for those who meet certain employment, income and language requirements. Comparing on that particular element, it does seem that the UK would be moving beyond what Denmark has done.

The other element that I would flag in relation to Denmark is that they indeed introduced these regular reviews. What they have found is that in practice they very rarely result in status being revoked because it is very unusual for the circumstances to have changed sufficiently in the country of origin for refugees to be able to return safely. A lot of time and investment of administrative capacity that goes into those regular reviews, but it does not tend to have the outcomes that might have been envisaged.

Our concern, as I have said, is that it places refugees in this situation of constant uncertainty. Indeed, in Denmark some of the commentary on the situation is that it has had an impact on refugees’ ability to put down roots, invest in their own education and employment and contribute fully to society. To us, that seems a bit out of alignment with the Government’s own aspirations in that regard.

Australia moved away from temporary protection and back towards more robust pathways to permanent residency. That was due to concerns not just about the human impact, which I have talked about, but the administrative burden and cost of constantly reviewing status and reassessing protection claims.

It is sometimes said to us that the refugee convention is about providing a temporary protection for refugees. That is right, but the reason it is envisaged as temporary is that it should be a pathway to a solution; it should be a bridge to a solution. We are concerned that this package is not aligned with the convention in that regard.

Article 34 does require states to make every effort to facilitate naturalisation. The long wait for settlement, coupled with the recent revision of the good character guidance in relation to naturalisation that would preclude naturalisation for people who have arrived irregularly, is certainly of concern. Alignment with Article 34 is of concern for us.

Lord Rook: Having had the privilege of working with both your organisations in a former life, it is slightly weird to be on this part of the horseshoe. I do not know which one of Patrick or Christa wants to answer this. We have had a previous panel today, and there was some focus in that conversation on the provisions in the Bill that mean that certain groups would be disqualified from protection, be it on the grounds of national security, threat to public order or bad faith. I am particularly interested to know what impact you think that provision will have on victims and what it means for our compliance, as a country and a Government, with international law.

Christa Rottensteiner: In summary, we believe that the modern slavery provisions risk harming victims and there are questions around the compatibility with international law. States can restrict protections when there are concerns related to public order, but the question is whether the provisions that we are seeing in Clause 43 are necessary, proportionate and aligned with international law. I will hand over to my colleague Patrick to elaborate further.

Dr Patrick Burland: As Christa has mentioned, existing legislation already allows for the disqualification of people convicted of serious offences, and our concern is whether the clause goes beyond addressing the serious threat to public order and the serious threat to the public by extending the disqualification to people who have received suspended sentences, to individuals whose offending was not considered serious enough by a court to warrant imprisonment.

There is an inconsistency in treating an individual as suitable to remain in the community under the Sentencing Act while simultaneously treating that same individual as posing the genuine, present and sufficiently serious threat required by Article 13(3) of ECAT to justify the disqualification.

Clause 43 also appears to strengthen the presumption in favour of disqualification under public order grounds, and there is a question therefore of its compatibility with the High Court case that Parosha mentioned in the earlier panel, R (ABW) v Secretary of State from 2025. In that ruling, the judge said that the presumption in favour of disqualification was an element that meant it was not compliant with ECAT. On the presumptive element, there is a question and a challenge in terms of compatibility.

Another concern is that, once a person is disqualified, an individual will no longer receive the full assessment of whether they are a victim of modern slavery. This raises questions about whether the UK is continuing to meet its obligations under Article 4 of the ECHR and Article 10 of ECAT, both of which require identification.

Identification is also key in the larger objective of combating and preventing modern slavery. It is not just the impact that this will have on those individuals who are disqualified; it is the impact that it will have on other victims or the public at large while traffickers are able to operate more freely or with impunity, if they are able to see that trafficked people are not receiving the protection and support that would help criminal proceedings and investigations.

In terms of the bad faith disqualification—as we heard earlier, there are elements about public order and bad faith—there is already a function to dismiss NRM claims under bad faith grounds that examines the specifics of the claim. It is worth remembering that since 2023 only 20 people have been disqualified for a bad faith claim. Under Clause 43, there would be an assumption that anyone making a claim after being given notice of removal should be automatically considered as having made a bad faith claim.

There are several issues with this. First, it does not allow for an individualised assessment of the claim. There is no evidence that all NRM referrals at point of removal are made in bad faith. The Government have acknowledged that some of these very late claims have received positive decisions. The current modern slavery guidance recognises that victims are traumatised and might disclose late because of that trauma. The data shows that late NRM referrals are not common. They only represent an extremely tiny proportion of the NRM caseload. Last year, only about 0.15% of the more than 23,000 NRM referrals would have been made after 11 pm on the night of a scheduled removal. There were 36 people in this category.

Finally, you referred to the impact on victims. We think this will be significant. The clauses would punish people for late disclosure and very likely for crimes that they committed as part of their exploitation, as we heard earlier. Two-thirds of victims who have been disqualified on public order grounds have been referred to NRM as a potential victim of criminal exploitation.

There is a risk that people are denied protection because of conduct and as a consequence of their exploitation. A woman forced into sex work who is arrested for solicitation could be disqualified from the NRM under the threat to public order provision. As I have said, this not only harms victims but strengthens traffickers’ business model, which harms other victims and the public at large.

Afzal Khan: The Bill would allow the Government to require asylum seekers to pay back what they have received in asylum support. Is this compatible with the refugee convention?

Vicky Tennant: First of all, I should say that, from the UNHCR perspective, we recognise the legitimate interest in reducing asylum support costs and ensuring that the asylum system is sustainable over the long term, but the way to do that is through faster, fairer and more efficient asylum processing. The longer people sit in the system, the greater the costs to both the individual and the state.

We do indeed have a legal concern that requiring recognised refugees to repay the cost of asylum support is difficult to reconcile with Article 29 of the convention. That is the article that essentially says that refugees should not be subject to duties, charges or taxes that are higher than or different to those that might be imposed on nationals in similar circumstances. It really reflects the principle that refugees should not be subject to additional financial burdens simply because they are refugees.

That is particularly important because asylum seekers of course do not have the ability to work. Asylum seekers who are forced to rely on asylum support are doing so simply because they do not have other options and they are in a situation of destitution.

Clearly, there is no exact equivalent of asylum support for UK nationals, but certainly there are similar provisions for ensuring that people do not fall into destitution. When that happens for a UK national, they are not expected to pay back the cost of that support.

As I said, our concern is really that recognised refugees under this provision would be treated differently simply because they are refugees, but there are also very practical concerns. This really relates to when an asylum seeker is recognised as a refugee, moves out of asylum support and starts on that integration journey. If a person, potentially with a family, starts that journey with a very significant debt hanging over them—we have been told up to £10,000 per person—it is going to be a significant obstacle to embarking on that journey.

The Government have said their objective through the work and study route, for example, is to support integration, self-sufficiency and contribution. We agree with that objective. From our perspective, once someone is recognised as a refugee, the policy should really be to help refugees get back on their feet, work and become economically independent. There is a legal issue, but there is also a very practical issue related to integration and supporting refugees to get back on to their feet.

Tom Gordon: The Government have said they will continue to provide safe and legal routes for asylum seekers to enter the UK and it has recently announced new refugee sponsorship routes. Are the Government doing enough to provide safe and legal routes as an alternative to illegal entry? Are those routes an answer to the concerns about the rest of the reforms that have been announced by the Government?

Vicky Tennant: We absolutely welcome the measures related to safe and legal routes and the reopening of resettlement, which is an important protection-led pathway, and the willingness to lean in and build a new sponsorship route. As UNHCR, we have been very engaged in and very supportive of that. We have agreed to play a role in helping to deliver those routes as well.

We always talk about having a constellation of pathways and not relying solely on one pathway as providing the answer. I have talked about the suspension of family reunion. That was a rights-based pathway that was operating at scale, mainly benefiting women and children coming from countries affected by conflict or situations where they were living as refugees in very precarious situations. We have really been engaging with the Government and encouraging them to look again at what can be done to reopen that particular pathway.

Safe and legal routes will never be the entire answer; a whole range of measures are needed. I have talked about the need to offer real alternatives upstream to refugees and migrants on the move, including the work that we have done recently looking at the movement of Sudanese towards Europe. We know that Sudanese arrivals in the UK are going up, and the reason that many of those people are on the move from Sudan is they are not able to secure protection and access to opportunities where they are. There is quite a bit of data on that. Yes, we need safe and legal routes to the UK, but that is not going to provide the answer for everyone.

Finally, safe and legal routes are not an alternative to access to asylum. It is important that the UK continues to uphold access to asylum for those who are arriving and claiming in the UK.

Christa Rottensteiner: I would like to add that IOM also very much welcomes the opening of the safe and legal routes. We are playing a role with the Government in conceptualising and implementing them. That variety of routes that Vicky mentioned is absolutely key. We are very glad to see that there will be a sponsorship route and that they will be looking at opportunities for students and people to come to work here.

We have seen the impact of that very directly. IOM has supported, together with the Red Cross, people to reunite with their families in the UK. Seeing that drop-off and the impact of that is very tragic. Again, it creates incentives for dangerous journeys, which we would want to avoid.

Clearly, we need the safe and legal routes. They need to exist at scale, but they need to be part of an overall system. We are working very closely with Governments, including the UK Government, to see what works in addressing the drivers. Evidence is very much a key to that. We need to have interventions that support people in their countries of origin or in host countries, which are often the neighbouring countries that are very poor. We know what works. We can make a difference so that people do not have to take this dangerous journey. It all needs to be part of a well-functioning system.

Baroness Hamwee: Could I ask one supplementary? Do any of you have any feel for the extent of the sponsorship that might become available? How long is it going to take to put it in place? It will be a work in progress.

Vicky Tennant: The Government have talked about numbers being in the hundreds per year initially. We are really leaning in to work with the Government to see how we can increase those numbers as quickly as possible. The applications for the student route will open fairly soon because that has to happen quickly, if students are going to be able to arrive by autumn next year. I understand that the spring is the target for opening applications to the other routes. It is a good example of the Government working well with civil society, with organisations like ours, to co-create this.

We are all really leaning in and doing our best to move it forward and to put something in place that can be brought to scale and deliver the number of arrivals that we would like to see.

The Chair: We will move to the last question. I will conclude the session when the bell rings in about five minutes. Bear that in mind when you respond.

Lord Rook: I mentioned working with your organisations earlier and that was on the creation of the original community sponsorship scheme back in 2015 following the Syrian crisis. I am sure that part of the answer to my question will be, “Please ensure that the Government speed up and grow the community sponsorship scheme as much as possible”. Other than that recommendation, what other recommendations should our committee be making around how the Bill and the wider policy package are implemented going forward? It is a free-for-all. You have five minutes.

Christa Rottensteiner: Speaking again about Part 5, the modern slavery provisions, we think the committee may wish to consider whether some of the assumptions that underpin Part 5 are supported by the available evidence. We have heard some of the evidence. We are very happy to share some more of it. There seems to be a proposition that increased NRM referrals mean there is misuse, which is not underpinned by the data. There is also an assumption that referrals are being regularly used solely to stop removals and that they are not genuine claims. The current situation may be that the system is working well. This is what the data indicates.

The other recommendation would be around considering whether operational improvements could contribute more effectively than the draft legislation to the Government’s objectives. We believe that it is important to expand non-statutory first responders, to increase the investment in their training and to facilitate swift referrals into the NRM. We can make the current system work better through operational improvements.

Finally, we think it is really important to have a distinction between modern slavery policy and migration policy. The two issues can overlap, of course, but they are not synonymous. Many victims of modern slavery, as we have heard today, are British nationals. Anti-slavery measures are generally most effective when they are focused on identifying and responding to exploitation.

Vicky Tennant: I have already spoken about a number of areas in which we would encourage you to make recommendations., such as core protection and the extended route to settlement. I have mentioned family reunification, and we have talked about the recovery of asylum support costs.

One area that we have not talked about but I know you have taken a great deal of evidence on is the operation of the new appeals body. We have made a number of recommendations in our observations. Those focus on the technical expertise of adjudicators, making sure that we have high-quality and consistent decision-making and transparency, independence and oversight. Paying attention to those areas, we think, would be really critical if the body is to deliver fair and efficient outcomes, as we all hope it will.

The Chair: The Division Bell has gone, so I am going to have to end the session. Thank you.
