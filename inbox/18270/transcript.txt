Chair: Thank you all for coming to the Administration Committee’s fourth session in our inquiry into general election planning. We welcome our witnesses, whom I will invite to introduce themselves in a few moments.

We have already heard from four Members who were elected in 2024 and from four who departed. Much of the evidence was very complimentary about the support in place from IPSA and the House services both at induction and in the departing Members’ area, but as part of our inquiry we want to explore the lessons learned and what improvements can be made to the induction programme, help with office set-up, support in finding suitable London and other city accommodation, and recruiting staff.

I now invite each member of the panel to introduce themselves and briefly describe their role.

Kim McGrath: I am Kim McGrath, head of the Members’ HR advice service, which supports Members in their roles as employers.

Karen Walker: I am Karen Walker, chief executive and accounting officer at IPSA. I led IPSA’s response to the 2024 general election.

Karen Lacey: I am Karen Lacey, the director of MP services at IPSA. My team is the team of account managers that supports MPs on a day-to-day basis and during the process of an election.

Chair: First, Karen Walker and Karen Lacey from IPSA, you said in your written evidence to the inquiry that IPSA’s response to the 2024 general election was your most successful to date. Can you describe what made that response so successful?

Karen Walker: If we draw on feedback from our own review and the recognition of improvements noted by current MPs and in earlier Committee evidence sessions, that is how we surmise that it was successful.

The election response in 2024 involved careful planning. We started planning in late 2023. We had taken all the lessons learned from previous elections, and we tailored the support for new, returning and departing MPs. All IPSA’s people were repurposed into general election roles, so we did not take on extra surge resource. We managed the resource internally and we educated those teams to be able to respond, depending on which team we assigned them to, so we had the capacity and the intelligence internally to do this.

Departing Members received more dignified, compassionate winding-up support. New MPs benefited from phased onboarding. We gave them all a named account manager; we gave them a cash advance, if they wanted one, to support them in the early days; and we increased the amount of induction support. Returning MPs received continued assistance throughout, particularly those who had had boundary changes. Close collaboration with the House services through the general election planning group provided joined-up services. Our agility and resilience helped us to manage the record turnover of 350 MPs, with 51% of MPs being elected for the first time.

We always do an annual survey: 44.6% of respondents rated the election support as four out of five or five out of five, just over 21% rated it as three out of five, and 11.4% rated it as one or two out of five, while 23% of respondents did not respond to the question. The boundary changes did add some complexity to the election; we planned additional support for MPs affected by that and for their staff, who might have faced longer commutes. All in all, the response did receive very positive feedback.

Chair: Thank you for that. It was a huge churn—a real change election, which would have been a shock to the system across the piece. When we held our first evidence session with MPs elected in 2024, they all described the overwhelming nature of those first few weeks. One of the more difficult tasks was finding and setting up a constituency office. Can you tell us how IPSA could help make that process more straightforward?

There are two supplementaries to that. In your evidence, you referred to a trial with leasing properties. How is that trial going, and how might it support new Members arriving after the next election? Of course, there are still ongoing problems and challenges for some Members finding an office within the budget. What is your assessment of that problem at the current time? One question, three parts: tell us about the overwhelming nature of the first few weeks and setting up a constituency office.

Karen Lacey: Selecting and setting up a constituency office is indeed one of the most challenging parts. We have already made changes to the way we support Members to do that. Our standard process for registering or amending a property has been simplified, and we have moved away from a dedicated property team. Now, each account manager individually supports Members when they want to make changes or register a property. That has led to a speed-up in the process—in the handling time—and improved relationships. Instead of sending long email trails, we are having conversations and supporting Members through the process, which is where we want to be. In addition, we are going to make the forms much simpler and try to reduce further the burden on Members, particularly at an election.

One of the other ways we can help at an election is with our lease trial. We are working towards IPSA taking on leases for constituency offices at this time. We have signed two leases to date, and we have five further leases in the pipeline. It is a trial, so we are learning a lot. It is a fairly challenging process from a due diligence perspective, but it has proven really successful for the two Members, who have leases that are signed and owned by IPSA. In addition, we have supplied their broadband through our centralised service, and one of them will be in receipt of our electricity. That is reducing the admin burden for them from other things associated with their office.

We hope by the time of the next election to have some offices in place, where one Member departs and a new Member arrives, and we could explore whether it is an option to hand the keys over. We accept that that might not work for all Members, but we are hoping to be flexible.

The last key area is that one of the other challenges was the length of the winding-up period at the last election. It meant that many departing Members maintained the lease on their constituency office. We might want to look at the departing process next time, so that we can free those up sooner to allow newly elected Members perhaps to take over those leases, or certainly to explore them.

Karen Walker: May I add one point about the cost of renting? There is an increased budget for London MPs. However, there are areas of the UK that are more expensive than others. We applied a one-on-one relationship where MPs were struggling to find anything within budget, and we gave flexibility across the budget. For example, where business rates may have taken them outside their office cost budget, we gave additional support, and we will continue to do that.

With the surveying, the legal fees from negotiating the contract and then a right-to-occupy arrangement with the relevant Member, it is a little more resource-intensive than if a Member chooses to sign their own lease directly, but it removes that burden from them.

Karen Lacey: Yes, exactly—spot on.

Karen Walker: Yes. Because MPs in the past have taken on their own leases, the expiry of those varies. More recently, we have asked for a two-month break clause in the event of a general election. Mary Glindon: This is to both Karens. You say in your written evidence, “Some new MPs had complex ‘pre-election’ employment arrangements and there were notable issues with understanding tax, correcting tax codes and a realisation of the impact of student loans.” Could you tell us about your ideas for improving information to candidates on tax and other deductions?

Karen Lacey: I think that IPSA, the House and parties could work really well together to provide more comprehensive information to candidates prior to an election about the obligations of the role and some of the personal implications of becoming an MP. IPSA cannot provide tax advice directly to individual Members, but we can certainly offer general information before the general election. HMRC has a specialist team that supports MPs directly, which we can contact and put MPs in touch with. For us, it was quite notable that some individuals, having come from very different backgrounds and jobs before being an MP, really had not understood the difference, so it is about doing anything we can do together to communicate with candidates about what that looks like.

We also undertook an equality impact assessment when we made our latest pay decision. We examined issues of the impact of MPs’ pay on tax, student loans and grossing up, and we are looking at that more widely with taxable expenses.

Mary Glindon: It is a very complicated system. I had not been classed as self-employed before—the annual tax form is a headache.

Karen Walker, you mentioned a cash advance for MPs if they needed it. Many MPs arrive in Westminster having fought an expensive election, and they might have left previous employment some time before they came to Westminster. How can we make it easier for new MPs to book transport and accommodation in those first few days without incurring financial difficulties?

Karen Walker: We did offer a cash advance at the last election. We offered £2,000, which was then deducted from the first salary that was paid at the end of July. That went very well: 124 MPs took us up on that cash advance. The whole premise of our doing that was so that they did not have to fund something up front, and we acknowledged that they may have been out of work for a period in the lead-up to being elected.

We also ordered the payment card for all MPs, and every MP had their card within two weeks of taking the Oath. We distributed them as quickly as we could, and the last card was delivered at two weeks, so we delivered quite steadily. That meant that people had access to a credit card—to funding—and they did not need to use their own money.

Ahead of the 2024 election, we also pre-booked hotels for MPs who were joining. We worked with the Parliamentary Travel Office. We secured several hotel rooms for several nights post the election as well, and we will do that again at the next election. Somebody booked accommodation and travel for all MPs who were coming to London to take the Oath and do their onboarding and induction.

We work really closely with the Parliamentary Travel Office, and we have since improved the travel and hotel booking system. Currently, MPs can book without needing to pay, and we pay the invoice that we get through the Travel Office. We tried to simplify that for the last election, and there is more that we can do next time around.

Mary Glindon: If people have been in financial difficulties, having to pay £2,000 within a month might still be a bit difficult; everybody has different financial situations. Karen Walker: If it is a loan, for all intents and purposes, that would help an MP settle their own finances as a result of campaigning and running for election, I think there is more we might be able to do in terms of the £2,000 cash advance. We are trying to make as many services as possible that MPs pay for centralised, so that we pay the bills and an MP does not need to, and it would come from their budget. With setting up a new office or setting up accommodation in London, for example, we want to take the pain out of it. We do not want MPs or their staff needing to fund things up front and claim it back. We are trying to reduce the number of claims that an MP needs to make, which is why we have centralised broadband and are going to centralise utilities. We could do the same for stationery and other goods and services that MPs need to buy. We will look at that.

We have also taken on a new payment card this year—we have moved from Barclaycard to Lloyds. We have the facility for what I would call, for ease, a virtual bank account. We would be able to set up direct debits and BACS payments on that as well. We are trialling that at the moment and have recruited some MPs to test the system with us. Ideally, you then have almost a virtual bank account for your business. We are trying as much as we can to remove the need for reimbursement and for anyone to fund anything work-related up front.

Kim McGrath: Thank you very much. That is very kind of you, and we love providing them. At the last general election, there were lots of layers of information available. First, the best practice guides were already in place on the intranet—ParliNet—and we could always get a printed copy if people wanted it. There was a best practice guide on recruitment. The layering came in through the induction process. There were the in-party, by-party sessions that we did up here in the Committee Rooms. Bambos Charalambous: In our last session, we heard from four Members who had departed. Some had retired; others had lost their seats in the election. They gave positive feedback about IPSA support and guidance during the winding-up period. The two Karens have answered part of this already, but can you outline how the winding-up process was improved at the last election, and what learnings you can take into the next election?

Karen Lacey: A key part of the process that made it work more smoothly for departing Members was the extension of the winding-up period to four months. That gave a reasonable amount of time. Certainly, from a staffing perspective—although we have touched on some of the issues—it was really supportive just to know that you did not have to run into the office and clear everything up within days.

My team and Kim’s team jointly met all departing MPs to talk them through the redundancy process, which is much simplified on previous years. We talked them through the exit of their different properties and the financial management processes needed to wind up their affairs with IPSA. We had a team of six account managers. They were the most experienced we had in the team at the time, and they were dedicated to supporting the departing cohort for the four or so month period after the election. It would be fair to add that, with the tone and the engagement that we trained into the team prior to the election, we were looking for dignity and pace in the winding up, even though we had extended that period. We wanted departing Members to feel like they had a supported exit from the process.

The thing we learned is that the four-month period was a great support, but that some former MPs did use that time to its fullest extent. That had knock-on effects on newly elected Members and perhaps led to the spending of funding that we could reduce in the future. We want to propose some clear milestones to talk about leases—perhaps not extending leases where they are already due to end within that winding-up period—and set some reasonable milestones to help give that process a bit more pace and structure for departing MPs.

Kim McGrath: We offered joint IPSA-HR meetings up to two years before the general election happened. That was for anyone who was thinking of standing down or anyone who was curious. We did several hundred of those. I might add that some people liked them so much that they came back for a second or third go, so perhaps we are a bit too successful. Things did change as we went through those two years—either IPSA rules or general employment law and so on—but they were so valuable. They helped people to come to terms with the fact they had decided to stand down. They got themselves so well organised by the time the general election happened that they were just ready to go. We will be doing that again.

Bambos Charalambous: Just to follow up, with the MPs that were reluctant to engage and took the whole four months, did you pick up any issues around why they were reluctant—was it just the shock of losing, or was it something else?

Karen Lacey: There are a variety of reasons why it took some longer than others, some of which were very personal. Some people were shocked and distressed. For others, maybe organising and closing things down was not their forte. Some had incredible office managers to lean on throughout the winding-up period. A bit more structure around that and the timeline will really support individuals in the future.

On assets and disposal of equipment, the challenge we have is that there are two different sets of rules for the House’s equipment and IPSA’s equipment. Equipment funded by IPSA can, for a fee, be retained by the departing member or donated to charity, whereas parliamentary equipment, which obviously has access to the parliamentary network, cannot be retained or donated because of the security measures. We took many steps to make that clearer and simpler at the last election, but since then, PDS has taken on the supply of much more of the equipment, and certain items can no longer be purchased with IPSA funding. That means that, come the next election, the rules will be a bit clearer. Fewer departing MPs will have the option to keep or donate their equipment, but it will be easier to give it back and get rid of it, and the House will take responsibility for the security of that.

Bambos Charalambous: On the departing MPs, our witnesses said that one of their biggest concerns in the run-up to the election was what would happen to their staff. It is great to hear about the inter-office loan scheme—that would be one thing for the staff of departing MPs to go into, although obviously, you would have to make sure that they did not all go into that, as you would have no one to wind up your office—but what improvements have you put in place to help MPs’ staff successfully find new employment?

Karen Walker: We doubled the winding-up period for staff as well as MPs, so that they could continue to support the departing MP. We also introduced a ringfenced development budget, which will hopefully build skills and improve employability for those people, in case they are made redundant. At the moment, we are exploring whether staff could access the same employment transition that MPs experienced during the last election, which I think would be really useful. IPSA also now recognises past service for previous MPs. That is taken into account in the redundancy payment calculation, which will increase the entitlement for some people and potentially encourage people to stick around and work for other MPs. As you mentioned, there is also the inter-office loan process, which we think will be really useful and will give somebody a test for whether they could work with someone else.

Kim McGrath: Up until the general election, there was training available on CV writing, getting ready for interviews, job searching and so on—I am sure that we could do more with that as well. We could also probably do something around targeting areas of high unemployment. One of the pitfalls we saw when the new Members took staff on was that, if they were in an area of high unemployment—this was for the constituency staff—they did not always take into account the local market rate. They would put people on high rates of pay, which makes it even more painful if they have to part company with the individual. If there is a general election and the Member loses their seat, the person is then in a difficult situation, but they will never get that rate of pay again, which makes it doubly difficult.

I am sure there is more that we can do when we talk to the new Members to reinforce the issue of looking at the local market rate. Also, when it is coming up to a general election, we can target areas of high unemployment to perhaps liaise with jobcentres, so that we get some extra support for individuals in those areas.

Chair: Thank you. I have a final question to colleagues from IPSA. Our predecessor Committee made recommendations on loss-of-office payments. What is your assessment of the effectiveness of the changes that you put in place for departing Members at the last election?

Karen Walker: The Committee recommended that we review the winding-up period and set an evidence-based timetable for the next election and allow case-by-case extensions. It also recommended treating loss-of-office payments as redundancy, payable regardless of whether winding-up tasks were complete, and then benchmarking that against comparable redundancy schemes.

In response, we doubled the winding-up period to four months, and we also extended the winding-up payment, which is equivalent to four months’ salary, to all departing MPs, whether they stood down or lost their seat. We paid that in full at the end of the election months. We also looked at MPs who stood and lost their seat, so they received the loss-of-office payment, which again is equivalent to four months’ salary, and we paid that on the last day of the month before the deadline.

For the 2024 election, we paid that at the end of October, and the four-month winding-up deadline was 4 November. We withheld this from just a handful of MPs who did not engage at all—we did not have a choice on that—because there were outstanding staffing issues and other things that we needed to look at, but that was a handful. We examined comparable redundancy schemes before we made recommendations to the board to allow for the changes that we made.
