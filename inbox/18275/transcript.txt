III

Nick Cockayne, Managing Director, Rappa Fencing Ltd.;

Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Lucy Monks, Balwinder Dhoot, Nick Cockayne and Nick Smith.

Chair: Welcome to the final panel of today’s session on export support services. I thank our witnesses for joining us. Lucy Monks, from the FSB’s point of view, can you give us a picture of how challenging the environment is for SMEs trying to export today?

Lucy Monks: It is a particularly challenging environment for SMEs. Our reporting shows that export growth has been in negative territory since about 2019, but the last few quarters have shown particular impacts because of some of the global economic shocks. A number of regulatory changes happening abroad and the ongoing consequences of pieces that have been in place, such as the US tariff regime, have meant that, slowly but surely, exporting has become more difficult and is dropping off for SMEs.

Chair: Are there particular factors that your members point you to?

Lucy Monks: As someone on the previous panel mentioned, some of the input costs for UK products and services have meant that they are not as competitive abroad. That is a huge issue. Part of it is that there is an ongoing headwind internationally. For example, the ongoing impact of the conflict in Iran means that moving goods can be more expensive; there is also the US tariff regime, and other items that can make it more costly to export.

Chair: If we look back through, say, the last 20 years, how do the current pressures compare with challenges in the past?

Lucy Monks: Over the last 20 years, it has become much more difficult and complicated than previously. These are particularly challenging headwinds. It is partly a domestic problem and partly an international problem. The UK Government can look to resolve that both through trade measures and agreements and through export support programmes.

Dan Aldridge: Can you give me a sense of how many UK SMEs might want to export but cannot?

Lucy Monks: We did a survey a couple of years ago, so we know that about a third of SMEs that do not currently export would like to. That is quite a substantial group. Probably more SMEs that previously exported have dropped off, and we should consider them as part of that group as well.

Dan Aldridge: It is really interesting that people have stopped. Do you know what is behind that?

Lucy Monks: A lot of SME exporters exported into the EU—that was the majority of export activity. After we entered into new terms with the EU agreement, they might have considered it a bit too difficult or not understood the new rules, so they might have just withdrawn or slowly decided over time that it was too costly and burdensome, especially for SME exporters that deal with low-value goods. A couple of pieces of EU regulation have come in over time that have made it slightly more costly—but, on the whole, a lot more costly—to send goods, especially over to the EU. Those are the kinds of things where people might eventually decide to give up.

Dan Aldridge: We heard in a previous panel about a 17% increase in exports outside the EU. Is it illogical to think that those businesses would start to look outside the EU, or is proximity such an important thing?

Lucy Monks: Proximity is important. I think it depends on the kind of business. Quite a big group of the export community among SMEs is what we would term “passive exporters”: for example, a customer will look on the internet and go, “I quite like that T-shirt,” and order it, and it will get shipped out. It is not really an attempt to sell into a new market, but it happens anyway. When those people see the costs increasing slightly or the paperwork increasing quite a lot, they might just withdraw themselves.

The other group, the active exporters, might try to ship around and find other markets, and they might do so successfully, but it depends on the market you are trying to access and how difficult that is.

Dan Aldridge: Nick, you seem eager to jump in.

Nick Smith: Only to say that I work for a company that is a manufacturer, an exporter and an importer; we are right in the middle of the aerospace sector. Two years ago, 40% of our exports were to the US; that is now 3%. That has really been affected by—I will use the term—the Trump tariffs. Some of the materials we were bringing in—some for recycling and some pure materials, which we were importing from China—would attract an additional 40% tariff today if they flowed through the UK into the US. Consequently, they found other routes to achieve the same outcome.

For us, the growth has had to be in other countries. We have done that successfully, whether it is in Australia, Japan, South Korea or some European countries, but it has been challenging. We are looking forward to the day that tariff block changes, and as an exporter we then intend to claim that section back from other people.

Dan Aldridge: Have the US tariffs been more impactful than Brexit, for example?

Nick Smith: Certainly. Brexit changed the paperwork we needed to do. For REACH regulations, for example, we had to set ourselves up as a UK entity as opposed to a European one. From the point of view of challenge, I have been in the aerospace industry for a long time, through various events—Enron, 9/11 and other very significant events—and this is one of those. From the point of view of people trying to move material into the US, we recycle scrap. When people break down an aircraft, we can purchase the components of that aircraft, such as the blades, and we recycle those. We can absolutely move that to the US—it comes under scrap, as opposed to a pure material—but the jam, if you like, is on the pure materials, and that has been hard to replace.

Nick Cockayne: We are a very small SME. The companies that you are talking about, which you want to get exporting, are probably like us. Three years ago, we were a £5.1 million company; this year, we will be over £8 million. When we were a £5.1 million company, £200,000 was export; export is now £1.5 million, and that is because we have actively gone looking for it. We saw the UK market, and we saw that we had to go looking for export, so we went looking for help—“Where can we get it?” I have experience of dealing with UKTI as I think it was then, and so on. I thought, “There’s help out there,” but it was incredibly difficult to find.

It is not easy, and when you find the help, it is quite involved and complex, but some of the things that we did find were fantastic; some were not. One example is that we were given a business export manager, run through what was then the Department for Business and Trade. They were the point of contact to put us in touch with country managers and things like that and they looked at the products and learned about us. They invited us to speak at an event in Oxford, when a load of American farmers came over. We went to that, and literally—they were purely American—they started whooping and hollering, the hats came off and everything like that, and we found out that we should go to certain places in America.

We, a British manufacturing company, went to America. We went there with a 40-year-old product and, at the biggest cattle show in America, called CattleCon, we won best new product in the show, so an absolutely fantastic success, and we are now exporting to America. We had the advantage that Mr Trump came in as we were at the show, basically, and no one knew the price of our product, because it was brand new to the market, so we could factor that into the process. We are starting to really grow in America and in Canada, which is great.

Where we were let down is when we looked at some of the areas in Europe. A show on at the moment in Hanover is called EuroTier. It is the biggest agricultural manufacturing show in Europe. We have someone there this year, visiting. Last year, we went and were very kindly invited to the British pavilion. They said, “Come to a reception at about 5 o’clock.” There were drinks and things like that, and we thought, “That is nice.” It was aimed not at us, but at people from foreign countries wanting to be distributors and things like that.

We got to the show, and everyone else’s country had a very nice stand, which was themed and quite large, the size of this room sort of thing. We got to the British one: it was probably the length of that table, open sided, with three panels up on it. We got there, saw my contact, who said, “Hi, sorry about this,” and passed me a plastic glass—I think there was a choice of beer, a soft drink or some such thing. I am not saying that I need to be watered, champagned and things like that, but that is an impression that we give to a lot of companies, a lot of British companies, and there are a lot of people there who see that. There was a lot of comment about it. On the one hand, you are doing the other stuff, but on the other, there is that.

Chair: That is a very useful perspective. Balwinder, will you help us round out the picture from your sector?

Balwinder Dhoot: I represent food and drink manufacturing, the largest manufacturing sector in the UK, which contributes £42 billion to the economy, with jobs everywhere, in every single region. To provide some context to the trade picture for food and drink, we published some data for the first half of the year—the first six months—showing that exports are down about 10% and imports are up. That is a continuing trend. Just in the first half of the year, the trade deficit on food and drink is about £21 billion. That is the biggest it has ever been. It is not a one-off; it is a trend and a signal. It sets out a problem, but it is actually also a massive opportunity, because I think we can close that gap. It needs the right export support and help for SMEs.

With food and drink, there is lots of complexity to exporting. We have everything from health certificates and rules of origin to labelling requirements. The sector’s products are among the most complicated to export, so you really do need support in there for businesses that are trying to do that. You need to take out a lot of the friction that businesses find. What we have just heard is that there is almost a bit of randomness about whether you find the right support or product to help you export. We need to make it much smoother. Free trade agreements are part of that.

The third thing, which has been touched on, is the domestic business environment, and whether the domestic business environment adds on costs to doing business here. Energy is a great example. Energy makes up at least 10% of the production costs of food and drink manufacturing. Energy in the UK is double the EU average. It is very hard to have a comparative advantage when you are paying double the energy price that others pay. On top of that, you have other regulatory costs.

Export support is important, but we cannot lose sight of the fact that export success starts at home in terms of the domestic policy environment.

Mr Reynolds: We have heard a little bit about it from Nick, but which specific bit of the support that the UK Government give to SME exporters is the most useful and which is the least useful?

Balwinder Dhoot: Food and drink manufacturing is not part of the industrial strategy, so you have seen the traditional support that would have sat in DBIST or DBT be withdrawn. That is the kind of support that you receive overseas. DEFRA is picking up a bit more of that slack, which is helpful.

DEFRA has traditionally focused on market access barriers. The support that really helps is more commercial support, where you are connecting businesses with demand in market—so helping them get into the market. You can remove a market access barrier. That will tend to be for primary products, so poultry or pigs, et cetera.

It does not help if you have a manufactured or branded product and you are trying to get that into a market. What you need to navigate that is someone who will help you identify who the buyers are, do the connections and help you with matchmaking. That costs money, but it does have proven results.

We work closely with the Food and Drink Exporters Association, which runs its own events. For example, they hire a hotel in London where they will bring in Mexican buyers and invite loads of British businesses. Those are quite successful.

It is about shifting from just market access to how you connect supply and demand. There is demand overseas. We can supply it subject to the caveats around having a successful business environment. How do you match those two people?

Chair: Nick—which bits are helpful and which are unhelpful?

Nick Cockayne: Things such as getting delegates in are helpful—basically, getting a reverse trade mission, having them in to talk to people. Unfortunately, a lot of those have been cancelled this year, as in the funding has been removed for us to actually host things like that.

The business export managers were a fantastic opportunity. They really got to understand you and introduce you. A good business manager like the one we got opened doors for us in various countries. However, their numbers were halved earlier this year. That has had a massive effect on us because now we do not have that support.

What is not good is the focus of Government—I will use Government as a whole as opposed to one party—on export being service-based and not manufacturing or product-based.

This morning I was at the Turkey-UK free trade agreement negotiations with various other businesses, and they basically admitted that the free trade agreement has an absolute focus on service. If we have an absolute focus on service—which I understand because that is where the real money is—we are never going to grow manufacturing or product.

Chair: No, and it is also hard to separate goods and services these days.

Nick Smith: This time last week I was in Florida at an industry conference. There were probably 400 delegates from various countries. We arranged multiple meetings. We call it speed dating. Within that, we are therefore meeting our potential suppliers and customers in a very condensed timeframe. We came away from that with quite a big action list of follow ups. That worked for us, so we are not really relying on Government support. We tend to just get off our butt and go hunt it down ourselves really.

Alison Griffiths: I am particularly interested in the trade missions and the consular support that you get from embassies in different jurisdictions. I will start with Nick, because I think you may have a view. Do you find that in the English-speaking jurisdictions there is more straightforward support, even in the US, which we know is a complicated market, although you have obviously been successful there? In the non-English-speaking jurisdictions, or possibly even in multilingual jurisdictions, like Switzerland, is the support appropriate? Are you being given enough understanding of the nuances of the local markets from the local consuls and embassies?

Nick Smith: One of the areas where we have had the opportunity for significant growth over the last 12 months is Japan. Part of that is because we have, during that period of time, employed a Japanese speaker. He worked for a Japanese company. He's an English guy. That enables us to get past switchboards that we would never get past because it is a very difficult country to actually do business in. They are very traditional; they stick with the same business partners over many, many years. To break into that is difficult and you need the language to be able to access the people that you want to get to. Again, it’s still not really Government officials, because we go manufacturers to manufacturers.

Alison Griffiths: Nick?

Nick Cockayne: Yes and no. In America, we had fantastic help from the embassy, which was great and useful. We are starting to do bits in Australia, which has proven to be good. And when we went to Thailand, we had great help from the embassy—that was again very much exposed. As Nick was saying, across Europe, we had basically no help because we had to just crack on with it.

Alison Griffiths: It is a difficult market as well.

Nick Cockayne: We just had to do it—as you said, to get out there and meet people and so on. It is in these markets where we have met people who could help, and we have grasped that help. I want to make it clear when I am talking about all these things that I am not after a freebie. We just want pointing in the right direction, and to be in the right room with the right people—that is facilitation.

Alison Griffiths: Do you have any extra comments to add to that?

Balwinder Dhoot: On the point about Europe, for food and drink, I think we have seen exports drop by around a third. Do not look at value, because inflation hides what is really happening. Look at volume—volumes are down by a third into the EU. We are going to have an SPS agreement which will remove some of the barriers that are now there. Most of the third that have dropped out are small businesses. They are not just going to spring back. They are now cut out of the supply chains that they were part of and you really need to put in support to recapture that. We have said that to DEFRA, and I think they are listening to that.

On some of the earlier conversations about free trade agreements, opening the door is fine, but you really need to help people through it. If you do not help them through it, you just have an open door. People can only look at it; it does not make a difference.

Chair: That is very helpful.

Antonia Bance: We hear frequently that the Government should prioritise its efforts on the sectors that are most likely to produce growth, and to produce export-led growth. The Government have done that by of publishing an industrial strategy, and now what we hear is that people who are not in the specified sectors of the industrial strategy wish they had not done the prioritisation. Do you think that the Government are focusing too much on the sectors targeted in the industrial strategy? I think I know what your answer is going to be, Balwinder, but I will start with you.

Balwinder Dhoot: Thanks for humouring me. There is obviously a choice. There is a finite resource, and the Government have selected sectors. I think you have to then look at the implications of that. If that means the withdrawal of everything and of all the support, the consequences are you are going to miss some opportunities around growth. I would say if you look at food and drink manufacturing, if the Government's objective is growth in every postcode, then there is no sector in the industrial strategy that could compete with food and drink manufacturing to do that. They are making a choice which does not in some ways align with what is now a Government priority. There are 12,000 manufacturing businesses, and many thousands of other types of food and drink businesses.

There is massive export potential there, not only for capturing some of that EU market but globally. Our brands are recognised internationally and there is a big opportunity there. It does not require a huge amount of resource. One proposal we have put to Government is to look at what Scotland and Wales do. They specifically support food and drink exports.

In England there is a gap. We have estimated for a budget of around £2.6 million, and you could fill that gap. Those are not huge numbers. That will not get anywhere with Treasury in our experience; the Treasury is not particularly interested in our sector. There is an opportunity to invest in the sector, and you would get a return on £2.6 million quite easily. You can see what Scotland and Wales do, in terms of their investment in their region. Wales have got some impressive figures in increasing exports.

Antonia Bance: And £2.6 million is not even a rounding error for Treasury. Lucy?

Lucy Monks: It is understandable that the Government feels the need to make choices. There are some sectors and regions where it feels it can make the most impact. One question for me is this. When the Government is putting a huge amount of attention and resource into those sectors, how is it ensuring the SMEs are included, even if the focus is on the larger businesses? The Government are helping larger businesses enter into a difficult market. How is that large business meant to bring SMEs along? How can SMEs benefit? That question is still going around my mind.

Yes, on the larger picture, it is difficult when some sectors are prioritised over others. A lot of our members are doing B2C activity and would not necessarily be covered by the sectors the Government has prioritised. To be fair to the Department, they have moved significantly over the past couple of years to improve the support they give to SMEs through information and guidance, which is laudable.

Obviously, moving on the next stage, when you are talking about business that might have started exporting or do a little and want to push up to the next level, then they fall into a trap: if they are not in one of the priority sectors and are not a low-level exporter, what help can they get?

Nesil Caliskan: I want to ask about UK Export Finance. From your perspective, are you happy with the scale and types of financial support provided to SMEs? I would like to start with Lucy.

Lucy Monks: UK Export Finance has done quite a lot in the past year to improve products and outreach to the SME community. They recognised that SMEs felt quite underserved by what they were providing. Mr Reynolds asked earlier what the least helpful service was available for exporters. I think the least helpful service is the one they do not know exists. UK Export Finance had a problem with that. People did not know it was there, or found it too impenetrable when they did get there.

To be fair, UK Export Finance have put a much greater focus on the SME community. It will take a bit of time to bed in and see how the results pan out. It is something we have been helping them with, promoting their activities out to our membership. We can continue to do that. It takes a little while for there to be a realisation of change.

To contextualise how SMEs think about this, 9% of SMEs have someone exporting and have someone who works on trade for them. For the most part, SMEs are just trying to learn about all these new things on top of actually doing their day job. It can take a little while for the message to get through.

Nesil Caliskan: Is that 9% of those that have accessed?

Lucy Monks: No; 9% of SME exporters have a member of staff or a team that works on it. The rest of them are just trying to figure out anything else on top of actually doing the job and their activity.

Nesil Caliskan: Obviously, there was a profile issue in what was available when they were navigating the process issue. I want to get a sense of how hard it is for SMEs to qualify, even if they were able to navigate and whatnot. Is there a threshold? SMEs play an important part in the overall picture.

Lucy Monks: Yes. I do not have specific numbers but I have heard anecdotally that some SMEs that have gone forward to apply for export finance have found it too difficult, because they have not met certain thresholds, but I do not have the figures.

Chair: Nick and Nick, what is your perspective?

Nick Smith: From my point of view, the help that could come would be about things such as currency hedging. I do not see anything on there but that is incredibly important for us. We tend to buy in different currencies, hold our inventory in GBP and sell in euros or dollars. Those currencies can be very volatile for us, so we sometimes need help and advice, I think. Maybe that help is out there and we just do not know where to look.

Nesil Caliskan: Do you need a bigger buffer for the business?

Nick Smith: It is more about the volatility and the fact that our performance in a month can be impacted significantly by dollar to GBP exchange rate without doing anything. Those sort of things can be significant, so some help on how to control them would be really useful. I am not sure that we see that.

Nick Cockayne: We use UK Export Finance. The only reason we do is that we went to find it ourselves. The bank knew what we were doing, we were talking about it and nobody said, “UK Export Finance”. We found out about it; I arranged a call with UK Export Finance and they said, “You’ve really got to speak to your bank.” Like Lucy said, it got very complex and involved but I thought it would be stupid not to do it, so we stuck with it and now we have something in place.

The big thing is that our borrowings—without saying too much about it—are too small for the banks to be interested. They are just not interested in that level of business. Basically, as an SME, you struggle to get a letter of credit for under £10 million. If you have that in place everybody hits their targets because they get the big numbers in, whereas in dealing with me for half a million or £1 million that is going to take a long time. That is the issue.

Chair: How long is a long time, in your book?

Nick Cockayne: I meant that if they are lending out only £1 million here and there, when they could lend one person £20 million—

Chair: I see. You persisted with the process; how long did it take you to get through that treatment?

Nick Cockayne: Over a year.

Chair: Over a year!

Nick Cockayne: Yes, although we were also going through a lot of changes as a business. We got a half a million pound UKEF trade loan but it took forever to go through. Part of that was the bank. We had a great bank manager—Adam was absolutely superb—and he made it go through quicker because he knew what we were trying to do.

Chair: Can you give us a sense of how much of your time, or your colleagues’ time, was involved in getting that sorted out?

Nick Cockayne: For me it was nearly full time because I was the only one driving it. We are a small business. Everybody else was trying to do their job to keep the company going. I am kind of doing that part of things and trying to get that going, so it took an enormous amount of time.

Chair: Is that not crazy?

Nick Cockayne: Yes, of course it is.

Chair: Thanks for confirming that.

Dan Aldridge: I have the privilege of having the Food Works in my constituency, which is the south-west’s innovation centre in that sector, but despite my enthusiasm for trade deals the response from SMEs and food providers in my constituency has been quite muted for lots of the reasons that we have discussed. Should the Government do more to demonstrate that those benefits of the trade deals should be felt across every postcode, as we talk about growth in every postcode? What help would you need to make more use of the benefits that have been negotiated? Do we accept that it is for big business and big business alone—although I do not accept that?

Balwinder Dhoot: For food and drink I would not accept that it is just for big business. There is definitely an opportunity for smaller businesses to export globally. I think that we export to over 200 markets, so it already happens—the evidence is there. There are a couple of things you need to think about. When you negotiate a free trade agreement, factor in how you engage with the sectors that will be able to meet demand in that market. Sometimes we get a sense that when you look at a free trade agreement you look at goods that already go to that market and think, “How do we increase those?” There are goods that are not going to that market because there are barriers. How do you remove those barriers?

There is no data on these exports—it does not exist, but it could. An example we would give is in the free trade agreement with India: India has really high tariffs on oats, and they were not removed. Our speculation is that that was because the data showed that oats were not traded with India, but that is because of the really high tariffs. There was a market that could have been opened up. It is about making sure that you open markets in the right way. That is not just about tariffs; it is the regulatory barriers and friction at the borders—all of that makes a difference. That should be part of the agreement.

Then, it is about the communication and support that you give businesses. If you are moving into a new market, it can be very challenging. How do you remove as much of that friction as possible? I use an analogy for this: if Amazon did export support for the UK, what would it do? It would be data led; it would identify the demand and supply, and it would match them; it would remove friction so that you could do a transaction as quickly as possible; and it would continue to evaluate the data and improve the service.

Chair: There may also be problems around unionisation of workers, pricing and exchange of unsafe goods, but I take your point.

Balwinder Dhoot: It is an analogy. I am not saying that we should outsource to Amazon, but it is a business model. Amazon is trying to drive sales, which is what the UK should be doing. We could learn from that, in taking a business-minded approach that is really data driven. In some ways, it is quite simple: you are trying to match customers with suppliers, so how do you do that as effectively as possible?

Chair: Yes—it is an analogy.

Nick Smith: We would like more visibility of the trade deals. Maybe the fact that we are busy doing what we do means that we therefore do not tend to see things happening at that level—that is the reality. The comment about knowing your customers is incredibly important: for us, that part is fundamental. In some cases, we know buyers who have the power almost at their fingertips: they might get free bids, but they make the decisions. I know one UK buyer has the power for $40 million, and I know—I wrote it down earlier—that one US buyer has $300 million-worth of power. You have to understand the individuals, so for us, relationships at our level are incredibly important. You are talking about further up, which I am sure is also incredibly important, but we do not see visibility of it.

Nick Cockayne: I think free trade agreements are largely irrelevant to SMEs. I go to a lot of events and talk to a lot of people, and there is a little phrase everybody uses about these things: “It affects the 1%.” It is the 1% right at the top—the massive companies—that will benefit. Some of them are pushing back on it, because some of the agreements are not quite what they seem at the moment.

SMEs need somebody to say, “Are you thinking about exporting? Here’s some practical help and advice.” Some of that goes on. Some of the Chambers of Commerce—though not many of them—are really good, and say, “Let’s get in a room. Here you go: if you want practical advice, here is how to get into America. If you want to take the next step, here it is, but you as a business have to decide to do it. We won’t hold your hand, but we will push you.” That is great. That is the sort of advice an SME needs, not, “Here’s a fantastic free trade agreement with India.”

Lucy Monks: It is really important, because there is a real risk that people think that free trade agreements are not for them—that they are a Westminster thing, and are not really applicable to how they do business. I always think that export support should be a translation job. First of all—the UK Government have been good at doing this over the last few years—it is about getting SME chapters into free trade agreements. Basically, that is a chapter dedicated to making sure that the outreach and translation work is done, and that there is a commitment to making sure that they do work for SMEs. I hope what we have seen in the SME strategy, and what we would see through a trade strategy and into the future, is an ongoing commitment to providing support on the ground, so that people feel like something is there for them.

Chair: That is super helpful. That takes us to time; thank you very much indeed. That is a really useful set of evidence that will help us no end in this inquiry. Thank you very much for your time this afternoon. That concludes this panel and this session.
