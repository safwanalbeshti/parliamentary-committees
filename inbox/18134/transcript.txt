Q36 Chair: Welcome to today’s second panel on AI safety and governance. Thank you very much indeed to our witnesses for joining us today. I know that the parliamentarians among you will need no reminding, but to give false, misleading or incomplete evidence to a Committee is, on the face of it, a contempt of the House.

Let me start with some of the warnings that we have received from the leaders of your organisations. Mr Altman told the US Senate in 2023, “My worst fears are that we…cause significant harm to the world.” The leader of Anthropic went on the record in 2025 to say, “I think there’s a 25% chance that things go really, really badly.” Mr Zuckerberg said in 2024 that individual bad actors may be able to use the intelligence of AI models to fabricate entirely new harms. Demis Hassabis has said that advances on the frontier are now outpacing our understanding of the technology.

Nobody in the world knows for sure what is going to happen from here. Can you assure the Committee today that your models can do no harm to people or businesses in our country and will do no harm in the years ahead?

Tom Duff Gordon: Good afternoon, Chair. Good afternoon, members of the Committee. We take safety incredibly seriously at OpenAI. We listened very carefully to the previous panel. Our position at the moment is that we should not progress with AI as fast as we can make it and develop it; we should progress AI as fast as we can keep it safe. Those are not just words; they are also deeds. During the summer, after the Hugging Face incident, we paused the training and reinforcement learning of our most advanced model. Just recently, we held back a model—Astra 6.1—that we were due to release. We say that we want to move at the speed where we can make sure that capabilities are not outstripping safety, and we are matching that with what we do. We have very significant processes internally to ensure that we manage risks. We have a preparedness framework.

Q37 Chair: Tom, that is great, but it is not the question I asked you. The question I asked you was whether you can guarantee that today your model is not capable of being used to cause harm to people or businesses in this country.

Tom Duff Gordon: Yes, I can reassure you that we are confident in the processes that we have internally to ensure that we manage frontier risks, particularly risks with regard to cyber, bio and loss of control. We have a preparedness framework and will not deploy a model unless it has passed that framework. I can assure you that our deployed models are safe and do not pose a risk to the UK.

Q38 Chair: Lord Booth-Smith, can you give the same assurance for Anthropic?

Lord Booth-Smith: We have rigorous safety processes in place. We were founded as an AI safety research company. Alongside those safety processes, we also believe that we need to pace the frontier. We consider deployed models safe. They have mitigations in place.

It is also worth pointing out that we are learning a lot as we go about how these models are used. It would be wrong not to say that. As new issues emerge, that is something that we believe should be publicly shared and debated. We factor that into all our future thinking about safety research.

Q39 Chair: You think that harms could be caused.

Lord Booth-Smith: We have seen in the last six months some instances where models have done things that we would not have wanted them to do. Safety is stratified, as to how one would determine that, but we have seen models do things that we would not have wanted them to do. I believe that it is entirely plausible that we have the mitigations in place to deal with those. As models become more capable, we need to make sure that the safeguards in those models are in place to deal with the additional capabilities that we see as the models improve.

Q40 Chair: The question was whether you can guarantee that the models are currently safe. You started off by saying yes, but then said that there have been some problems in the past and, no, you cannot absolutely guarantee that model development will be safe in the future.

Lord Booth-Smith: I am confident that the models on the market right now are safe. I am confident in the processes we have around them. I am just saying that, given the testimony we heard earlier and our own research, we know that, if models become ever more capable, we will need to have testing and evaluation that can keep pace, but also safety mitigations that can match up to the capability of the model. That is why a few months ago the CEO of the company called for a pacing of the frontier, including embedding evaluators, which is something that we have already done.

Q41 Chair: The capability of the models may be proceeding so fast that we may not currently have the systems in place to keep the models safe.

Lord Booth-Smith: That is a genuine concern. One reason why we should pace the frontier is that, if we are able to pace the frontier properly, we will buy ourselves time and space to do two things. The first is to make sure that the testing and evaluation processes and frameworks that we have in place are adequate to the capabilities of the model. Secondly, we will create the time and space for policymakers, elected representatives and Governments to think through what the appropriate governance framework for these models is, both pre and post‑release.

Q42 Chair: The technology is moving faster than our ability to keep it safe, and therefore we need to slow down the speed with which the technology is proceeding.

Lord Booth-Smith: It is more about where we see the technology going. It is exponential. It is a trajectory. If one assumes that it continues progressing at an ever more rapid pace, then, yes, the current testing and evaluation framework does not meet some of the future risks that may appear. We want to be confident that that matches up properly, and you can only achieve that by pacing the frontier of the technology.

Q43 Chair: Our concern is that many technology bosses at the moment appear to be behaving almost like the Dr Frankenstein of the 21st century. They are seeking a freedom to deploy powers beyond our ability to control the harms that might arise. You think that that is a risk, and therefore legislators need time to develop stronger safeguards.

Lord Booth-Smith: We have consistently believed that there should be binding rules on companies and that voluntary commitments are not enough. We have been very consistent about that for a number of years. The industry and the technology should be subject to Government rule setting.

Q44 Chair: Mr Larter, what is the commitment that you can give on behalf of Google DeepMind as to whether the model is today safe for people in this country?

Owen Larter: Thank you, Chair and members of the Committee. This is a very important conversation at an important time. We at Google DeepMind have been doing AI research in the UK for over 15 years now. We think that there is an enormous amount of opportunity around the technology. It is fantastic to see the way in which people right across the UK, whether scientific researchers, teachers or tradespeople, are now using this technology. It is absolutely fundamental that we do this in a way that is safe, secure and responsible.

Alongside the work that we do to advance AI capabilities, we invest in our internal governance processes. We have a frontier safety framework internally that tests our most advanced systems for misuse risks, so the idea that a bad actor could weaponise an advanced model for a cyber-attack or create a bioweapon, and issues of alignment, making sure that advanced models are following instructions.

At the core of this safety framework is a critical capability level. We make sure that we are not putting models out there on to the market that are beyond a certain risk level. We test them rigorously internally to assess that. We then apply mitigations. We have those mitigations red-teamed by both internal experts and, critically, third parties. They give us good confidence that the models we are then putting out there for people to use are not breaching these critical capability levels.

Q45 Chair: Can you give the commitment today that the model you have available is not capable of causing harm to people or business?

Owen Larter: Yes. The whole point of the frontier safety framework is that we would not put something out there unless we were very confident that risks had been mitigated to an appropriate level.

Q46 Chair: Do you think that the potential risks of your model in the future are containable under the current safety regime we have available?

Owen Larter: This is powerful technology that will have all kinds of incredible opportunities and all kinds of applications in scientific research and healthcare, but we should take AI capabilities seriously and continue to invest in safety science and mature—

Q47 Chair: That sounds like no.

Owen Larter: If I could just finish the answer, please, Chair, a fundamental aspect of this AI governance debate is how quickly it is moving. The AI continues to become more capable. We are also learning a lot more about how the AI performs and how to mature our governance processes. We have had our frontier safety framework in place for two years. We have updated it four times to iterate to improve it. We have to keep investing in that safety science so that we are able to keep speed with the capabilities of the technology as well. That is a fundamental.

Q48 Chair: Do you think we have the right regime for keeping these models safe?

Owen Larter: We have to continue to invest in the scientific study of AI safety, mature these best practices and work with others. This will be a partnership as well. There are institutions such as the UK AI Security Institute, which is world leading, and its equivalent in the US. They have an enormous amount of technical expertise as well. If we can pool that knowledge and expertise and build these best practices, we must find a way to keep pace with the capabilities of technology.

Q49 Chair: Given what we have learned about the incidents this year, why should we trust you to do that?

Owen Larter: This is fundamental to the systems that we have put together.

Q50 Chair: Sure, but why should we trust you?

Owen Larter: A fundamental part of what we do is to perform our internal testing and apply mitigations, but then also, critically, work with third parties to have that verified. I would draw us back to the AI Security Institute, which is a crown jewel that we have here in the UK. There are a lot of research collaborations and pre-release testing that we do with organisations such as this. The AI Security Institute mission, which is to make sure that Governments have an understanding of advancing capabilities, is very important as we seek to continue to mature this best practice that I am talking about.

Q51 Chair: It does not have compulsory access to models. It does not have access to the early testing regime that it needs. It does not have a kill switch. It does not have the ability to stop models coming on to the market. It does not have any of the tools that could help guarantee to us, as legislators, that it has the power to keep us safe.

Owen Larter: There is a really important conversation about the role of the AI Security Institute, which has been right at the forefront of maturing AI safety science over the last few years. One benefit of the AI Security Institute is that it is not a regulator, so it has open discussions with the labs. We do testing with it and learn from it. We share knowledge. We do research collaborations as well. It is really important in terms of maturing the science.

If you turn it into a regulator, you are going to completely change the dynamic with which we engage with the AISI. I think you might lose a lot of that ability to have the open, collaborative discussion that is maturing the science at a rapid lick, which is what we need.

Q52 Chair: That brings us back to a position of basically trusting your good intentions.

Owen Larter: I do not think that it is a case of trusting our good intentions. It is also important to understand that we have a good body of regulation in the UK to start from. It is interesting hearing the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum talking about the existing law that applies. Alongside that, we need to continue to invest in this ability to test, understand and mitigate risks. It is interesting hearing the contributions of the Governor of the Bank of England recently, looking at what AISI put in its blog and focusing in on this safety science and maturing things as quickly as possible as the place for us all to be focusing.

Q53 Chair: Let us come back to some of those questions. Rob Sherman, what is the assurance that you can give to the Committee that Meta systems are not capable of harming either people or businesses in this country?

Rob Sherman: Chair, thank you so much. Thanks to the Committee for the opportunity to be here and for this important conversation. We are sitting at a critical moment in the development of this technology, and thinking about how to build it safely is critical. When we think about how we should approach building AI safely, we think about the appreciable benefits of the technology. Our particular approach is on a concept called personal superintelligence, which is around taking this technology and making it available to help individual people reach their potential. We realise that we are only going to do that if we build technology that is safe and trusted. Our approach is to use multiple layers of safeguards, including both internal and external ones, in order to make sure that we are shipping models and products that are safe.

Q54 Chair: Your conclusion is that your model today is what—safe, risky or dangerous?

Rob Sherman: We have what we call a scaling framework, which talks about the standards that we use for evaluating our models as we are building them and deploying them. That looks at three different areas: cyber-security, chemical and biological weapons, and loss of control. In each of those areas, we evaluate our models as we are developing them and before release to see what the level of risk is. If we concluded that it was not safe to ship a model, we would not release it.

Q55 Chair: To be clear, the commitment you are giving to the Committee this afternoon is that the models that you have available are safe.

Rob Sherman: Correct. If we felt that a model was not safe to release, we would not release it.

Anthropic identified three incidents affecting three different organisations where the evaluator allowed internet access when normal safeguards were absent. In May 2026, a Gemini system gained unauthorised access to systems affecting three real companies. Those incidents are known. Lord Booth-Smith: We published a blog post on Friday that detailed nine incidents that affected US entities, and they were informed. Obviously, as and when the process is complete, we will update and report to the affected entities any incidents we uncover.

Q57 Chair: At this stage, you cannot assure us that there are not more to come.

Lord Booth-Smith: I am not close to the technical process and it would be wrong to suggest that I can give you that assurance. I cannot. We are going through the process right now. Q58 Chair: There may be more incidents reported.

Q59 Chair: Okay. Tom.

Tom Duff Gordon: It is a similar answer, but first of all, at OpenAI we certainly apologise if our agents access Government websites in ways that are unexpected or ways that we do not direct them to.

Chair: I am sure that everyone will be reassured to hear that.

Tom Duff Gordon: Similarly, after the Hugging Face incident, which occurred in July, we launched a very extensive look-back investigation, looking at our models that were pre-deployment in training to see whether they had gone and visited other websites. You are probably aware of what happened in Australia and other places. There is a very deep, ongoing investigation.

There was also a report in the press recently in the UK that one of our agents had accessed statistics from a UK Government website. To be absolutely crystal clear to the Committee, this was publicly available data and the Government said that there had not been a breach of their cyber-defences. Nevertheless, we notified. Our commitment is to notify whenever there are incidents such as that.

I will say that sometimes, when these agents go to the website, if they are acting in a way that we do not expect, we do not think it is necessarily on us to disclose that publicly. It is on the recipient, because there is potentially a vulnerability in that website. From a security perspective, it is almost incumbent on the Government to say, “We have had these reports.” It is incumbent on us to report them, but I do not think that it would be responsible for us to say immediately, “We found this kind of credential on the internet that allowed an agent to do X,” because we think that that opens the door to bad actors. Our commitment is to be very transparent through that investigation and to make sure that the Government know as soon as we know that there has been activity that we have not expected.

Q60 Chair: Are we fully aware of any other major incidents that may have occurred, or is there still news that may come?

Tom Duff Gordon: Hugging Face was by far and away the most significant incident that we have seen. I do not want to trivialise them, but all the other incidents, or most of them, have been data retrieval, so an agent has gone to a Government website to get information.

Q61 Chair: Do you think that there are more to come, or can you assure us that that is it?

Tom Duff Gordon: I cannot assure the Committee or you, Chair, that that is it. We are going through that as fast as we can. As soon as we identify that, we will notify the Government. There is a handful of others, but there may be more.

Q62 Chair: Mr Larter, what assurance can you give us?

Owen Larter: There are a few important pieces here. With our frontier safety framework, we talked about the importance of testing, identifying risk and mitigating it. This is where we, as Google DeepMind, benefit from being part of Google, which has almost 30 years’ experience of securing information across various different services. With our latest model, Gemini 4 Argon, which is forthcoming, we have very secure sandboxes that we are using whenever we are doing any high-risk training or testing.

Q63 Chair: Are you happy that we are not going to learn about more incidents, or major incidents, in the weeks ahead?

Owen Larter: I would distinguish the one incident that you mentioned in relation to Google DeepMind, which was in connection with a testing partner. The system slowed down and stopped before it was able to do any harm. It did not break out of a secure sandbox.

Q64 Chair: You do not think there will be more news of incidents to come. This is your opportunity to let us know that there are other problems.

Owen Larter: I am not aware of any other incidents.

Q65 Chair: Okay. Mr Sherman.

Rob Sherman: When we develop and test our models, it is important for us to have a range of safeguards in the way that we build them. That is one example of many safeguards that we should have in place as models are running so that there are checks and balances to make sure that models do not behave in unpredictable ways. It is incumbent on us at Meta, and all the companies on the panel, to adopt safeguards in the way that we build.

Rob Sherman: The major incident that I am aware of is the one that you mentioned.

Q68 Chair: You are not aware of them, but you cannot necessarily swear on behalf of the company.

Rob Sherman: I am not aware, and the company is not aware, of similar instances to the one that you described, but it is important, if we are acting responsibly, to have safeguards to look for those and guard against them.

Q69 Alex Sobel: I have a follow-up to Tom. GPT-6.1 Astra was due to be released late last month. That release did not occur and it was reported that that was due to high levels of deception after alignment tests and that the model failed to accurately disclose actions it did or did not take. We know that today’s models are the least capable models we will see going forward. Can you tell us why GPT-6.1 Astra was not released? What did it show that concerned you so much that you did not release it?

Tom Duff Gordon: Thank you for the question. You are absolutely right. We decided not to release it because it did not score high enough on our alignment tests. It did not refuse enough when we were testing certain things and we were not comfortable that it had progressed in terms of alignment from 6.0. We actually saw a regression in the alignment of that model.

Now, 6.0 was our most aligned model, and much more aligned than 5.6. While 6.1 is still an aligned model and it was going in the right direction, we noticed a regression in alignment and because of that we decided not to deploy it. That is evidence that OpenAI takes safety really seriously. We are not racing to the bottom. We are not deploying models that we do not think are safe, and we are pausing training where we see significant incidents such as the Hugging Face incident.

Q70 Alex Sobel: In plain English, what do regression and alignment mean?

Tom Duff Gordon: It means that it was not as good as the previous model in doing its tasks in a way that we would want ethically, based on human values.

Q71 Leigh Ingham: Tom, specifically on the incident with Australia, our report says that you guys discovered that in mid-August, but Australia was not notified until 10 September. Coupled with what you just said about not notifying Governments when your agents do things that they are not expected to do, to me as a legislator, that is really concerning. I want to understand why there was such a significant gap, but also who has decided that legislators should not be made aware when agents are doing things that they should not be doing with Government data. Tom Duff Gordon: We apologised. One of our very senior executives flew down to Australia to be in a committee like this and to say that we are very clear-eyed about the fact that we did not do as well as we could have done, particularly in that Australia situation, and we could have and should have notified earlier.

There are all sorts of questions internally with regard to what point you notify at. Is it as soon as you spot some activity that you think is irregular? Is it after you have evaluated that and figured out the damage? As we will perhaps come on to, this is why we called publicly, as much as five weeks ago, for durable and mandatory capability-based requirements.

One of those in particular that we think could make sense would be common incident reporting. Then you would have standardisation about what to report and categories for different types of severity. Some of these incidents are information retrieval but done in an ad hoc and unintended way. Others are breaking in through the front door and stealing someone’s passwords. There are different levels.

Precisely to your point about the role of legislators, we would humbly submit that it is to think about whether there is a mandatory requirement that is suitable around incident reporting, so that we know who to notify, when to notify and how to categorise the events. That would also have a really beneficial implication for the regulator or AISI, because they would then be able to look, from different companies, at those different incidents and see apples with apples, spot patterns and help to make the overall ecosystem safer.

The answer to your question is that we took too long. We apologise for that. We are moving much faster, but we think that there can be more done with regard to standardising incident reporting.

Q75 Dan Aldridge: Tom, what is keeping you up at night?

Q76 Dan Aldridge: What would those vulnerabilities look like? This feels quite abstract, and I am aware that it is an abstract policy space.

Tom Duff Gordon: Let me try to explain it in a different way. What has kept us—Governments, corporations, schools and hospitals—safe up until now will not necessarily keep us safe moving forward. Owen Larter: I will maybe make three points. This is a critically important time to get right. There is a lot of opportunity here around AI. I want to make sure that the UK is right at the forefront of that. Every country around the world is trying to work out how to get AI right. This is a critical part of the discussion.
