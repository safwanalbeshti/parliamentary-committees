Examination of witnesses

Katie Barnes, Dr Adam Bower and Lee Willcox-Jones.

The Chair: Today we are holding our second session on space resilience. May I ask the witnesses to introduce themselves?

Katie Barnes: Good afternoon. I am the executive director of the National Preparedness Commission. We are an independent and politically neutral body, set up about six years ago to promote policy and related changes that will leave the UK better prepared for major shocks and disasters.

Dr Adam Bower: Good afternoon. I am a senior lecturer in international relations at the University of St Andrews, where, among other things, I work on outer space security and governance issues.

Lee Willcox-Jones: Good afternoon. I am from the Satellite Application Catapult, which exists to support the growth of the UK economy by helping with the development and adoption of space data and technology. I also have a broader defence background, which I understand we may draw on today.

The Chair: Thank you. We have about three-quarters of an hour and a whole series of questions to pose to you from around the table. Perhaps I could start with the landscape of the satellite sector, its criticality to our wider services—the critical services that we depend on—and what disruption would look like. What do you see as the greatest concern about what that disruption would look like? How damaging could it be?

Lee Willcox-Jones: I would start by framing the space sector and resilience around it through the lens of the services that space is delivering in the terrestrial domain. Through that, I would look at the space segment, the orbital segment and the ground segment—both ground stations and user terminals—and then the link between them. I would bring in non-space resilience measures as well—options that can back up or be backed up by space capability.

To your point on the greatest threat, for me it is a cascade of failures across multiple terrestrial sectors, looking at it through that terrestrial lens, especially if any disruption or degradation is a longer-term outage, which would lead to societal and economic impacts. I draw especially on PNT—positioning, navigation and timing—in particular jamming and spoofing issues. Earlier this year, the Secure World Foundation found that in active conflicts at the moment, those non-kinetic means of counterspace activities are prevalent.

For me, PNT is the key area of concern, particularly where timing dependence is not necessarily well understood. I give the example of maritime shipping. In October this year, the Maritime and Coastguard Agency advised shippers and port authorities to understand how they might be able to operate without GPS. That is not just for navigation itself; GPS is tied into other systems within the ships for their communications and the way in which they operate. It is not just a case of going back to charts and maps and being able to operate in a way that is as efficient.

I will give a couple of facts to back that up. In June 2026, 970 ships per day were jammed in the Strait of Hormuz. There is evidence at the moment of these activities taking place, including in the Baltic region, and there is concern around our ability to operate in a jammed or spoofed environment. A point I would like to get across is that understanding our resilience also involves exercising our countermeasures or our ability to operate when such activity is present. That is a key part of how we could mitigate or increase resilience within that sector in particular.

The Chair: We are told that 18% of our GDP—in essence, our economy—is dependent on satellite services. Is that typical of a developed nation or are we an outlier?

Lee Willcox-Jones: I am afraid I do not have the facts in front of me to support a response to that. I can certainly find out and submit that to the committee.

The Chair: Dr Bower?

Dr Adam Bower: Thanks very much. As we have already heard and as many submissions have indicated to this committee, it is correct to think of resilience in a very holistic manner. If I may, I would like to highlight one of the concerns that I have in working with colleagues from across a variety of disciplines: the vulnerability that satellites have to natural or human-made debris. I want to raise that in the context of interrogating an assumption that is quite widely held that the proliferation of satellites or the creation of larger constellations, particularly in low-earth orbit, is a strategy that can provide the kind of resilience that we would be looking for. It makes sense in certain contexts, but the risks to those satellites from human-generated or naturally occurring debris in earth orbit are a greater threat than is often appreciated.

It makes a lot of sense to shift much of the work that we do through satellites from traditionally and historically very large, expensive and bespoke satellites down to these commercially made constellations. It can make a lot of sense for resilience purposes, and that is necessary in order to provide continuous or regular global coverage for communications such as high-resolution imagery, for example. Proliferation is dispersing these risks, and that might make sense from a resilience perspective. It could also be a strategy for deterrence by denial, as I think we have seen in some of the submissions to the committee. Denying an adversary the ability to knock out one node in a constellation would probably have limited effect on the overall useability of a satellite network.

It is worth thinking about the ways in which that proliferation may not provide a total solution in the way that is often assumed. Orbital space is very big, but constellations are, interestingly, concentrated within what we think of as the earth’s orbital environment, in a couple of ways. These individual satellites are now much smaller than they used to be. Individually, they are not large targets but if you think about them in the aggregate, they have a tremendously large surface area and cross-section, including the satellite buses themselves, their solar panels and the like. They are deployed not randomly but in distributed planes and at very specific orbital altitudes—I am thinking of something like the SpaceX Starlink constellation—within low earth orbit.

In those respective ways, the satellites are moving very quickly but they are concentrated in parts of earth orbit, and that presents a series of challenges for what is often called space traffic management, in the sense of how we manage it, much like we do very successfully in an air environment. We do not really have anything equivalent to air traffic control for outer space, and that is putting additional burdens on operators themselves, including commercial and governmental.

Taken as a whole—this is the point I would like to emphasise—those constellations are proportionally much more vulnerable to the risks of debris strikes. There is an awful lot of debris already in earth orbit and more is accumulating as the number of satellites grows. It can be natural micrometeoroids or existing debris, but the thing I want to highlight briefly for the committee’s attention is the risk that this could also be an adversarial strategy. It could be the use of so-called kinetic or destructive anti-satellite weapons to disable, disrupt or even destroy UK or allied space capabilities.

I will give two brief scenarios and then I will wrap up my comments. One is that, as we have seen in the past, a number of Governments have tested destructive anti-satellite systems—they have deliberately destroyed one of their own satellites. Four countries have done this since 2007. One could envision a scenario where an adversary deliberately destroys one of its own satellites—so this is not a hostile attack and would not be seen as a resort to armed violence by the affected party, but in destroying one of its own satellites, it generates debris that then imposes costs on other operators, including, potentially, the loss of satellites, certainly making the operations of large constellations more difficult. Alternatively, we could envision this as part of a hostile attack, which would probably be a larger part of an armed conflict.

To conclude, it is well recognised that there is a need for greater global governance mechanisms. Some of this is ongoing; importantly, the United Kingdom has taken a leadership role in space security governance in recent years. There are a number of promising possibilities, and I would be happy to speak to that further, if that is of interest to the committee.

The Chair: Katie Barnes, from the viewpoint of the preparedness commission, what are your greatest concerns around the provision of critical services?

Katie Barnes: There are three parts to this. One of them is about looking through the lens of satellite services as used in the resilience system. I am thinking specifically of satcoms, for example, used as an emergency response capability and often as the back-up capability for telecoms when they are not available.

The second is the proliferation of satellite services as embedded in everyday critical services, essential functions and everyday systems. Here, PNT is the obvious primary example, particularly the T for timing. The instances of PNT embedded in systems are enormous—too many to count. My concern would be whether we know where all of those are and whether there are resilience plans should there be disruption to those services, particularly around PNT as a trusted part of an automated service—the PNT signal comes and the rest of the service or the process runs because that has come through without human oversight or relevant flags around the integrity of that signal. There are also the safety critical services—for example, its use in automated vehicles, rail traffic control or anything where there is a positioning element that will help avoid collisions or other risks. Then there is the integration of PNT in particular as operational technology in critical infrastructure and services.

Those are the two buckets I would look at: satellite services as part of resilience and resilience response; and satellite services as part of everyday life—and so national resilience. I also have thoughts and concerns around the oversight governance awareness strategy, but I think that will probably come up in later questions.

The Chair: We will come back to that. My question is about the 18% of our economy being underpinned by satellite systems. Is that something that you have a view on? Do you have any idea what other nations do?

Katie Barnes: I am afraid I do not have any data on what other nations are doing. The only anecdotal piece I have is from a round table we held after the publication of our paper that was written for us by the Royal Institute of Navigation in 2023. The PNT office contributed an anecdote to the discussion. It said that the US and other countries are putting in place policies to reduce their reliance on GNSS in some of their government departments and critical systems. The reliance bit is being looked at in some other countries. The economic bit is different, and I do not know how we compare.

Baroness Kidron: Does the space strategy go far enough in its ambitions to improve civil resilience to space disruptions? I have some more detailed questions to come, so I am asking for quite a top-level response from each of you. Katie, do you want to go first this time?

Katie Barnes: The space strategy does not try to go too far. As I read it, it is a sector participation and growth strategy. There is not much about resilience in there. The key stakeholder, from a resilience perspective, seems to be defence.

Dr Adam Bower: I work principally on international relations and the ways in which the UK Government would relate internationally, so I will defer to my esteemed colleagues on this question.

Lee Willcox-Jones: The direction of travel for the space strategy and the strategies that preceded it—the national space strategy and the defence space strategy—is absolutely good. The bringing together of different government departments and different elements of government need, as well as the one-government approach, are good things. From a resilience perspective, I am keen for resilience not to fall between departments as implementation of the strategy takes place. We can go more into that, if you like.

Baroness Kidron: What I wanted to follow up on is something that Katie has already referred to: the organisations that are providing critical national infrastructure and that are dependent on satellite provision, but perhaps not in a direct way. Do you think there is enough awareness in the system of that dependence?

Katie Barnes: The short answer is no; I suspect that there is not enough. Part of the problem is the way we think about critical national infrastructure in general. There is a one-size-fits-all designation, whereas infrastructure—or, more correctly, the services provided by the infrastructure on satellites—underpins so many of the other CNI sectors, and so it becomes a more important level of criticality.

What I do not know is how initiatives such as the CNI Knowledge Base are configured. I do not know whether it is looking at the kind of questions that you want to be answered in this committee. Is it configured to understand the dependencies on satellite services in other CNI? More importantly, does it understand how CNI configures and reconfigures to deliver the capabilities that underpin essential national services? That is the end of the telescope that I would start at.

I am not sure whether there is anyone looking at what we are doing on resilience planning, as well as resilience risk mitigation and preparedness, from that end or taking that view across the piece. That is partly because ownership and the responsibility for operating CNI sectors, services, et cetera is so fragmented. What I do not see is where that coheres. It certainly does not go into the space strategy, and I do not think it coheres in the resilience action plan—it is barely mentioned. The action plan mentioned a few mechanisms that could be used to assess and test, but it is not part of the plan itself.

Baroness Kidron: That is interesting because it came up during the passage of the cyber Bill, when we asked: where does this come together, if you have it in each area separately under its own regulatory umbrella? Do you have a suggestion about where it would usefully land and come together, or is that something for someone else?

Katie Barnes: Personally, my suggestion is that resilience as a whole should have a much higher and unified place in the machinery of governance. The report published by the House of Lords National Resilience Committee this morning said pretty much the same thing: there should be a Cabinet-level position that is responsible solely for resilience and looking across the piece, as well as a mechanism for feeding into the department. It recommends having a head of preparedness in every department, so that there is a mechanism for translating the intent down.

On mechanisms, one of the challenges is that the way in which resilience is conceived is focused very strongly on individual risks, particularly those on the NRR and the classified version of that. What we are talking about here is not on that risk register. As I said, the resilience action plan mentions mechanisms. For things such as the catastrophic risk boards with lead government departments that were trailed in the resilience action plan, the example was for a national power outage. You could do a version where you are looking at what would happen if there were disruption or unavailability of satellite services, but you will not do that if what they are doing is going through the risk register, because the risk register bit of satellite services is just about PNT.

I understand that other things, such as the assurance boards that are being run by UKRA, are following the risk register’s risks—but I might be wrong in saying that. If that is all they are doing, they are missing an opportunity to look at the dependencies around critical services’ essential functions. I would be tempted to ask: what would happen if that capability were taken away, rather than if that particular risk materialised?

Baroness Kidron: I am going to come to Lee, if he has anything to add. Just before I finish, Katie, can you tell me whether the Government are doing enough work with local authorities and community groups to understand and to test severe disruption?

Katie Barnes: I do not know what they have done on this particular issue. Across the piece, there is too little awareness, first, of what satellite-based services are and, secondly, whether they are in our systems, where they are and how we are using them. Part of that is to do with terminology. I do not know where the advice is, except for the excellent advice, guidance and checklist that RIN has put together. I do not know how somebody would find that if they were looking for advice on resilience, for example. You could google “PNT resilience”, if you knew you were looking for it.

Baroness Kidron: If you had thought about it.

Katie Barnes: If you googled “PNT resilience”, you would get posted to the PNT office, and it would link to RIN. That is how you would find that guidance. It strikes me that there could be a much more proactive dissemination of that.

Baroness Kidron: Lee, do you have anything to add to that?

Lee Willcox-Jones: I agree with what Katie has said. She mentioned the national PNT office, which is worth raising at this point as an example of how cross-government collaboration can work. As PNT involves cross-cutting space capability, the PNT office can bring together the different entities and departments involved or that could be impacted by the degradation of that service. Each department still owns its own risk, but the office co-ordinates the mitigations, and a number of different departments will have the same mitigations. That is a useful example of how government collaboration can work. I am not necessarily advocating for a national PNT office for every technology area or resilience area, but it is worth highlighting.

Lord Arbuthnot of Edrom: I declare my interest as a commissioner on the National Preparedness Commission. I will ask a question about our vulnerability to cascading failures and the value of exercising to guard against them. Mr Willcox-Jones, you answered both of those queries in the first minute that you began to speak, but how well do you think industry understands, first, the vulnerability to cascades and, secondly, the value of exercising? What can we do to improve that understanding? That is my first question.

Lee Willcox-Jones: I do not think that resilience is a fixed measure; it is constantly changing. Therefore, to your point on exercising, it is a really powerful way of understanding where we sit within our ability to be resilient and to respond to and recover from incidents. Exercising must be a way in which we understand and keep testing and refining our ability to respond.

To your point on whether the industry understands, I do not have the information on how the industry as a whole views resilience measures, testing and exercising. It is certainly a priority area that government and others can support in order to embed that exercising culture within capabilities and technologies that, in and of themselves, must have resilience built into them.

Lord Arbuthnot of Edrom: My second question is to Dr Bower. You talked about space debris very convincingly. A couple of years ago, Michael Mainelli produced proposals for bonds to help fund the clearing of space debris. Has that got anywhere? Do you think it has any prospects of getting anywhere?

Dr Adam Bower: One of the things I would highlight at the beginning is that there are commercial operators that are beginning to develop what is known as active debris removal as a strategy for removing particularly large objects which would proportionately be the biggest risks. One of those is joint UK company, Astroscale; I imagine this committee will speak with its members, or they will have made submissions in other places.

I am not familiar specifically with the idea of bonds, although I have heard other ideas about trying to think of ways to incentivise operators to behave better. One of the things that is important to note is that not for all, but for much of the debris—particularly the largest pieces, such as the rocket bodies—there are technologies now that would allow for those not to be left in orbit. SpaceX is an industry leader in returning portions of their rockets back and making them reusable. Economic incentives may be one of the ways in which that can be done. As I say, active debris removal has been proposed as a strategy. There are a number of companies that are testing these kinds of capabilities.

Something else that is interesting to think about here is a phrase that we often hear: dual-use capabilities—the ability for something to be either a benign or a threatening technology. Active debris removal is exactly one of those kinds of categories, insofar as these are technologies that would be immensely useful for removing probably not all debris but perhaps the most worrying individual pieces. We have seen spacefaring nations practising these kinds of capabilities to capture an object, and so you can imagine how that could also be used in an adversarial context too.

It is interesting to highlight the ways in which commercial operators and Governments are working together, which is probably a theme for this committee as a whole. To your point about bonds, there are ideas around economic incentives that could work in that direction. Right now, what I see happening is Governments being willing to offer contracts for the development of technologies and test beds that could then become an operational capability in the future.

Catherine Atkinson: Can I start with Katie? You have already been talking about the higher governance mechanism that would be desirable, and you set out the Cabinet level, but can I ask about the Government’s current governance structures? How adequate do you think they are for managing the impacts from disruption to space-based services?

Katie Barnes: Yes. From a resilience perspective, the model that is in place is of a lead government department owning different risks and different CNI sectors. That is inadequate in this kind of scenario, because the services are used across so many different CNI sectors, and so what is missing is the co-ordination and coherence piece. I know that for systemic and cross-departmental risks, the Cabinet Office has a co-ordinating role—but that is in response.

What is missing is the understanding of how to ensure resilience in the face of disruption to enabling services such as these, and how to ensure that actions are taken across government departments, in step with each other. So there is a whole co-ordination piece that is missing. There is a lot of renewed energy in cross-government working—a one-government approach and so on—but unless that becomes structural rather than just something that happens because that is what people think they need to do, then there is a risk that it will not be systematic enough.

Catherine Atkinson: Moving to international governance, Adam, you contributed to a paper that argued that “The existing space governance system is fragmented and … out of date, including five multilateral treaties that are more than half a century old”. What impact do you think that has on UK resilience?

Dr Adam Bower: The starting point is that this has been noted increasingly in recent years. The primary legal instruments that we have are very old, as you mentioned. They do not directly contemplate commercial operators or an environment in which we have so many different space operators.

One of the ways this matters, which has been noted in public by leaders of the US Space Force and Space Command, is that we do not really have a lot of rules of the road when we think about military space operations, which overlap here because they can create, as I indicated, harmful debris that can affect civilian systems as well.

Broadly, we are in an international environment that is very fragmented in global governance—that is the bad news part. But if I can offer one small piece of good news, it is that there may be limited opportunities for co-operation. The opportunity I would highlight comes off the back of my initial comments. Because space debris is such a threat to commercial operators, civilian systems and militaries—and particularly to the spacefaring actors that have the largest number of objects in orbit, particularly the United States and China—it seems to me that there is at least a self-interested argument to limit the deliberate destruction of satellites, as one very small piece of a much wider global space governance puzzle. The benefit there is that it overlaps with existing obligations and commitments not to create long-lived debris in orbit. That already exists in a variety of civilian-style agreements—you could read it as going all the way back to the Outer Space Treaty, as well as things such as the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee. My point is that there may be instances where security, civilian and even environmental logics might be overlapping.

We have not got too far yet, but one of the things that has been identified in successive UK-led initiatives on the open-ended working groups, which have been looking at developing rules and norms of responsible behaviour, is an agreed ban on the testing of these anti-satellite systems that create debris. There is no formal agreement on this yet, but the United States voluntarily agreed to forgo these tests in 2022 under the Biden Administration. The United Kingdom and others have agreed to follow that, even though many of those countries have never tested.

There are limited opportunities for self-interested co-operation, but more broadly, I still see the space governance environment as fragmented. There are, frankly, differing views on the issues that are of greatest importance and the mechanisms to get us there, but I highlight the possibility of limiting debris as one area in which there actually could be some room for progressive development.

Catherine Atkinson: Building on debris, which seems to be a real theme of the evidence today, in written evidence you cautioned against the proliferation of satellites because of the risk of collision, and you have been speaking very powerfully on that again today. The committee has also heard evidence that proliferated architecture is exactly what the UK needs to improve resilience. How do you square that circle?

Dr Adam Bower: The way I would approach it is to think about how proliferated a system needs to be and how many satellites are necessary. The one most people will be familiar with is SpaceX’s Starlink system, which, by my last count, now has over 12,000 satellites in orbit. There is a logic to building very large constellations for certain kinds of use cases—global internet connectivity might be one. But it strikes me that in many other use cases, you could actually have quite useful systems, whether for intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance or even communications, that do not require thousands or tens of thousands of satellites—and now there are even proposals for hundreds of thousands of satellites. So one way I would attempt to square that circle is to think about what the upper limits actually need to be for the use cases.

The argument that I would make, and my colleagues have made, is that it is not necessarily a certain number of satellites; the problem is this increasing, very rapid growth and the aggregation of those into larger and larger systems.

The way I would think about it is: what do Governments, whether at the level of the Ministry of Defence or civilian operators, need to achieve the use cases they have in mind and can they envision doing that with a more limited number of satellites? It is possible to change the orientation of satellites in low Earth orbit to reduce the number, potentially, and that would be one way I would think about trying to limit the overall growth. What I take away is that, proportionally, debris becomes a bigger and bigger problem as you scale up the number of satellites, as do the operating challenges between operators as well.

Baroness Tyler of Enfield: I would like to pursue the issue of the cross-government governance arrangements with Ms Barnes; I know she has already spoken about it. The strategic defence review called for a reinvigorated Cabinet sub-committee or ministerial group. The most recent list of Cabinet sub-committees that came out did not list a space sub-committee, and my understanding is that the Minister for Space does not sit on the National Security Council. Given this, do you feel that, building on what you have already said, the Government should now follow through on that recommendation to set up a Cabinet sub-committee specifically focused on space? If they did, what difference would it make and what would its impact likely be?

Katie Barnes: It is an interesting question. If we were to do that specifically for space, what are the other capabilities we would need to do that for? It goes back to my earlier point about critical national infrastructure being critical only because it combines to deliver services and essential functions; it is not critical in and of itself and some are more critical than others because other CNI sectors depend on them. So, if we are doing that for space, maybe there are other CNI sectors we need to do that for.

The difference it would make would be to provide that coherent view of all the stakeholder interests and all the perhaps competing interests in what happens in space. I said at the beginning that I have comments on strategy and governance, and that is the bit that is missing for me; there is not that coherent bit of governance of space in all the different ways that it is characterised and used. For example, the SDR talks about space as an operating domain but also as where the defence effort houses and operates its integrated digital targeting web, which is central to the success of the integrated force. It is a key stakeholder need for a big capability; that has come through really strongly in the space strategy.

The civilian side of it and the civil stakeholder requirements of space and satellite services are not represented with anywhere near as much clarity or force in that strategy, so whose strategy are they in, and which place brings those together and weighs one against another? So it would benefit from understanding the trade-offs.

Baroness Tyler of Enfield: Do Dr Bower or Mr Willcox-Jones want to add anything?

Lee Willcox-Jones: I agree in the sense that a cohering function particularly for resilience in the space domain, again through that lens of how space contributes to terrestrial capabilities, is important. It is important to focus not just on space itself but on the terrestrial services it services.

In an NAO audit in March this year, there was confusion between departments and no agreed resilience levels, specifically relating to space weather as one of the threats that the orbital environment has. That is perhaps evidence of not allowing resilience to slip through the gaps between departments and evidence for a central cohering function.

Lord Tunnicliffe: To what extent is the present level of resilience dependent on international co-operation, and what would the consequence be of a breakdown of such co-operation?

Lee Willcox-Jones: I think that working with allies and partners, using our and their capabilities, delivers us resilience. We have talked about diversified and proliferated capabilities on orbit, and about how perhaps too many individual satellites will present a resilience hazard, in and of themselves. But having access to allies’ and partners’ capabilities is a strength for resilience, particularly if we have receivers in the terrestrial domain that are able to operate using different constellations and different providers; if one service is degraded or denied, another service can be substituted into that capability in the terrestrial domain and ensure that the service continues.

We must also understand the dependence, and we must have secure alternatives, building redundancy into the system. Strong contracting with commercial entities in the UK and internationally is part of the picture, and a strong UK sector is a key component of having international resilience. With a strong industrial base, underpinned by a strong skills base in the UK, we have companies that build fantastic capabilities and solve problems. We can support our allies and partners with those challenges and problems, and vice versa. I think that reliance is something that needs to be understood and dependencies need to be understood, but working together also brings us additional resilience.

Katie Barnes: I totally agree with what Lee has said, but I think there is another aspect to partnership working, which is the cost of it and the maintenance of those relationships, which sometimes does not come cheap. I think of partnerships and alliances as relatively cheap when all the interests are aligned and the alliance works well. But if interests start to diverge, for example, if we are not as successful in our ambitions of growth in the sector and if we do not make ourselves an indispensable partner, then keeping our seat at the table in some of these discussions might become quite expensive in terms of time and effort. Therefore, partnerships are hugely important in amplifying capability, but they also bring vulnerabilities and risk with them.

The Chair: In the last couple of minutes, perhaps I could ask Lee about the commercial side. I am interested by the catapult and the work that is being done there. How good are the UK Government in supporting UK businesses in the satellite sector in terms of helping to develop and secure those products and services and ensure they are resilient? What have the UK Government been doing traditionally? How can they improve and is there a good practice in other countries that we could learn from?

Lee Willcox-Jones: I think there are some really good mechanisms across government that support organisations within the space sector to develop. The catapult works with businesses that have significant potential for growth and innovative capabilities, and we are part of that mechanism of supporting companies to flourish and to grow within the UK.

Perhaps there is an opportunity to bring together the different support mechanisms so that they are greater than the sum of their parts. We are quite inspired by the sovereign AI unit within UK Government, which provides funding and access to compute and supports with visas for skilled individuals at pace to support the growth of the AI sector. That bringing together of different support mechanisms, whether that is access to talent, capital, business support, grant funding, test facilities or regulators, in a really joined up way, offers a strong opportunity. I also note that the House of Lords space committee recommended long-term procurement and suggested that Government as an anchor customer is a way to go, and I agree with those comments.

The Chair: We have these incredible clusters in Glasgow that I have read about. It seems phenomenal that you have one business and there are spin-outs from that, and the sector grows. Is that something where you see that the UK Government—alongside the AI unit you have just mentioned—could be doing more in broader support for start-ups and scale-ups?

Lee Willcox-Jones: Could you reframe the question? Sorry, I am not quite—

The Chair: I am asking whether the UK Government are doing enough to assist with the financial scaling-up of businesses, and getting start-ups supported at the outset.

Lee Willcox-Jones: There is always more that can be done, for sure, but there are good mechanisms out there that the Government use to support the sector.

The Chair: Finally, Germany seems to be investing heavily in this area—something like five times as much into the sector. To your point about Government stimulating investment through demand, do you think there is there should be more ambition coming from the UK, Lee?

Lee Willcox-Jones: Yes, there certainly could be more ambition demonstrated in particular key capability areas. I do not really have the information or the data here to directly compare that to the German model. I understand that significant investment is certainly happening across the full spectrum of space capabilities, but I do not really have the data here to compare the two. I can of course work with my catapult colleagues to come back to the committee with a written piece of evidence.
