Examination of witnesses

Liz Webster, Peter Leary and Rahila Gupta.

Q17 Chair: Welcome to the Joint Committee on Human Rights public evidence session on security, safety, and protest. The Joint Committee on Human Rights is a committee of both Houses made up of 12 members from a broad range of political backgrounds. Today we will be looking into security, safety and protest and we will be hearing from three witnesses: Liz Webster, founder of Save British Farming; Peter Leary, deputy director of Palestine Solidarity Campaign; and Rahila Gupta, trustee of Southall Black Sisters.

As members will be aware, the sub judice resolutions of both Houses mean that live legal cases should not be discussed in parliamentary proceedings. Please remember to avoid referring to any live legal cases in our discussions today. I also remind the gallery that you are here to observe and not take part in the meeting in any way.

I will start the questions. You have all been involved in organising protests in the past few years. Have you noticed any change in the actions of police towards your events in that time? We will start with Mr Leary.

Peter Leary: Over the past three years in particular we have been involved in organising a large number of protests in response to what we regard, and others widely regard, as Israel’s genocide against the Palestinian people in Gaza. We have experienced a level of policing and type of policing that is not familiar to us from the past and it is not familiar to our members, colleagues, and partners who in some cases have a very long track record of organising protests and demonstrations, going back decades. That includes the routine use of the public order legislation to impose restrictions on our marches, restricting the time, duration, routes and end time of marches, and in some cases preventing us from protesting at places where we would have wished to protest—for example, at the BBC or the Israeli embassy or other sites that we regard as sites of legitimate protest that we want to protest at.

As I say, the use of conditions is a new experience. I will explain what I mean by that. We speak to the police prior to the demonstration and discuss the route of the march and the time we plan to set off, and we reach agreement on that. Then usually the day before or a couple of days before, the police impose conditions. Say we said we were assembling at 12 and will set off about 12.45. The police impose conditions saying, “You must have set off by 12.45”. Theoretically, if we are still there at 12.50, we could be charged with violating the order.

Similarly, people taking part in the march must follow that particular route, Again, theoretically, someone who leaves the march to go to the toilet, even, could potentially be in breach, in violation, of those orders imposed by the police. We say we hope to be finished by 4.30 and then a condition is imposed saying, “You must be finished by 4.30”, again, putting on extreme pressure. We often have speakers at the end of the demonstrations: Members of Parliament, trade unionists, Palestinians who have lost families—the Palestinian ambassador often speaks. People do not always stick to time but, because of the way in which these orders are used, it could be an arrestable offence if people are still speaking at 4.35 when we said we were aiming to finish at 4.30. That is a very new experience, and it has developed over the past few years.

Chair: I will ask a follow-up question before I move on to Liz Webster. You have said that technically, they could make arrests if you run over time. Have they made arrests?

Peter Leary: No one has been arrested for running over time, but we have been very fearful of that. We have had situations where the final speaker is speaking and we are getting agitated and the police are approaching the stage, and we are pushing that. We have had situations where conditions have been imposed. On one occasion, for example, conditions were imposed governing where people could be in various parts of Whitehall at various points during the day. People were arrested literally for finding themselves on the wrong side of the road at the wrong time. There can be confusion and uncertainty. The other thing, as I say, is that it creates a sort of chilling effect. We have also had occasions where speakers have not spoken because we were running out of time. That affects freedom of people’s expression and their ability to address the rally. While they have not been arrested, it has obviously had that suppressing effect.

Chair: Liz, what is your perspective on this?

Liz Webster: We did the first demonstration in London during 2020 and the Covid years. Our experience is probably a little bit the opposite. It was much tougher when we first started. It was the first time tractors had ever really been brought to London in any number, so the police were quite concerned about what we were going to do. They were helpful but they were sort of, “Don’t do this” and “Don’t do that”. There was a lot of mistrust to begin with. We did another bigger one in 2024, and they were going around checking our machinery and making sure it was legal. It was a slightly different protest because it was a moving vehicle protest. When we came to do the much bigger protests at the end of 2024 and in 2025, by then we had built a better relationship with the police. They knew who we were, and we spoke to them and had meetings with them ahead of the protests. We knew what the parameters were and we decided to work with them as much as we possibly could.

It improved. It was much tougher in 2020, probably because of the Covid restrictions as well. All in all, it got better for us. Also, I was clear with them that we never wanted to see anyone participating in a tractor protest go on the wrong side of the law. We were adamant that we did not want to see any farmers arrested. That would not have helped us or them.

Chair: Have you had any of the issues Peter has had with time restrictions, threats of arrest, people maybe straying off a tractor route and being arrested?

Liz Webster: Legally, there is a slight difference between a moving vehicle protest and a physical protest of people. They at times were saying, “We want you to finish at this time”, and you cannot always guarantee that you will get rid of people. Some tractor drivers decide to hang around and park in Whitehall afterwards and go for a drink. We did everything we could to make sure that we did what the police asked us, because we felt it was more important for the protest to go well and positively and we did not want to have a negative relationship with them.

When they came through to us and said, “We want you to clear Whitehall now”, that is exactly what we did. We did our best to do what the police asked us to do, but we had to consider that it is a vehicle protest and we did not want to get in the way of hospitals. We did not want anything that would cause significant problems for London or for the people in London, so we tried to do everything we could to work with the police as positively as possible, and that has helped us. We have built a good relationship with the Met and that has made it more positive, so it is an opposite experience.

Chair: Rahila, what is your perspective on this?

Rahila Gupta: First, thank you very much for inviting Southall Black Sisters to talk on this important subject. I want to make my comments a little bit broader than those of my colleagues here, and to start with the really simple point that we would not be here today if we had not been out there on the streets continuing to make our voices heard. The right to protest is very important to us, and it is also the right to belong, which I think is very important for racialised minorities. As we know, the right to belong is enshrined in Article 15 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and, of course, Hannah Arendt has talked about the right to have rights. I wanted to start with that introduction.

I do have some provisos, and it goes back to the fact that we cannot separate the policing of protests from our experience of policing generally. We come from a history of the Mangrove Nine, the Bradford Twelve and, most recently, the King’s Cross Three, when our director, who is sitting right behind me, was bitten in a racist incident on the Tube and the police arrested her for common assault. We fought the charges, which were dropped, but I am giving you this incident to explain how our experience of policing comes from a particular bias, if you like, but a bias that we feel is justified by our history.

This might feel a little bit too far back, but it is relevant to the point I want to make about the two-tier policing. I am sorry to use that term because I think it has been usurped by the likes of Nigel Farage, but I come at it from a totally different point of view. The Southall Black Sisters came up in 1979 when we had the National Front wanting provocatively to hold a meeting in Southall, a predominantly Black and Asian area. They brought in the special patrol group on horses, criminalising and arresting Black and Asian men. We are still living with that double-edged or light-touch policing of the far right, whose right to protest seems to be better protected than ours.

Having gone back 50 years, then fast-forward to last year when we organised a demonstration outside the Home Office to protest against earned settlement in the new immigration proposals—these are still proposals. We were really anxious that the far right would turn up and do a counterprotest, so we did not even advertise this demonstration on social media. You might say that is a bit of an own goal because it is self-defeating if you cannot bring the numbers together.

That was our anxiety—the men who had appeared outside asylum centres and immigration hostels. The situation is dominated by men who have criminal convictions for racial and sexual violence. They have been known to photograph women leaving these centres and to put that on social media. With these sorts of dangers, we were worried about how we mobilise, how we bring our women into what could be a potentially dangerous situation. Our entire work is about restoring confidence to women, reclaiming the streets, restoring their autonomy. This two-tiered policing is problematic.

The second proviso is that, unlike my colleagues here, as I have just mentioned, we have organised a few demos and mostly marches through Southall, but that is not our primary tool for protest. I will be talking from our experience of participating in marches, many of them organised by the Palestine Solidarity Campaign. From that experience, I would argue that the law has now caught up with policing. What I mean by that is that what were heavy-handed measures in the past now have legislative backing. It probably goes even further, in that, if you talk about things like the excessive noise clause or facial covering and so on—

Chair: We will come on to those issues later.

Rahila Gupta: Yes. I am not going to talk in detail. I am just going to say that those were proposals that the police may only have dreamt about in the past. If the law gives tools to the police that are not proportionate, any reassurances that the police will use those proportionately do not reassure us.

Q18 Afzal Khan: To what extent do you see the police as the facilitators of protest and to what extent as an obstacle? Have you changed your protest approach in response to the changing restrictions?

Peter Leary: As I indicated in my previous answer, we always make sure we inform the police. We discuss things with the police ahead of our protests. We understand that they have a responsibility to facilitate protests, but also, we want to do so because we are bringing large numbers of people. These are often very large events and contain a wide range of people. Disabled people, elderly people, people with children and young families come on these marches, and we want to make sure that they can do so safely and participate effectively in the aims of the protest, as well as addressing the practical things. We want to make sure that the roads are closed so that we are not walking headlong into traffic.

For all those reasons, we always seek to engage with the police. I would not characterise the approach of the police as one in which they are seeking to facilitate us to exercise our rights. I think they are seeking to use the powers at their disposal to limit and curtail our rights. That includes, as I said, preventing us for several years now from marching to or from the BBC—somewhere we have wanted to be part of our national demonstrations—from protesting at the Israeli embassy, and sometimes imposing restrictions that make it very difficult to protest in a normal or standard way at all.

An ironic example is the day the Crime and Policing Bill, which contained a number of what we regard as restrictive anti-protest clauses, was debated in Parliament. We organised a protest outside Parliament on the day. We discussed with the police the location of the protest and where it should be sited. With an hour to go, they imposed a restriction banning us from using megaphones. Megaphones have been a feature of protests and demonstrations since before I was born. That means it is very difficult to communicate with the people who have gathered.

The day we held the rally we had to shout, which gave us all a sore throat. We were banned at very short notice from using that normal feature you would expect, which would enable you to communicate with people who have come together and to get the message of your protest across—but also for safety reasons, if you want them to move and so on.

Certainly, our feeling is not that the police are seeking to facilitate us to make our protests—throughout the country, in fact. Many times we have felt that their approach is that we are not really people going about our lawful business at all. We are people engaged in a quasi-criminal activity by taking part in a protest. Protestors perhaps exist over here, and the public exists over there, and the police’s role is to protect the public rather than to facilitate those members of the public who wish to express their democratic rights.

Liz Webster: In the beginning, after the farm tax was brought in, there were efforts by other people in the farming community who wanted to block supermarket depots. Going back 20 years, that would not have been illegal. I had the view that from our perspective, we wanted to run a PR campaign, and we wanted to get a positive press and to get people on our side. I did everything that I could to stop that block happening, despite the fact that that probably would have received more attention, because food supplies would have been interrupted and people would have understood the impact of what the farm tax means to them personally.

Were we able to do what we wanted to do? No, but what we decided to do was to keep within the parameters of the law. In my view, the more that we try to push the law and put the police in a position where they are going to start being heavy-handed with us, it would probably give more freedoms for the lawmakers to inflict more laws on protestors.

There were two things on my mind. One was getting the message through, which is about everyone’s food, not just the farmers, and also trying to show that protesting in this day and age is not the same as it was when Emmeline Pankhurst strapped herself to the railings, because we have social media and television 24/7.

I was very keen to try to get a positive campaign out to make sure that people heard the message, and to make it as orderly as possible. Controlling people in a protest is like herding cats, and that is difficult. I also respect the job that the police are trying to do. It is not their fault that the laws are coming through. Maybe some of them agree with the laws, maybe some of them do not. We are all humans, at the end of the day. I felt that we needed to try to get to a point where we were saying, “Look, these things are happening to British farming, which means it will impact your food, which impacts all of our human rights as well as access to food”, and to try to do that as positively as we can.

That is how we decided to approach the situation with protesting, and I think it has been more successful from that point of view because we found a narrow way through it. It was Covid that got us into the vehicles, because we would not have been allowed to do a physical protest in 2020, but we were allowed to. It was outside of the remits of the Covid laws to do a moving vehicular protest. In doing so, it gave us the opportunity. We have big machines, which is fortunate, so we are able to be seen. It looks as if we are being treated differently but we are not, really. We are trying to do the same thing; we are trying to communicate. I suppose that is what protesting is. We are trying to communicate so that people know what is going on in their country and what it means for them.

There is an element of people wanting to express their frustration with whatever they are protesting about, and, as organisers, I think that responsibility is enormous because you cannot control everybody and the law is quite frightening. I do not want to go to jail, and I do not want anyone else to go to jail and end up with a criminal record either. It is a big thing to do a big protest. It is a huge amount of responsibility.

Rahila Gupta: As I said earlier, we are not primarily organisers of marches, we are participants, so I do not have anything to communicate.

Chair: We will now move to a series of questions from Baroness Lane-Fox on police engagement and then understanding of protest law.

Q19 Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: You talked a bit about this earlier, so I do not want you to have to repeat answers. I think we all appreciate you have slightly different levels of direct engagement with liaison groups as part of the police, and with the police directly in your case. I am interested more in the solutions for some of this. For a starting point, I am assuming that Peter has a low level of trust in some of the police liaison groups that you meet and how they are working with you, but please correct me if I am wrong. I am interested in, first, if that is the case; and, secondly, how you meet with them beforehand—talk to us a bit about that—what barriers there might be in overcoming that trust issue and what you propose as a solution.

Peter Leary: We have a mixed experience of different police officers that we deal with. Maybe this is the wrong way of viewing it, but our marches have been a major issue of national controversy over the last number of years, following various comments that have been made. The chief inspector of the Metropolitan Police has made numerous public comments about our marches, which we have rejected and objected to and so on. There are a number of people who refuse to meet with the organisers of the marches, so I think from the top down there is a willingness to engage with political conversation about marches but not a willingness to engage with those responsible for organising the marches.

That is often reflected in some of the operational policing that we also deal with, which often places conditions on engagement. For example, we had a situation in May where the police refused our route for our annual Nakba Day commemoration march. They proposed an alternative route, and we had some concerns about it. There was a major far right demonstration taking place in London on the day, in the context where some of those groups had previously tried to violently attack our march, most famously on a date in November 2023. We had concerns about the route. We wanted to meet with them, and the police took the position that they would only meet with us once we had agreed to the route they were proposing. They were refusing to engage in dialogue with us unless we met certain conditions.

We have had similar experiences whereby, right up to the week of the demonstration, the police have been threatening to or saying that they will impose conditions on our march, including the formation point, which is obviously the most important thing—we have to be able to advertise in advance where people need to arrive—but refusing to tell us what those conditions might be and instead saying, “Can you come up with an alternative?” and so on.

My experience is that the law has given the police a lot of powers to impose conditions, often at very short notice so they are not subject to and cannot be subject to any legal scrutiny, or we would have no opportunity to seek any legal redress, given the timescales involved. That creates a disincentive for the police to engage in any kind of good-faith discussion with protest organisers, because they know they can ultimately just impose conditions and there is very little we can do about it.

Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: If you had more communication, what would you suggest would be the most important?

Peter Leary: I think it comes back to the issue of greater pressure on the police to recognise their responsibility to facilitate demonstrations, which I do not think they quite appreciate now.

Chair: Since you raised the Nakba Day march, I was going to ask this later but I will ask it now. I remember very well the difficulties because of the Tommy Robinson march on the same day and the pressures that put on your organisation. You mentioned that your route was in part determined by the fact that Tommy Robinson had decided to have his event—or whatever you want to call it—on the same day. There was contention in the Jewish community about the route of the march because it was proximate to Westminster Synagogue. Was that route decision due to the Tommy Robinson event, and would you normally choose not to march close to a synagogue or place of worship?

Peter Leary: There is certainly absolutely no intention of ours to march close to or pass or target any place of worship in any way. That is not part of our intention. It is challenging. Sometimes what the Metropolitan Police determine as “near to” or “in the vicinity of” is quite expansive. I have mentioned the BBC, and we have been prevented from marching there owing to a synagogue several streets away.

The occasion I was referring to when the police refused to tell us what conditions they were going to impose related to Hyde Park, a long-standing historic site of protest and assembly. We did not know at the time, but it related to a synagogue I think about 12 minutes’ walk away. The challenge is that there are very few places in towns—and certainly not in central London—that are not within those kinds of distances of a place of worship of one kind or another.

These are quite large distances. They are far beyond the exclusion areas that—in my view, quite rightly—are imposed around abortion clinics, for example, to protect those using those services, far beyond the protections given to accommodation for asylum seekers, to give another example, where there are very threatening protests often happening right outside. It is difficult, the issue there. The route, as I say, was imposed on us, it was not particularly a route of our choosing. There are challenges in organising protests in that sense.

Chair: I do not want to labour this too much, but would it be helpful if an exclusion zone were closely codified in law so that it was clear what the police could ask and what you would have to adhere to in your planning?

Peter Leary: We would obviously reject any suggestion that our marches in any way represent a threat to people who wish to use their place of worship, including people who wish to visit synagogues. We would point to the very clear messaging in our marches of opposition to antisemitism alongside all other forms of racism—Islamophobia, anti-Palestinian racism—and the fact that large numbers of Jewish people participate in the marches. Many of them are marching as part of an organised Jewish bloc, as platform speakers in all of the marches, including, often, Holocaust survivors speaking at the demonstrations.

However, that lack of clarity and discretion that is given to the police, among march organisers and among the public, leads to a false impression often. For example, Mark Rowley has said that when we march, we try to march near a synagogue, but he means 12 minutes’ walk away, whereas when that gets interpreted, it means past the very door or on the doorstep. That undoubtedly will create a false perception and may indeed create fear in certain communities about what the intentions of the marches are. Yes, some clarity on that would enable people to understand what is and is not happening in relation to these marches, for one thing, but it would also help us as march organisers to facilitate that.

Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: The same question to you, Liz, on your engagement with the police liaison and your experiences of that and how you might suggest that issues could be made better.

Liz Webster: I had never arranged a protest until 2019. That was the first time, when we brought sheep to London and People’s Vote organised all of that. It was quite a big step to first do it, and it was daunting, I suppose. You are not quite sure what is expected of you in London. However, once I breached that barrier and made connections with certain people in the Met, it got a lot easier. The communication channels opened, I got to know them, they got to know me, and we were able to have a dialogue and say, “Can we do a protest next week?” They would say, “That’s a bad day, Liz, because so and so is coming over and we have this going on”. That was fine. It was inconvenient, and perhaps on a given day we would have wanted to do something, but I understood that their resources are stretched.

I took the view and I still take the view that I want the protest to be positive, not negative, so I recognise that we have to work with them. I suppose that if there is more communication on that—what I would say to somebody who is organising a protest in London is, “What do you want to achieve? Are you doing it because you just want to let off steam, or are you doing it because you are communicating and trying to further that issue, so that Parliament acts on the issue you are upset about?” That was the view that I took.

As a citizen, I am concerned about the creeping laws that are restricting us to do these things. I honestly can say that I never want to be arrested and have a criminal record, and I would not want to be responsible for anyone else doing that either. I am acutely aware of the fact that those laws have become more illiberal. I am also aware of what happened in Hong Kong and how that slippery route continues. Therefore, I did not want to be responsible for provoking that and shutting down protest even further, which is a fundamental human right.

Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: Thank you. Rahila, you have said that you do not directly liaise with the police on protest, but do you have any reflections, taking part in so many of them, and any particular suggestions about how trust issues could be overcome?

Rahila Gupta: The first thing is that I take your point completely about the dangers of getting a criminal record as a citizen. However, what if your right to protest and anything that you do on that protest takes away your ability to continue to live in this country? That affects the people we deal with, because for a lot of both the workers and the women we deal with, their right to remain in this country is affected by the Immigration Rules. That puts us in a completely different situation from the ordinary citizen who, rightly, fears the policing of protests and fears arrest.

Because the immigration rules are quite complex, I want to refer to my notes so that I do not get it wrong. One of the big issues is this proposal of the earned settlement route. These are still proposals and it is within your remit as lawmakers to reverse them, because there is a good-character test that has a direct impact on this issue about participation in protest. This earned settlement route, which Andy Burnham seems to be supporting, is through economic contribution, high earnings, and good character. That is one of the big issues in relation to this.

Previously, you had an automatic right to permanent residence after a period of five years. Now the length of time can increase from anywhere between 10 to 20 years. This intersection of the good-character test and these expanding protest laws carries significant human rights implications for non-citizens of the UK. Therefore, these policies alter how fundamental rights are exercised.

Any of the serious disruption offences that are in the law at the moment—locking on, blocking the highway—all carry prison sentences of up to 12 months. There is a persistent offender clause that can catch anyone who is engaged repeatedly in acts of civil disobedience. They can receive cautions, or there are minor convictions. If there are three provocations, or you accept a conditional caution to avoid court, it could affect your ability to remain in this country. That is a big problem for us.

The other issue concerns Section 22 of the Criminal Justice Act 2003. It gave the police and the CPS the power to issue conditional cautions to foreign nationals. They were known as undocumented migrants at that point. The new law in 2026 has expanded that to all foreign nationals who hold valid but limited visas in order to enter and remain in the UK. Some of the categories that affect us—they affect us because we have workers drawn from these categories—are skilled workers on employment visas, individuals on family visas, international students, and recognised refugees or victims of human trafficking. Many of our workers, and women, fall into one of these categories.

We are not talking about a small number of people. These numbers are sizable. The people who are subject to the no recourse to public funds measure number somewhere around 4 million. The figure will only grow, given earned settlement and the refugees. All of this has a huge impact on our ability to participate in protest.

Q20 Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: Thank you. I think we have a sense of how you might answer our next question, which doubles down on how you see the changes that have been made to the law and public order and protest in recent years. Overall, do you think these changes have made it more or less clear when protest becomes unlawful and when police interference is justified? It is a broad question. Peter, you have answered some of that, but is there anything that you would like to add about the expansion of the law?

Peter Leary: As I have probably indicated, it has become much more difficult for us. The police have a lot more powers to effectively prevent protests by imposing conditions on it that make it difficult if not impossible to organise a protest. We have had experiences where, for example, we wanted to protest at the Israeli embassy in response to the passing of a death penalty law that applies only and exclusively to Palestinians, whether in the occupied Palestinian territory or Palestinians with Israeli citizenship. Clearly, the target of such a protest is the Israeli embassy—the Government are responsible. The police, with an hour’s notice, imposed conditions on us to move us to Downing Street. We did go ahead with it, but effectively we are not protesting against the body that we wish to be. We are not able to because they have those powers to move us.

I mentioned the occasion that was, I felt, extremely ironic, when we were at a protest in response to the Crime and Policing Bill and the anti-protest clauses in that, and we were banned from using megaphones and we had to shout. On the day that famine was officially declared in Gaza, we were banned from banging pots and pans at a protest. We were banned from using megaphones or amplified noise on the day when the President of Israel visited Britain and we wished to protest in response to that. This silences protests and makes them much less effective, and it makes it much harder to organise them and to communicate to people the location of a protest.

Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: May I ask you a supplementary and ask all of you in advance what would you propose to change? What would be your biggest ask of a change in legislation?

Peter Leary: Where to begin? There are many, many things and it is very hard to prioritise them. The proscription of Palestine Action has created a hugely chilling climate for anyone wishing to engage in protest or express themselves at all in this country. It is absolutely shocking, the consequences we have seen—huge numbers of people, often elderly and disabled people, being arrested simply for holding signs. The implications of that do not stop simply with those protests. They send out a clear message about the attitude of the police and the Government in this country to freedom of speech and freedom of expression. Even though that is not a protest I am involved in myself or that we are involved in, that would be one thing.

Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: I realise that it is hugely complex.

Peter Leary: Charges are being pursued against people for taking part in protests of a variety of kinds, and there has been a wave of anti-protest laws. Perhaps I would go chronologically and start with the Crime and Policing Bill and go back to the Police, Crime and Courts Bill and work backwards that way, but there has been so much that needs to be looked at and addressed.

Baroness Lane-Fox of Soho: Thank you. I will ask for reflections on whether you think that any clarity has been provided by the recent legislative changes. Liz, the same question again: what would you suggest as changes to what is, I realise, a complex landscape?

Liz Webster: The kneejerk reaction to keep legislating in response to protest—which is happening because of economic changes, which are also linked to immigration and other changes, particularly over the last 10 to 15 years—is just the wrong thing. It also makes the police’s job probably a lot harder, because they have to tick boxes as well in all of this. I am scared of it becoming an illiberal police state. That is not a country that I want to live in, and it does feel that we are edging closer to that. It does mean that you are less likely to want even to get involved in protest, or certainly managing it, because the responsibility sits with you. That is telling you that that is fundamentally wrong, if you believe in human rights.

I do not think that more legislation is the answer, but probably a pulling back from that. That also involves properly funding our police, who are, I know, overstretched to manage these different demonstrations. You have austerity, which has made everything worse as well. Politically, you have asked me a very difficult question and, yes, there is a lot that needs to be looked at. We are not in a good situation in having this going on. Certainly with Palestine Action, that police resources are having to arrest people for holding a sign is ludicrous.

Rahila Gupta: I have to echo everything that has been said before me, and I think we should be looking at repealing a number of these laws. Certainly, the serious disruption prevention orders should be repealed. The whole thing about megaphones and excessive noise—that is the joy of protest. Q28 Lord Rook: The Crime and Policing Act has introduced new powers to make it a criminal offence to use an item to conceal identity at a protest. My first question is: do you think this was needed? My second is: how has the change in the law impacted your ability to protest?

Rahila Gupta: This has a particular impact on us, because many of the women we serve have been escaping domestic violence. They are afraid of violent spouses and disapproving families. I have an incident to talk about from 10 October, when the Palestine march took place. We had quite a large bloc organised for Southall Black Sisters, and two of our women were wearing Covid masks. They were wearing them because of fear of being photographed, filmed, seen and so on. As far as I know, the police are not allowed to legitimately ask for a medical mask to be removed, because they do not know whether somebody is actually suffering from something or not.

We also had a woman in our group wearing a niqab, the Muslim face veil. The police did not approach her, because the police are required to ignore religious face coverings. As a secular women’s organisation, we are not, in any way, ever going to tell our women to wear a niqab in order to hide from police scrutiny.

These women were asked to take their masks off, and they did. One of them put hers back on, and the police came in very aggressively and said they would arrest her if she continued to do so. It was her first march that she had ever attended. I have a quote from her that I would like to read. The first woman, who has no status in this country, said: “This is the first time, ever, I have been on a march, and when I was asked to take off my mask, I did, but I felt unsafe because of the cameras and videos. I went into the background and hid myself. I will have to think about whether I come again”. The second woman, who has recently got her right to remain, said: “I finally got a chance to be there without fear and feel that I am on the right side of history. I stood by my words and principles”.

Those were two very different reactions, but the chilling effect is felt throughout the group. It is really problematic for us. The thread that has been running through everything I have said is the two-tier policing. We noted that there were men who were hooded and masked, all in black, in Dover and in Portsmouth. They were not challenged, and they were simmering with aggression—a huge world of difference from two women wearing Covid masks. There is no nuance in how this policing is carried out. How do you police for that?

Lord Rook: Thank you. Liz?

Liz Webster: It is having an impact on us, because we would like to dress up as animals—chickens, pigs, cows—and now we cannot. Peter Leary: I echo what has been said. There are multiple elements to this. I should say—and I do not know if any of you have attended the marches—most people who go on the marches do not cover their faces or wear masks, unlike some other protests where people are largely concealing identity. The people who wear masks do so for complex reasons: some for medical reasons, some for religious reasons and some wish to conceal their identity because they do not wish others to know they are on a demonstration or where they can be found or identified.

Many young people, who are very exposed in their lives on social media, may wish to conceal their identity. There are also those theatrical elements. A grim reaper was arrested recently—ironically, I think, while protesting the death of democracy in response to one of the prosecution cases relating to the right to protest. That implies that by going on a protest, I am not going about my lawful business, as I would be if I went trick-or-treating, and certainly, I am not engaging exercising my right to freedom of expression and assembly, which are protected activities that should be more enhanced than, not less than, trick-or-treating.

Chair: Now, we will go on to a series of questions from Peter Swallow on intimidation and harassment, followed by the final question by Afzal Khan.

Q29 Peter Swallow: Thank you so much. This sounds like a very philosophical question at this stage of the session, but we are all familiar with the debate around freedom of speech and where it should end, what limit there should be on it in terms of what should be allowed, even if upsetting or offensive, and what should not be allowed because it is threatening or abusive. Rahila Gupta: This issue has been so politicised that we are confronting a number of absurdities. For example, two people were arrested for using the slogan “Globalise the Intifada”, which to me is a political slogan not aimed at any individual group. I think it may have been the same day, people were arrested at a Unite the Kingdom rally, at which Black police officers were called the N-word, and they were told to, “Effing go home”. It is perhaps easier to define in theory—anything that does not cause harm or incitement to hatred, and so on—but in practice, when it becomes so politicised, you can get to situations that are quite absurd.

Liz Webster: On a personal level, my son was attacked in 2007 by a gang, and it was a racial attack. My son is white, but it was the result of racial tension. Liz Webster: It is very complicated, but you should be coming at it cohesively and as a whole, to make society safer and more tolerant. Those communication issues are far more important than a tick box saying this one is allowed and that one is not.

Peter Swallow: My reflection would be that, unfortunately, the reality of the law is that sometimes it has to work with tick boxes, because that is the nature of legislation.

Liz Webster: That is the problem with over-legislating.

Peter Swallow: Peter?

Peter Leary: Yes, it is complicated, and it does call for nuance. Freedom of speech is precious, and people also have rights not to be threatened, intimidated or abused. In terms of our work, I think we have strayed very much into the policing of political statements, the most obvious being expressions of support for Palestine Action—which I do not think anyone could plausibly describe as inciting hatred or violence, yet large numbers of people have been arrested and criminalised under the most extreme legislation available for doing just that.

Peter Swallow: The point is that it is well documented that there are people who find it personally very offensive and threatening. Would it not be a good idea just not to say it on any of your marches?

Peter Leary: What I find extraordinary is that if I go out and call for an uprising, that is grand, but if an Arabic speaker says exactly the same thing in their language, they are potentially going to be arrested and criminalised for it. I think that sends out a very dangerous message. It is hard not to see that itself as an example of racist and offensive policing—that someone using the same word, but in their own language, should be treated as a criminal offence.

Peter Swallow: Within the context of a march about Palestinian rights.

Q30 Afzal Khan: We are finally at the last question. The question is to you, Peter. Peter Leary: There are two dangerous conflations there, and there is something to be said about the particular context of that moment, of that march that we are talking about. The two dangerous conflations are: on the one hand, conflating protests with unrelated acts of violence. This came in the wake of the attack in Golders Green that, as far as I know, was not linked to any protest in any way. There is no reason to believe that the individual involved had any links to any form of protest. That conflation builds on other conflations of protests with terrorism that we have seen, such as the proscription of Palestine Action, but it is particularly dangerous in this context, where we start conflating political dissent with terrorism.

The second conflation is between Israel and the actions of the state of Israel with Jewish people and Jewish communities in this country. We are absolutely and consistently clear that Jewish people are not responsible for the crimes of the State of Israel. That is our consistent and clear message. In fact, many Jewish people are involved in the very marches that this moratorium was proposed against. There are hundreds, if not thousands, of Jewish people marching as part of an organised Jewish bloc, Jewish speakers on the platform, Jewish people among the key organisers. On several occasions, the marches have been addressed by Holocaust survivors—perhaps before some of the largest audiences Holocaust survivors have ever addressed in this country—to send out a very clear message. We are ensuring that that is the message that we are sending out.

The idea that the marches could be incubators of antisemitism seems absurd. An antisemite would find our demonstrations a very uncomfortable place to be, because of the messages and the make-up of those marches and protests. Indeed, anyone who believed in any form of bigotry would feel that way, because these protests bring together people from all backgrounds and every walk of life. That particular statement was made in the run-up to our Nakba Day demonstration, which is an annual event commemorating the displacement of Palestinians from their homeland since 1948.

It is a very solemn occasion and particularly important to the Palestinian community. I do not know whether the independent reviewer of anti-terror legislation consulted with, or took into consideration the feelings or sentiments of, the Palestinian community, because it is also a kind of national day on which Palestinians mark their identity. This was a very important march. Every year, the march is led by Palestinian children along with, what are by now, very elderly Nakba survivors. It is very important that we continue to commemorate that in that way.

It also came on the day that Tommy Robinson had called on his far-right supporters to come out into the streets. He has called on his supporters to attack our marches before. I believe he likely called on his supporters to come out on the day of the Nakba march in order to try to drive the Palestine solidarity movement off the streets, because he hates to see Muslims and immigrants alongside Jewish people and LGBT people—and all the others who represent everything he stands against—taking part in a protest. Had there been such a moratorium imposed, it would have been a victory for Tommy Robinson and the far right. Our response was to continue with our protest, and I think that was the right thing to do.
