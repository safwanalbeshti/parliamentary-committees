Examination of witnesses

Richard Allen and Anna Doherty.

The Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Finance Bill Sub-Committee, where we are questioning experts on the issue of low-value imports and, as we looked at in previous sessions, the treatment of benefits in kind and the correction of errors by taxpayers. First, Richard, could you introduce yourself and tell us your experience of all this?

Richard Allen: I will quickly tell you my background. I worked in the music industry, managing a successful band, and then I became involved in online retail from 1994, which was before Amazon started selling CDs, through to 2007. I saw the growth of online retail right from the very beginning when it was unbelievably primitive. I was affected by tax avoidance. I do not know whether anybody remembers a company called Play.com, which used to sell CDs and DVDs. It was the first company I came across that took advantage of a loophole to avoid VAT, which it did via the Channel Islands. This affected the music industry to a huge degree because goods were shipped out to the Channel Islands and mailed back VAT-free using a thing called low-value consignment relief. I will not go into the detail of what happened but essentially I ended that. In 2011, low-value consignment relief was removed from mail-order goods from the Channel Islands.

Back then, you had low-value consignment relief on the import of goods into the EU—we were still a member—which meant that anything below a certain level did not attract VAT. The UK set it at 18% but later adjusted it to 15%. Back in the day, before computerisation, everything had to be done manually. I spent 10 years in import and export before I was in the music industry, which is how I ended up being an accidental expert on this subject. That relief became redundant with computerisation as not so much needed to be done to deal with packages that were coming in.

In the example of that first company, it went overnight from being unknown to become the second-biggest music retailer in the UK, threatening HMV. That is how much of an advantage it had because it did not have to charge VAT on its goods. After that, the EU started looking at low-value consignment relief with a view to scrapping it because the Chinese, mainly, were taking advantage and starting to send goods into Europe in huge quantities. In 2021 low-value consignment relief was scrapped in the UK and in the EU, and this is where paths diverge. I will not go into detail on other territories but America did something similar. The whole world started to look at scrapping de minimis because the internet was essentially using them to avoid paying taxes. Then in 2021 the UK scrapped low-value consignment relief and unusually—I did not understand why—the UK scrapped looking at items as they entered the UK at the border.

I do not know whether anyone ever ordered anything from another country prior to 2021, but the postman would turn up with a small card that said, “You owe customs and duty on this item”. You had to pay, and then the Post Office would release the item to you. That was scrapped and measures were brought in so that online marketplaces collected VAT. The EU did not scrap that; it kept the postal collection and introduced a system where online marketplaces collected VAT.

I ended up creating an organisation called RAVAS to represent businesses that were affected by this, and I have lobbied on this issue for a number of years now. I can go into more detail later on in the discussion but, in effect, I am the only trade body dedicated specifically to dealing with abuses of import VAT exemptions. I hope that helps; I tried to cram it all into a short introduction.

The Chair: That is very helpful to me, anyway; thank you. Anna, can you introduce your knowledge and expertise?

Anna Doherty: I am technical director at the Chartered Institute of Export and International Trade. We are the royal charter body for those involved in international trade and customs. Established in 1935, we are a membership body for both professionals and businesses, providing training and education as well as a space for networking and peer-to-peer support. We have around 4,000 members in total across our student, professional and business members, which range in size from your tiny one-person/one-man band to the most known names on the high street. We also represent our members at various government bodies. Most importantly for this committee, we ran the Ecommerce Trade Commission a couple of years ago with the support of large e-commerce providers. We also represent our members at HMRC’s joint customs consultative committee, where I am the industry chair. That provides me with great insight into the impact of customs reforms on a variety of border stakeholders, all the way from those operating the border, software providers, CSPs, customs brokers, various agents, down to the importers and exporters that ultimately receive the goods.

The Chair: Broadly, what are the Government doing about low-value imports? What is happening in 2028 that should change things?

Richard Allen: The Government are moving in the right direction. It is worth referring back to what Sir Jon Thompson said when he spoke to the Treasury Committee prior to Brexit. He told it that HMRC would need a very large investment in order to deal with the customs pressures that were coming as a result of the increased border issues after leaving the EU. One of the things that he referred to was low-value parcels coming into the country. He suggested that that matter would have to go on a back burner unless large amounts of money were put in at that point.

It is fair to say that this issue has been on a back burner for a while but it has now got to the point where it is critical. I am sure everybody has seen the high streets and how things are affected. The store that used to sell clothes pegs, spoons, small household goods, the ironmonger kind of store, has been annihilated by Chinese sellers trading online at ridiculously low prices. The Government have moved and in 2021 made online marketplaces collect VAT from overseas sellers. I was involved in the consultation in 2020 and pointed out that collecting only from overseas sellers would result in them setting up UK companies to get around the tax, which is exactly what happened.

You may have heard the story of 11,000 businesses registered at a flat in Wales. Traders were just getting paper companies and then pretending they were UK based, which meant that the platform did not collect the VAT. In the current Budget, there is talk of expanding to collect the VAT from all sellers but the Government have left a loophole in that they exempted second-hand goods and anybody with a turnover below £90,000 on each platform. Anyone selling less than £90,000 on eBay or Amazon will not have their VAT collected. We already see lots of sellers deliberately keeping their turnover below £90,000. I saw an example the other day of someone who had 11 companies, all slightly different, all selling the same products, all from the same address. They had basically just disaggregated their business, and that was on the VAT threshold. The rule change is welcome. In theory, online retailers will collect VAT from all sellers apart from those earning below £90,000 on their platform and those selling second-hand goods, but this is also abused. New products are being sold as second-hand, particularly in the music industry, where deleted CDs are sold as second-hand when they are brand new.

As I mentioned earlier, the EU has a system where VAT is either collected up front by the platform or by the post office. Each package that comes into the EU is identified with a number showing that the VAT has been prepaid. If there is no such indication, the carrier will collect the VAT from the recipient and charge a handling fee on top. That makes it difficult for the seller to be competitive, so it forces them to use the prepaid system. We do not have that; we scrapped it completely. If you do not sell through an online marketplace and just post it directly, it sails through; there is no collection of VAT; it goes straight to the consumer and no VAT is paid.

The system does not force the seller to then comply. It is not voluntary—the overseas seller is obligated to register for VAT in the UK—but in reality you cannot force sellers in China to register for VAT in the UK; they will just ignore you. While the duty is due legally, it is not collected. That issue is not being addressed at the moment, which for me is the biggest problem. I hope that is helpful.

Anna Doherty: I want to look at it from a slightly different perspective. I appreciate Richard’s expertise in VAT. We pay close attention to the proposed complete removal of the low-value imports relief, where currently any goods coming into the UK with an intrinsic value of under £135 are not subject to payment of any duty. That means a lot of both business-to-consumer and business-to-business goods are imported using a simplified dataset, where very little information needs to be provided at the border.

A low-value imports relief—de minimis or whatever you wish to call it—is a genuine trade facilitation because when this was implemented through a trade facilitation agreement with the WTO, the volume of parcels was quite small; people were not ordering online to the degree they do now. Things have changed over the last few years. Obviously, departure from the European Union meant that there are more import requirements on UK-to-EU movements, and vice versa. Looking back at Covid, everybody staying at home and sitting on our phones instead of going to the shops meant that consumer behaviour has changed drastically. The US and the EU are looking backwards, trying to react to changes in behaviours quite few years after they happened.

The Government state that the relief will be removed by October 2028; that may seem like a really long time to go but it is not actually that long when you consider what needs to happen. We are at the draft stage of primary legislation at the moment and the detail of how that regime will operate is still not yet fully defined. Therefore, this is the big area where further engagement from the Government will be needed because it will affect not only business-to-business but business-to-consumer movements, and these businesses are not yet paying attention to the significant changes coming their way.

The EU has already started to tackle this problem. It called it a “Tsunami of parcels” because the volumes coming through—plane loads of individual parcels all under €150—meant that you could not realistically check them not only for duty and VAT due but the safety of products placed on the market. That is why these reforms are now being introduced—not only for collection of duty and VAT collection that is due and payable, but to ensure that imported products are indeed safe for consumers.

Lord Leigh of Hurley: Can I take this opportunity to thank you, Mr Allen, for what you have done, public-spiritedly, independently and personally financed by you, in raising these issues? I have raised a number of them in the Chamber of the House of Lords a number of times, and Treasury Ministers recognise that, but I have been able to do only that because of the assistance you provided me in terms of your research, background and knowledge. Your contribution has not been properly recognised but I want to pay tribute to you this morning before we start.

Richard Allen: Thank you, and thank you for listening.

Lord Leigh of Hurley: On the specifics that we just heard, why will it take the Government until October 2028? The argument has been made that there are lots of mechanics to be put in place before then, but the EU seems to have its act together well before October 2028. Why are we taking so long?

Richard Allen: It is interesting. A good example of how far behind we are is worth pointing out. I posted something to America the other day. I went on to Royal Mail’s website and filled out the boxes to get the postage. At the end, it asked me for a fee for customs of 50p, which I paid. Royal Mail is collecting customs for America and for Europe. It does not make sense to me that we do not do the same the other way round. It seems crazy that Royal Mail is acting, in effect, as a customs broker for other countries but not for us. The problem is that we were already on the back foot in terms of investment before Brexit occurred, and as Sir Jon Thompson said, there needed to be a lot of money put in to bring in systems that worked. I do not know what the thinking was but I suspect the funding was not available in 2021 to bring in a system to immediately to collect VAT and duty.

I did not mention that the same problem applies to duty. The proposal is to collect duty and VAT only through online marketplaces but not everything is sold in this way. Shopify is a system used by lots of overseas sellers, where goods are sold off their own websites. That is not covered, and there is no mechanism in the UK for collecting that tax as goods come in, in the way I described where the postman collects it from you. The EU had that in place and it never got rid of it, so we are missing that part of the custom structure, which needs to be replaced. It will not be an easy fix because we are starting scratch, in effect.

It is also worth mentioning that there is a degree of friendly fire with regard to the EU and the UK when it comes to dealing with Chinese goods because the EU brought in all these measures to limit imports into the EU by mail order, which we should do as well, but that affects us. When UK mail order businesses sell into Europe, they are treated in the same way as a Chinese seller. I represent all sorts of people who are affected by this. They come to me and say they cannot sell to Europe because they are hit with a handling fee and a customs charge. There needs to be a corridor with the EU, where we both fend off all these cheap imports but also do not harm each other’s imports. Talks are coming up shortly. That needs to be looked at, as we are affected by that.

To give you some idea of the scale, at the moment we are losing £42 million a month in duty alone, based on the Government’s own figures. It is worth noting that the figure of £500 million in savings—I cannot remember the figure per year—is in terms of duty only; the VAT I talked about is not factored into those figures. We collect VAT only on online marketplace sales, not on any other method, unless sellers voluntarily register for VAT, which in my view is simply never going to happen.

There are a number of reasons why we are behind. We could introduce measures in stages—do something now. Retailers were calling for a charge on each item but the problem is: who collects it? That mechanism has been removed; the Post Office no longer collects it, apart from on gifts and items with a value above £135.

Lord Leigh of Hurley: You came up with the idea of a QR code on every package. Could you explain that to us?

Richard Allen: Yes, I came up with an idea that I called a VAT passport, or a tax passport, where the seller can sign up to a portal and pay the VAT and duty up front, which is essentially what happens in the EU but we could do it better than it does. When you sign up to the EU system, you are given an identifier as a business, which goes on the package. I have tested this and have sent items into Europe that were not from the company with the identifier, just to see whether they would get through, and they did. We could identify each transaction: if somebody prepaid VAT and duty on a particular package, that item would be given a number such as a QR code, that would be put on the package. It could be printed out by the sender and when it came through customs, they could scan it and see the contents, the value and the amount of tax paid. Anything with the QR code on it would essentially go through a green lane; anything that had nothing on would go through a red lane.

To give you an idea of the impact of this, when the EU introduced its charges recently, in Finland imports from China fell by 70%. Just to put that into perspective, that brings things back to 2023 levels. Although some think it is a disaster that people cannot get their goods, that is down only to 2023 levels of imports. The Chinese have started putting warehouses into the EU that employ people and raise taxes within the EU. Essentially, it goes back to the model before all this happened, where wholesalers bought goods and then sold them within the territory. We are trying to push away the direct sales and make people sell within the UK; that way we get some benefit from it because a sale on Temu gives zero benefit to the UK.

Anna Doherty: To cover Richard’s points, in terms of sellers who are not UK-established but do not sell through a marketplace, the legislation very clearly covers that they will require a fiscal representative to be able to trade into the UK. Measures are being put in place to make sure that any duty and VAT due will indeed be paid and collected. The reason why no duty is currently charged on goods coming in under £135 is because the relief still exists, so there would be no duty payable. If the value is over £135, Royal Mail, or any other fast parcel operator or customs intermediary, will collect that duty because that declaration goes straight into the customs declaration service—CDS—and therefore those goods are presented to customs and duty is payable where it is due, or there may be an opportunity for preferential treatment if purchasing from countries where we have a free trade agreement. Full customs measures apply above £135.

The reason we are behind what the EU is doing is twofold. When the UK left the EU, we were replacing our old legacy system—CHIEF—with new customs systems that needed to be representative of the Union customs code that was to go live in 2016. The UK was one of the couple of EU members that were quite ahead in terms of implementing that system but the system was built at a time when we did not foresee the significant volume of declarations that we have to deal with right now.

Particular mechanisms were put in place to allow smoother movement of goods across the borders, including BIRDS—bulk import reduced data set—that authorised fast parcel operators can use to declare goods, meaning that the number of declarations is reduced. If we were to simply remove the low-value relief and just say everything, regardless of value, comes in with a duty amount, the CDS system would probably not cope with the volume of declarations that needed to be placed into it; and therefore the impact would not be just on low-value B2B or B2C shipments but on the entirety of freight movements and the entire border infrastructure. This is why the Government need to set up a separate system that works alongside CDS and the border infrastructure to make sure that this operates.

It takes time to make sure that we build something suitable for the volume of declarations that will need to go through it and to ensure that the declarations are indeed compliant. It is one thing to stand up a system, let it run riot, then try to fix it as we go along. That is happening on the EU customs reform side, where it had the great idea of the data hub, but the €3 is a temporary measure. From the outset it said, “This is going to be temporary until we have a separate system for the declarations for the low-value imports”.

The UK Government could have done the same. They could have just put in a flat charge and that might have given immediate results. Right now, the challenge is that in the EU we see a significant drop in the number of direct parcels and an increase in a containerised movement. From a customs officer point of view, it is great because you want a container filled with the same product so that it can opened and checked rather than having to go through thousands and thousands of parcels. The potential risk with the UK’s timeline is the circumvention of the parcels that might have been sent from China to the US, where the US closed its de minimis, and are then redirected to the EU. We are potentially going to see a significant increase in the UK because that advertising budget needs to go somewhere; those goods need to be sold somewhere, and right now the UK route is very much easier than that of the EU.

Baroness Wheatcroft: Are you being given any indication that this is featuring in the talks with the EU over a new relationship?

Richard Allen: I am not sure but it should be. I know many businesses that sell mail order that cannot now sell into Europe because they are getting these charges on their shipments.

The EU has a system called the Import One Stop Shop—IOSS—where you prepay the VAT and duty. It started on that in 2015. The measures to add fixed charges on to things are more recent but it brought the system in then. For all its faults, it was effective; I know this because every business I know selling into the EU ended up having to pay these charges. My main worry is that some people involved in the consultation on this are the fast parcel carriers that use BIRDS. The checks are absolutely minimal. It is abused: goods are brought in, undervalued, in containers marked as individual packages. In these discussions, we should not have policy shaped by businesses that benefit from large volumes of parcels going through the system because it is not in their interest to introduce measures that will reduce the number of parcels coming into the UK. In the shaping of this policy, it is important to have the UK businesses and retailers that are affected by this rather than companies that carry these packages. From what I have seen, most of the discussions tend to involve couriers and Royal Mail, which I feel is a bit one-sided when it comes to designing policy.

Baroness Wheatcroft: That is really helpful. I was going to ask you both whether government consultation was taking place with the right people and was effective in any way. Anna, what is your experience?

Anna Doherty: With any government consultation, you will have very strong voices where people are heavily impacted. There is no shying away from the fact that fast parcel operators are heavily impacted by this. Retailers have a gigantic stake in this as well. It is all about levelling the playing field. We have heard from members on both sides of the fence: some say, as Richard just did, the EU measures have disproportionately impacted small and medium businesses, where suddenly the entire market is closed off to individual sellers of low-value goods. It is not just because of the €5 handling fee that is now operational from 1 November; it is about all the additional non-tariff barriers that relate to the customs declaration. It is about product packaging regulations and labelling requirements. All this means that small sellers are less and less inclined to deal with the EU; we are going to see a similar shift here.

The consultation was launched as part of a tax update. There is more the Government could do to make sure that those who will be most heavily impacted are aware of this consultation. A lot of our members are interested in international trade and are paying attention, yet there are so many businesses operating specifically in the SME space that are so busy running their businesses on a day-to-day basis that they do not have time to think about changes. No matter how hard we try to encourage engagement and be very vocal about the impacts on everybody, people only ever react when it hits them, on the day when their first consignment is hit.

There should be more engagement right now when the secondary legislation is not yet drafted. Businesses will need a significant amount of time to prepare for this in terms of people supporting the operation of the border who need to adjust their systems, but also those who are on the receiving end of the measures. As a UK importer of goods, suddenly changes may be coming that affect the purchase of spares, for instance. We keep talking about e-commerce and direct to consumer or retail, but it affects business-to-business movements as well. Lots of businesses use the de minimis relief to get samples, spares or bits and bobs transferred between themselves and their suppliers. There are official or appropriate customs channels to deal with these but the majority of the time the compliance element with that is so disproportionate in terms of the benefit that it is much easier to use the low-value relief and get those goods very quickly.

In my experience, any sort of facilitation will always be open to abuse because it is designed for a particular reason. There is usually a genuine case to bring it forward but, the moment that there is a loophole, there will be those who look to exploit that loophole.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: Am I right in thinking that we will have two separate systems, one for VAT and one for import duty?

Anna Doherty: No. The indication, and how I am reading it, from the Government’s response is the main system will be for customs declarations on goods that are not subject to the LVI, and it can collect import VAT as well; that is the CDS that we currently have. The Government are working on a separate system for the foreign sellers and marketplaces that are subject to the LVI regime and will use this system to provide data for payment and collection of customs duty and VAT.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: Both systems are going to depend on fiscal representatives in the UK, is that right?

Anna Doherty: Fiscal representation will be applicable only to the low-value imports regime. That concept is not currently used for freight movements. Within freight, you have two types of representation: a direct and an indirect customs representative. The indirect customs representative is jointly and severally liable with sellers for the customs debt; on direct representation, the agent acts in the name of the principal only, unless they are acting against instruction. The fiscal representative is a brand new concept that it is being brought in specifically to deal with the foreign seller; it is probably also a testing ground in the LVI space to see whether it could operate in the UK and then maybe spread out to other areas outside the LVI regime.

It is being brought in to make sure specifically that foreign sellers, non-UK established entities, have a physical presence in the UK: that there is a door that HMRC can knock on to chase that debt. I do not think many customs intermediaries would be willing to step into that place. Some might, depending on the risk appetite, because even indirect customs representation is something that only very few customs intermediaries provide. Ultimately, why would they share the liability? Large sellers will probably find a fiscal representative quite easily because, again, they will just pay the cost of it. There might be some agents springing up who are willing to take that risk. The challenge is going to come with non-established sellers, small businesses in the EU and US or anywhere else in the world, that may suddenly struggle to sell directly to consumers in the UK because they cannot get a fiscal representative.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: Who will these fiscal representatives be? What sort of company would do that?

Anna Doherty: Customs intermediaries may step into that space, maybe one of your Big Four accountants. Some people may make this their sole business model.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: It sounds like a high-volume/low-value business, which is not the business model of the people you are talking about.

Anna Doherty: No, it is not.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: Presumably, new people will come in to do it, but I do not see where those new people are going to come from.

Anna Doherty: I do not know either.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: That tells us something.

Richard Allen: I do not believe that is workable because, as you pointed out, normally a fiscal representative would act on behalf of a large company because it is quite an expensive thing to do. I am also not convinced that it is even legal because if you are offering only a route into the UK, a fiscal representative will be, arguably, some kind of customs broker and that breaches WTO rules. They could immediately point to the EU and say, “Well, if I am selling to the EU, I have the option of prepaying the VAT and duty”. Say you had a bicycle shop in Idaho that had three customers in the UK. Is it expected to get a fiscal representative? That is not going to happen.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: It will cost a fortune.

Richard Allen: Yes. It will just not sell to the UK; there is a trade barrier, in effect. There is no way around the fact that the UK needs to develop a system along the same lines as the EU’s, but better. In effect, you have two systems: bulk freight clearance, and low-value packages for postal, carrier, courier, direct-to-consumer sales in the same way that the EU does it. You have to give people the option to pay the VAT and duty up front.

I do not whether if anyone saw that Amazon came out suddenly and said, “We are for this”. I was surprised. It has got to the point where companies such as Amazon and major high street retailers—Primark was up in arms about this—saying that the volumes coming in are seriously damaging their businesses, and they want to see this issue fixed. My concern is that if we do not address the prepayment of VAT and duty for a non-online marketplace seller, we leave a massive loophole, which already exists, that they are going to exploit. The EU’s system is not perfect but, without a shadow of doubt, it is better than what we have at the moment.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: Anna, is that your view as well?

Anna Doherty: The collection element versus the responsibility can be two separate things. A representative may be needed for dealing with customs entry, and you may have a separate fiscal representative that takes on liability and responsibility if something goes wrong. Those can be two separate things. Some customs intermediaries will take on fiscal representation; they could see it as an additional revenue stream. The prepayment of duty and VAT is also part of the Government’s response to the consultation and will be an option on the table. It depends on how businesses operate. It should be feasible for large marketplaces and quite easy to calculate it. However, the calculation of customs duty and import VAT is dependent on having data about the goods; it is driven by commodity code classification and country of origin. Therefore, everything will be reliant on the availability of data being submitted, be that through the marketplace or directly to the customs intermediary. Currently, if you import low-value goods, you may not be asked for that information and the sender may not be either.

Baroness Bi: We touched on the consultation. What are your views are on the Government’s approach to consultation and ongoing engagement? In particular, how much public awareness is there of this proposed change? The public are used to low prices and easy availability. In the light of the cost of living crisis, how do you think the public will respond to the idea of having to pay more VAT and customs duties on their small parcels?

Richard Allen: I do not know whether anyone remembers the pasty tax. That was a very straightforward fairness issue where chip shops had to charge VAT and Greggs did not. The way it was communicated to the public was a bit of a disaster. The way to communicate this is to say, all businesses that sell into the UK that do not pay VAT and duty have a benefit that no other business in the UK has: they do not have to pay VAT and duty when they sell goods directly to a UK customer. We are removing a benefit, not adding a cost; that is the way to look at it.

Baroness Bi: How much awareness is there at the moment of this potential change? How much of a reaction has there been to it?

Richard Allen: I feel slightly frustrated with these consultations in that we are talking about compliance. Some aspects of compliance are not really open to negotiation, are they? You would not consult on criminal law with the public. You would just say, “This is what we are going to do. Do these things have an impact on you? Is there any way that we can look at that?” With this issue of VAT and duty, I sometimes feel there is an element of leaving loopholes in to satisfy complaints that really are not justified. If you sell into Europe and the UK and do not charge VAT, you really do not have an argument that you should be allowed to continue doing that if every business in the UK has to charge VAT. Sometimes, I feel that the scope of these consultations takes in views that should be ignored from the outset.

Baroness Wheatcroft: How much money are the Government losing every year because of this?

Richard Allen: The figure of £500 million is duty only; it is silent on the VAT. It is very important to understand that, at the moment, the focus is on online marketplaces collecting VAT and duty, which is fantastic. However, a huge amount of stuff is sold not on online marketplaces. If I was to look at this consultation and ask, “What does it say?”, I am more interested in what it does not say. What it does not say is what will happen with the non-online marketplace trade. There is no mention of it in any of the paperwork that I have seen, which is why I personally believe that it is never going to work unless you have a portal for prepayment of VAT and duty and a customs check to ensure that packages have the VAT and duty prepaid. The online marketplace stuff is the gravy. They will collect it; you will get a cheque every month or whatever—that is easy. That is how I look at that.

The Chair: To be clear, are you saying the loss of revenue is, at the minimum, £500 million but could be a lot more?

Richard Allen: To give you a comparison with previous estimates, to date, every single estimate on this subject has been wrong. For example, when HMRC introduced the first measure in 2021, it estimated £350 million; the actual figure in the first year was £1.4 billion. That gives you some idea of how out of skew the figures were.

Lord Newby: What proportion of imported goods are subject to customs duty, given that we have all these free trade arrangements with all sorts of people around the world?

Richard Allen: Some 50% of commodity codes do not attract any duty at all. Rates of duty on some things are very low. I am not suggesting we go full-on Donald Trump, but there are areas of duty that could raise revenue. A tiny amount on something is better than nothing.

Anna Doherty: I will add just a few points on the previous question about consultation and engagement, then follow up on the duty element. When the consultation was first launched, HMRC and HM Treasury ran a webinar to try to gather engagement, but that was a one-off and was probably not sufficient in making businesses aware of this consultation. Trade bodies such as ours and others were very vocal about it and encouraged members to respond directly. As much as we have a voice—we are their voice and we have submitted a response—it is more impactful when government hears from a variety of stakeholders; 165 responses is not a lot to shape policy.

Right now, we are in a bit of a limbo. In June 2026, so a few months ago, we had a government response and then they went quiet; they went internal and said, “We are now considering how we are going to do it”. It is not right to work in silos internally in government, when fora exist for meaningful consultation. The last thing you want to do is come to the table with a solution to then be told, “Actually, that is not workable; that is not feasible; that is not going to work”.

We keep talking about business-to-business and retailers. The people who will be most impacted by this are consumers who currently purchase goods on the internet. Sometimes they are not even aware that they are purchasing imported goods. They go on a .co.uk website that is made to look like they are buying domestic goods. Suddenly, they receive a tracking number that shows that those goods are coming from China. They never even knew that they were imported. Consumers will need to be well aware of these coming changes. They will be most impacted both in terms of the potential increase in costs but also procedures that they have to follow, unless the UK introduces a similar concept to the EU has with its deemed importer, which makes the marketplace the importer rather than pushing that down on to the consumer. That was not part of the consultation but may be something to consider.

In terms of the duties, the UK global tariff average duty rate is currently at 2.4%, but that is spread across thousands of commodities, and some will be 0% regardless of where they come from. These are generally goods that we want to be importing. These are things that we cannot do ourselves. For some, the standard duty rate can be quite high. If you think about pieces of clothing, if you are not buying from countries where we have a free trade agreement, either bilateral or unilateral, the duty rate on T-shirts is 12%, for instance. There is quite a significant difference in terms of goods that are coming in without the payment of duty.

The LVI regime is very specific in terms of the goods that are included because the Government have gone down the route of particular exclusions, such as how the duty rate is calculated, whether a trade measure is attached to it or a licence is required. All these things will not be done through the simplified system; they are going to go down the route of full customs declarations. For non-established UK sellers, when they are not a marketplace and do not fall under the LVI regime, they will probably go down the route of standard customs processes. That means exporting to the UK either on a DAP basis, where the importer is responsible for all the clearance, or if they are willing to send on a DDP basis, the customs intermediary pre-charges for the duty and VAT. There will be a mechanism for that. It is just, where the relief is removed, more people will be pushed down the route of standard customs processes.

Baroness Bi: Why are the Government not following the EU example?

Anna Doherty: The EU is 27 different member states that have different challenges to ours. The Government are definitely watching what the EU is doing. One of the consultation questions asked whether we should introduce the bucket system for calculation of duty. That was the initial proposal in the EU customs reform. It went away from it; we still consulted on it. The majority of trade said, “It’s not feasible; it’s not going to work. You’re just going to overcomplicate it”. We see some things that the EU is doing.

Richard called it out: it is almost like friendly fire in terms of who can do particular legislation better. We see it across other areas, such as the carbon border adjustment mechanism. The EU likes to overcomplicate things and then try to walk them back. The UK is watching what the EU does, hearing the concerns and seeing whether we can do things slightly better. There is always a risk that, by trying to oversimplify, you lose the gist of the message and what you are trying to achieve. It is a fine balance in finding the right solutions for the environment in which you operate.

Lord Carrington of Fulham: If you are a low-value purchaser of goods, the chances are you want to return it when it does not fit or is not fit for purpose. How does that work under this new scheme?

Anna Doherty: It will probably go down the customs route of returns. What happens right now with large-scale online sellers is you could buy your products from them and receive them in small, multiple parcels depending on where they come from. When you try to return that product, often they will issue a refund but let you keep the product because it is not viable for them to receive and sort because they often do not actually want those low-quality goods. Going back to how many parcels are checked, it is very few but one of the things the reform is looking to do is to introduce a handling fee, partly to pay for the infrastructure, but it could also pay for checks to make sure that goods are properly declared, that there is a visibility of them coming in and that duty and VAT are collected. Lord Carrington of Fulham: Could I come back to the returning of goods?
