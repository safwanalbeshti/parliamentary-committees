Chair: Welcome to the Northern Ireland Affairs Committee. Today we are considering the work of the Secretary of State for Northern Ireland. I welcome Sir Chris Bryant MP to his place, and Sarah Owen MP. We are also joined by Baroness Anderson, by Julie Harrison, permanent secretary at the Northern Ireland Office, and by Fleur Johnson.

Before we start, the Committee notes the absence of the right hon. Gavin Robinson, who is attending the funeral of Lord Morrow’s daughter today. The Committee sends its deepest condolences to Lord Morrow on the devastating loss of his wife and daughter.

Secretary of State, what is the latest update on the budget discussions with parties in the Executive?

Chris Bryant: Stasis. We have asked for information from them. My basic point is that in every meeting I have, people keep telling me they want more money, they want more money, they want more money, and I cannot say it in a clearer way than that there is no more money. We have made a full and final offer. That is all there is.

I am very conscious that I have two roles as Secretary of State for Northern Ireland: I am the Cabinet person in Northern Ireland but I am also the Northern Ireland person in the Cabinet, and I try to get the best possible deal I can. I think you would agree that, as a team, we are pretty much a set of three live wires. That is the way all three of us operate as politicians. It breaks our hearts when there are things that we would desperately love to get engaged in, such as the issues around violence against women and girls, community tensions issues, or—something I have been devoted to for a long time—tackling brain injury and problems relating to that, but I cannot get past this first stage of getting a budget sorted. In the end, the only people who have the legal responsibility for setting a budget are the Northern Ireland Executive. This is one of the symbols of the complete abnormality that now seems to pervade Northern Ireland politics, and there is a bit of me that just wants to say, “Everybody’s got to get real.”

Chair: How much more do they want?

Chris Bryant: Oh, it changes every week. One version is another £1 billion. I think the latest version from John O’Dowd on Monday was £1.5 billion this year.

The most important thing is that we need to make sure that Northern Ireland gets fair funding. For a long time the technical version of that has been that Northern Ireland should get funding per head of 24% above England’s. We are meeting the 124% of England’s funding. In fact, if the Northern Ireland Executive accept our offer of £500 million a year for this year, next year and the year after—plus the consequentials that come from Barnett—they will be at 126%.

I hear sometimes the argument that this is all very unfair because Wales gets more and Scotland gets more. That is not actually an accurate state of affairs. If you look at all the financial packages that have been given to Northern Ireland over roughly the last decade and you transpose that to Wales, Wales would get an extra £26 billion.

The point is that Northern Ireland is getting fair funding. Whatever the envelope is—if you were a councillor, you would have to set a legal budget. You just have to make the sums adds up. What really frustrates all of us, I think, is that I have tried processes of adjudicating between the different parties—because, let’s face it, the parties are not talking to each other at the moment and will not sit in the same room as each other. We have a situation where neither the First Minister nor the Deputy First Minister was able even to attend Remembrance Sunday or, for that matter, the National Economic Council with the Prime Minister. You would have thought that that was a key moment when everybody would want to say, “Let’s stand up for Northern Ireland in all the conversations about devolution and making the economy work for the whole of the United Kingdom.”

Chair: What are they saying they want the money for?

Chris Bryant: One of the things that they say they want money for is Northern Ireland pay awards for the whole of the civil service, and in particular for “Agenda for Change”, which of course includes the whole of the NHS. They have not made their pay award for 2026-27. But I make the point to them that they knew they were going to have to make a pay award in this financial year, so that is not unpredictable expenditure—it is very predictable expenditure. What they needed to do was take some possibly unpalatable and difficult decisions around revenue raising.

I have done a little bit of statistics—I do not know whether this helps, but maybe it does. I looked at different constituencies: Belfast East, Cumbernauld, Wythenshawe, Gower and West Dorset.

Simon Hoare: Hopefully North Dorset.

Chris Bryant: I have actually done West Dorset, sorry.

Simon Hoare: It was all going so well, Secretary of State.

Chris Bryant: Actually, maybe I am misreading—we will check the record afterwards. My point is that if you add up the domestic rates or the regional rates—they work in different ways—and the water rates in those different constituencies, for a median property, in Belfast East it is £1,158 a year; in Cumbernauld it is £1,716; in Wythenshawe it is £2,201; in Gower it is £2,921; and in whichever Dorset constituency it is, it is £3,153.

There are decisions that could be made in Northern Ireland. They have to be made by the Northern Ireland Executive. They are the only people who can make these decisions. I am not saying that they should necessarily align all those figures, but if people complain, for instance, “We can’t build enough homes in Northern Ireland for people who need them because there is not proper water infrastructure,” someone is going to have to go, “How are we going to make sure we’ve got proper water infrastructure?”

Chair: Very good point.

Chris Bryant: Sorry, that was quite a long rant, but I think all of us feel—and the Department feels—deeply frustrated about this. We have tried to hold meetings in Hillsborough. If I can get one other thing off my chest while I am here—

Chair: It’s not therapy, Secretary of State.

Chris Bryant: If only. I think it is a very odd system where you have a series of meetings with different political parties, and they all go out and do a press conference beforehand and basically say, “I’m going to tell him what’s what,” and then afterwards they have another press conference in which they say, “I told him what’s what.” That is not a way to negotiate. It turns all the politicians in Northern Ireland into commentators on politics, rather than players who are actually making decisions, and that is what we need.

Robin Swann: I want to pick up that point, Secretary of State, as a Northern Ireland politician. I have listened to your points. I am the Ulster Unionist rep on the Committee, and I think I can speak for all the Northern Ireland politicians here when I say that we understand your frustration about this.

I think the three parties represented here—I am sure my two colleagues will speak for themselves—see the frustration that is coming from the impasse that has been created by the two larger parties, where we are not getting papers coming through the Executive. There was a budget put forward in January this year, but that very quickly fell apart. I think the angst that you are expressing here today about how this moves forward is also shared by the political parties of Northern Ireland and quite the majority of Northern Ireland society.

Your portrayal of those press conferences is unfortunately a manifest of Northern Ireland politics and the engagement with political media. Many people have said that if that level of media and social media interest was around in 1998, we would never have had the Belfast agreement, but that is the situation we live in.

I hear your point about “Agenda for Change” and pay parity. I was the Minister who negotiated two pay deals that solved industrial action in our health service at that point, and we are getting to that point again. We have doctors in Northern Ireland going on strike on Friday and on Monday. Our health service workers’ unions are now balloting again for industrial action, because they have not received their pay parity agenda—well, that pay award. That is because there is no agreed budget, and my party colleague Robbie Butler does not have the legislative responsibility or ability to give that without Executive sign-off.

You told the BBC on 5 November that “it is not my responsibility” to set a budget for Northern Ireland, and I think you have expressed that here today. Previous Secretaries of State have been pushed to a position where they have had to set a budget for Northern Ireland because, as you have expressed, the Executive has been unable to do that. At what point in the year will you bring forward a budget yourself if the Executive does not? The people of Northern Ireland need a budget. If the current impasse between the political parties cannot be resolved, will you bring forward a budget?

Chris Bryant: I have to be responsible, and we have to be responsible as a Government. We are talking about UK citizens in Northern Ireland. We need to make sure that they get the public services they want.

One of the other frustrations for me is that we spend more in Northern Ireland per head of population than anywhere else in the United Kingdom, yet on health waiting lists, 53.7% are waiting for more than a year, whereas it is only 2% in England. In the justice system, 81% of major cases are waiting for more than a year, compared with 27% in England and Wales. That is despite work that has been done in the Justice Department over the last couple of years, which has been quite effective. On education, 12.3% of adult of working age in Northern Ireland have no qualifications. That is double the average across the whole of the United Kingdom. Just spending money is not an achievement—getting outcomes for it is.

I think the crisis that you portrayed in terms of strikes will potentially get considerably worse. This will seem very technical, but the Northern Ireland Executive was originally able to spend up to 45% of last year’s budget on account. It is now able to spend up to 95%. That lawful expenditure will run out fairly soon. It is difficult to be precisely clear on when that will be. I think the Northern Ireland Executive and the Department of Finance would have a clearer idea of that, which is why we have been asking for all that information. That has been coming in over the last couple of days.

If we get to the end of January and there is no more lawful expenditure, I am sure that schools and hospitals would stay open, but a lot of elective and other work would simply not be able to proceed, and somebody would have to make decisions about what cannot happen in February and March in Northern Ireland. That is a public service crisis that I am desperate to avoid. I do not have a plan at the moment to introduce a budget, but I am not shy of my responsibilities if that is something that I end up having to do.

Of course, it may be that the Northern Ireland Executive wake up one morning and go, “All right, we can’t do it. Please, Secretary of State, do it for us.” There are two elements here. They keep on saying that this is about, “Give us more money,” but there is also an element of trade-offs between different Departments. One of the things that is completely abnormal about Northern Ireland politics—I understand why it is in place, but none the less it is completely abnormal—is that there is no collective financial responsibility within the Executive. That is one of the things we will surely have to address.

There is one other thing that I would say. There is a “Julius Caesar” quote that I think is quite helpful: “The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves.” I often hear people blame the system, but actually it is how we all operate within the system that makes the difference. Sometimes, people just have to get over themselves, sit down with their political opponents and come to decisions, because that is what leadership is all about.

Robin Swann: You are a competent and experienced politician. Do you believe that Northern Ireland politicians are going to do that with an election coming in May, or do you see that as an impediment to bringing forward any agreement on the budget that brings about the necessary change? You spoke about the Northern Ireland waiting lists. You should acknowledge as well that they are coming down.

Chris Bryant: Yes.

Robin Swann: The challenge in health has always been the lack of a non-recurrent budget, which did not allow the transformation pieces we needed to take place. You are waiting on the Northern Ireland Executive to come to you and say, “Will you set a budget for us?” I do not think they ever will. At what stage do you say to them, “I am now setting a budget for you”?

Chris Bryant: As I say, at this moment today, I am not at that stage, but I am very conscious of my responsibility for ordinary, decent people in Northern Ireland. Incidentally, whenever I meet anybody in the street, they say, “How on earth do you deal with Stormont? Can you please make Stormont really work for Northern Ireland?” I think this maybe takes us to much bigger questions of reform that we are going to have to address. I have tried looking at some of the smaller things.

I am conscious that Claire Hanna raised the question with the Prime Minister a couple of weeks ago about one party being able to bring down the Executive. Actually, I do not feel at the moment that any one party wants to bring down the Executive—there is a little bit of trying to goad each other into doing that. I have tried to curate a process where I could say, “If you will give this, I could persuade the other party to give that,” but even that process has not succeeded so far. I am not trying to avoid the question; it is just that I have not come to that moment yet. But we are very conscious that we cannot let this run on forever and a day.

I should say that it is not just about this financial year. If there is no budget in place before the end of this financial year, they literally cannot spend anything next year—including their own receipts—so there has to be a budget in place. Of course, the new financial year starts in the middle of purdah. We are very conscious of the issues here. I want to make sure that people in Northern Ireland get a decent deal.

There is one other thing here. We talk about the big Departments that run things in Northern Ireland—there are Departments that have no idea what their budget will be next year, and that is not an efficient way of running any Government—but just think what it is like for the third sector, who are intrinsic to lots of the community cohesion policies in Northern Ireland. They have absolutely no idea whether they will have any money next year. As I say, I am conscious that we have to get this right.

I have met people in every single one of the political parties that I enormously respect, but I am trying to tease them, cajole them, persuade them, entice them into more co-operation. I would also add that I think sometimes all that talking to the media emphasises the divisions rather than the collaborations. If everybody switched off the media, did not go on anything for a month, it would probably be very good for the people of Northern Ireland. Sarah, do you want to come in?

Sarah Owen: I just want to say that I am so glad, Mr Swann, that you mentioned the public sector, but there are also businesses. I have done 18 visits to the business community since being in post, and every single one of them cannot fathom it, because they would not get away with not setting a budget for eight months or taking the difficult decisions, so why is it that the Northern Ireland Executive can? They have got used to—in their words—getting on with it without them, or finding workarounds. That is not where we should be at.

The Prime Minister’s growth in every postcode agenda includes BT. We cannot do that if businesses, particularly ones that we want to invest in Northern Ireland, are seeing a constant merry-go-round in the media and instability. There is nothing that kills off growth faster than instability, which is why I think that it is not just the public sector or the third sector, but business and the future of Northern Ireland’s economy that is at stake here. I completely echo everything that Chris has said.

Chris Bryant: Good! We are a team.

Sarah Owen: It is every part of society. I want to convey to the Committee just how angry I think every part of the Northern Irish community is and how let down they feel by the inability of the leadership to come together, set a budget and take some of the difficult decisions. They all have to take difficult decisions: every family in Northern Ireland have to live within their means and set budgets. They make really tough decisions every single day, and that is all they are asking of their politicians as well.

Robin Swann: I agree with you, Minister. That was the frustration that we felt as a party that Northern Ireland was not represented at the National Economic Council. If the two larger parties could not send somebody, there have been instances in the past in North South Ministerial Councils where other Ministers represented the Executive, and my party’s leader expressed that we were willing to do that. The people of Northern Ireland lost out because of that.

Simon Hoare: I want to underscore the point about the massively negative impact on the community and voluntary sector. The power of the work that they do is incalculable. We heard on a recent visit of the difference they made. They are often looking for grants of £130,000 or £180,000 to keep youngsters out of young offenders institutions, which would cost hundreds of thousands of pounds a year per capita. The economics are just so demonstrably clear.

Secretary of State and Minister, I do not think anybody would fail to agree with every word you said in your analysis of the importance of setting a budget, and the demonstrable negative impacts of not. Could you clarify why you are not already at the position of setting a budget? The elections are coming up, which provide their own imperative for division, trying to find wedge issues and the idea of “them and us”, which continue. Are we not in danger of continuing to accept a level of Northern Irish exceptionalism that we would not tolerate in any other part of the kingdom? What will be the trigger? Is it a date? Is it a fact? Is it a circumstance that says to you, “Right. Pen and pencil out. Here’s the budget. I’m imposing it. Crack on and deliver it”?

Chris Bryant: I want to look first at the long-term economic situation and fiscal accountability in Northern Ireland. The Executive has a legal responsibility to set a budget, but if they do not, nothing happens other than a crisis. They do not face the crisis; everybody else has to face the crisis. That is something that we have got to address. For instance, is it right that they can effectively go up to 95% on account? If it had remained at 45%, we would have faced all this back in July, when it is easier to make decisions in year that can have an effect to balance the budget.

Secondly, we may need some reforms of institutions to enforce fiscal accountability. I also, incidentally, want to look at political accountability because the structures do not really work. Most of the information sits with the Northern Ireland Executive, not with us. We have now asked for all that information, which they are required to provide under section 67 of the Northern Ireland Act. That is all coming in now from each of the different Departments. We may need more information.

It is unprecedented for the Westminster Government to set a budget over the heads of the Executive. Thus far, they have told me that they do not want me to do so, and I do not want to—

Simon Hoare: They are the authors of their own salvation, aren’t they?

Chris Bryant: I do not want to bring down the Executive, but we are very close to a moment where we have absolutely no choice, in the interests of the people of Northern Ireland.

Sorcha Eastwood: Good morning, Secretary of State. I think you rubbed up a few people the wrong way whenever you referred to this place as abnormal, but I completely agree with you. We need to have more people saying that. Frankly, I do not know of any other part of the UK—and Northern Ireland is part of the UK—that would tolerate this situation.

The Under-Secretary of State referred to business. Last week, I was with Lord Sarwar in my constituency. We went to two fantastic businesses—Camlin and Smiley Monroe—which are not just successful in Northern Ireland but global leaders bidding globally. It is beyond embarrassing to sit in this mother of Parliaments when we have fantastic indigenous local businesses that are able to lead the way, yet this is what we are reduced to. I am sure you have heard, Secretary of State, about train services now ending at 10 o’clock in Northern Ireland.

There has been talk of you imposing a punishment budget when it appears that Executive colleagues are attempting their own punishments for the people of Northern Ireland, at a time when my colleagues around the Executive table in our ministries have stretched themselves to ensure they are living within their means. You referred to Justice as one of them. It is beyond astonishing. Whether you are Unionist, nationalist or neither of those things in Northern Ireland, you are sick to the back teeth of it. It is a professional embarrassment.

We had a discussion last year around the issue of revenue raising and conditionality. You already referred to the sugar rush—the addiction—to short-term injections of money over the past decade into the Northern Ireland Executive. Yet, we seem to have never gone beyond a budget for a year, which is shocking. Is there conditionality around the £1.5 billion package, should it be agreed and offered at the moment? Is there some sort of figure around that in terms of revenue raising that you would be expecting, or what would be the conditionality on that settlement?

Chris Bryant: Yes, there is conditionality. There are a series of conditions, because I want to put Northern Ireland’s finances on an even keel in a lasting and sustainable way. That is why we have been keen on a three-year budget rather than a single-year budget, meeting the 124% in every year. With the £500 million, we actually get to 126% in each of the three years. When it comes to the spending review discussions that will start in the new year, we will want to make sure that we look at all those issues into the next spending review period.

Some of the conditionality around revenue raising is £100 million to be raised next year and £200 million the year after. That is actually a tiny amount of money. You only have to look back three years, I think, to see a block grant of roughly £15 billion, and it is now £20 billion. That is a significant increase in a short period of time, and there is no reason why the Executive should not be able to live within that if they do some of the self-help.

I will be very straight with the Committee; my anxiety over the next few months is this. Let’s say for the sake of argument that they set a budget and know that it does not balance—well, that’s no use—and on top of that, they do not legislate for any revenue raising for next year.

Sorcha Eastwood: Just for understanding, is this the scenario where people have said that they will seek to agree a budget now, in the interim, and continue spending running into the election period?

Chris Bryant: Yes.

Sorcha Eastwood: My understanding was that the Treasury itself said that that was not legal, in so far as they do not have the vires to sign off on that.

Chris Bryant: They have to settle a balanced budget.

Sorcha Eastwood: Correct.

Chris Bryant: And, on public sector pay, I really would like nurses, doctors, prison officers and everybody else in Northern Ireland to get their pay award, but this is something that the Executive should have been preparing for since last April and before, rather than suddenly finding towards the end of the year that it is not going to be possible for them to do.

Sorcha Eastwood: Very briefly in conclusion, Secretary of State, the scenario that you have just outlined underlines to me the cynicism at the heart of our set-up. I do not want to stray into future situations, but with a situation where a built-in failure is a constant, where the institutions can collapse at any point in time, surely to goodness—setting aside the political arguments around reform for a moment, so purely in financial and economic terms—does it not make more sense to take away that capability, that destructive built-in failure within this set-up, so that we have a constant assurance that never again will we be sitting here in November knowing that, potentially within weeks, our public sector workers might not be paid?

Chris Bryant: I think that this crisis, which is still slightly slow-burn but could accelerate quickly, really must make us all think long and hard about whether we have the right structures and institutions in Northern Ireland. That takes us back to a big discussion in relation to the way that everything was set up back in the Good Friday agreement and then in the St Andrews agreement: does a lot of this need review now? If we get to the 30th anniversary of the Good Friday agreement and we have not done some radical surgery, we will think that we are failing our citizens in Northern Ireland.

I should just say that that £200 million is not even 1% of the budget. There are lots of Departments in Government that are having to make much bigger savings and do much more revenue raising. The point that I was going to make is that I do not have the power to do that revenue raising in Northern Ireland; the only people who do are Ministers, but not if they become only caretaker Ministers. How we sort out next year’s finances—as well as what we do for this year—is one of the things that we are thinking very hard about at the moment.

Claire Hanna: I want to come in on some of the reforms. “Not in our stars, but in ourselves”—I suppose he was cautioning against the sense of powerlessness and destiny overtaking us. We are trying to do the opposite: to shape the conversation and enact some reforms and evolutions to prevent us from getting to the crisis. You just talked about what will happen if we get to the 30th anniversary—that is 2028—and have not reformed. Are you still planning to do that before May, so that we have the comfort of a return of the Assembly after May? How will you be initiating that, and what do you see as the priorities for reform?

Chris Bryant: As you know, because we met in one of those meetings, I have certainly been interested in doing what others have referred to as keyhole surgery before the elections next year, to make it more difficult to prevent the Assembly from sitting and doing its work—and, for that matter, to make it more difficult for one party to bring down the Executive. That is still in my mind, but I also have two other big issues that have, in a sense, steamed ahead.

One is the issue around fiscal accountability. Three months ago, I never thought that I would still be in the position where nobody had set a budget in Northern Ireland. When I met with party leaders in the Executive during the first and second rounds of budget discussions, people were saying, “You know what? If you get to about £500 million, we will probably be fine.” Then suddenly it is, “No, we need £1.6 billion”—or £900 million, £763 million this year, and so on.

What I am saying is that I am still interested. We have not made a final decision on where we are going on this. What is the big priority for us at the moment? Top of my list is fiscal accountability and making sure we can put Northern Ireland’s finances on an even keel, not just in this year, but in next year and the year after.

Claire Hanna: I appreciate that it is a moving picture. We are still unclear about exactly what process is happening at the moment, where the blockages are, and how we still have such different analyses from the different parties. Rocking up to the microphone is how politics gets done in Northern Ireland—the story presents itself at a bank of cameras and microphones, and it is spun in that way. I am not clear exactly what the process is, or where the bulges and contractions are and are not in those figures. It would be helpful for people to understand that.

Do you have in mind a specific package, programme or reform that could address that and could be folded in with some of the other keyhole surgeries? Chris Bryant: The three of us have all tried an awful lot of persuasion, and that has not achieved much thus far. At the moment, the political parties do not have the incentives to come to any kind of co-ordinated position. On a lot of issues, one week somebody will be wholeheartedly opposed to something, and two weeks later they are wholeheartedly in favour of it. That is difficult as well.

We are conscious that in the very near future we will have to provide a chunk of leadership and lay down what we think we need to do in this space. It is a bit of a work in progress at the moment, not least because we have only just had the numbers back from the section 67 requirement that we asked for.

My understanding is that the Northern Ireland Executive and the civil service have done a chunk of work on red, amber and green cuts that they could do in this financial year to make the budget add up. We have not seen that yet, but it will be a helpful piece of work. I have no desire to set a budget; I also have no desire to be the person who goes, “Right, you have to do this piece of revenue raising.”

Chair: You can, though.

Chair: That is available to you—to take significant new powers, if need be.

Chris Bryant: The very fact that I am mentioning it means that it is the kind of thing that we do have to think about, but I believe in devolution. I want devolution to work. Power sharing is difficult. I believe in devolution in Wales; I believe in devolution in Northern Ireland. To paraphrase from, I think, either “Rerum novarum” or “Quadragesimo anno”, it is better not to arrogate to a higher authority a power that could be best deployed more locally.

Chris Bryant: Well, we are developing them. It would be best if we had a system that made it more difficult for them not to take their seats. Baroness Anderson: I am so shy and retiring usually, but yes.
