Chair: Today, we are holding a further oral evidence session on the draft Conversion Practices Bill. This Joint Committee has been appointed to consider and report on the Government’s draft Conversion Practices Bill, which sets out provisions to prevent and criminalise abusive and harmful conversion practices. Des Johnson: Hi, everybody. I am Des Johnson. I am the chair of FFLAG. We are the longest-established UK charity that works specifically with parents, carers and families of LGBT+ people. Chair: You have also left us, I think, a paper expressing your views. Thank you very much indeed. I am going to put a general question first to all of you. Are the definitions of “abusive conversion practice” and “serious harm, alarm or distress” sufficiently clear, particularly in how they would apply in family settings?

Sarah Branson: That is an interesting question, because the language in the Bill—particularly in the schedule to the Bill, which would be the area of law that would impact on my jurisdiction, because those orders could be made within the family law setting—is not language that I recognise as a family lawyer.

We have a very clear and well-defined definition within my jurisdiction regarding circumstances in which the state can intervene in the lives of families. You may know about the threshold criteria contained in section 31 of the Children Act 1989, where a state can only intervene in a family if the court is satisfied that there is a likelihood or evidence of significant harm that is attributable to the care being provided by the parent, that not being reasonable to expect a parent to provide.

We are very familiar with terms such as “significant harm”. That is not the language of this Act, and therefore I am not clear whether it is the intention of Parliament in drafting this Act that there would be a different test for intervention in family life if the issue of conversion practices is engaged or whether it is drafting that is meant to replicate “significant harm”, but that is not what it says. When you are asking about whether I think the definitions are sufficiently clear, I have to say, as a family lawyer, they are not. I do not know whether the test is less or more than evidence of “significant harm”. Of course, we do have similar sorts of orders that already exist within the family court arena, and I will come on to talk about those if you wish.

Chair: I think we can develop that a little bit further, particularly with questions from Baroness Prentis, if I might leave that over just for a moment. Des Johnson, how do you feel about this? Is the language good enough?

Des Johnson: There are four specific areas where we would request changes. I will come to those in a minute. What I would like to say initially is that FFLAG and I believe the Bill is very welcome. It tackles a very specific form of abuse that is not covered elsewhere under current legislation. It brings us in line with a growing list of jurisdictions around the world, and it responds directly to calls from both the United Nations Commission on Human Rights and from the Council of Europe, and therefore it is long overdue, in our opinion.

We welcome that it offers protection on the basis of both sexual orientation and gender identity. We are well aware they are different, but they are both equally worthy of protection from abuse and harm, in our opinion. We like the fact that, as we understand the wording, it also includes those people with no sexual orientation—asexual—as well as those with no particular gender identity—in other words, agender.

We know that these abusive conversion practices happen. I think you have seen evidence. Of the 108,000 respondents to the 2018 national LGBT survey, which is the most authoritative source that we seem to have available, 2% had undergone conversion practices. That rises to 4% for trans people, and that has been confirmed by more recent research from both Galop and YouGov on behalf of Stonewall.

Chair: Do you have figures for the third category?

Des Johnson: What is the third category?

Chair: You have dealt with people who are gay, if I can put it that way, and you deal with the transgender changing people, but the third category is those who say they have no gender either way.

Des Johnson: We do not have reliable data on those people, but we know from our work with families that they exist.

Chair: They do exist, but you do not have figures.

Des Johnson: No.

It is helpful to set out the context of where the compulsion to change somebody else’s sexual orientation and gender comes from, because I think that helps understand the root cause of this abuse. It appears to come from two, in our opinion, false beliefs. The first is that these are matters of choice, and the second is that they are wrong—that somehow you are less than and that you are a less valuable part of society if you have a sexual orientation or gender identity that is different from what that person believes you should have. We know our children. We know that they know who they are, and we know that they are not broken and they do not need to be fixed.

In summary, we welcome this Bill because it protects all LGBT people from this form of abuse, and it sends a very clear and unambiguous message from the state that my kids and all the LGBT+ kids of the parents that we work with are not broken, they do not need to be fixed and anybody that believes that they do and forcibly tries to change them rightly risks criminal sanction.

I will be very brief on the specifics of the definitions. There are four that we would change. In clause 1(2), we think you should add suppression. Suppression is just as harmful as change, and there are a growing number of jurisdictions that include that, including France, Canada, Norway and New Zealand. Clause 1(3) deals with healthcare professionals. We are very comfortable that there is a healthcare exemption, but it needs to be limited in scope to professionals who are regulated by a statutory regulatory body or listed on a register held by an organisation accredited by the Professional Standards Authority. Otherwise, anybody could claim that healthcare exemption, and we do not believe that is the intent of the Bill.

In clause 1(4), we request that you consider rewording the term “abusive conversion practice”. In our opinion, all conversion practices are abuse and are abusive, but not all of them will meet the threshold of abuse laid out in the Bill. The long form of that would be something like “conversion practices that meet the threshold for criminality”. Maybe you can call them “criminally abusive conversion practices” to add an extra distinction, because all conversion practice is abuse, as defined in the Bill.

Finally, in clause 2(1), we ask you to add “or is likely to cause” alongside the definitions for serious harm, serious alarm and distress. By the time that a potential offender reaches that point, you have clearly already established conduct that is a conversion practice and meets a threshold of abuse. Therefore, at that point, we believe that courts and the police should be capable of determining whether something is likely to cause harm. You already use the test of “likely to cause” harm elsewhere in the draft Bill—in clause 3, as it relates to overseas, and later, as it relates to protection orders.

Chair: Thank you very much.

Matilda Gosling: I am concerned about the definitions of abusive conversion practices as they relate to gender identity specifically, and particularly in the context of children and young people. An equivalence is assumed within the Bill between sexual orientation and gender identity, whereas gender identity brings risks of long-term physical and mental health effects through a medicalisation pathway. The Bill also overlooks the relevance of social context, trauma and reasons other than an innate, immutable identity, for which somebody might identify as transgender. I can perhaps talk a bit more about data on, for example, the proportion who have experienced prior sexual abuse, and the over-representation of looked-after children within young people who identify as transgender.

The Bill potentially makes an abusive conversion practice of helping children and young people feel comfort in their body, or helping them feel that they do not have to conform to stereotypes. I am sure the panel is already aware that data from the former Gender Identity Development Service shows that the vast majority of adolescents who were referred to the service were same-sex attracted—either lesbian, gay or bisexual.

There is a risk of potentially targeting those young people and making them feel as though there is something wrong with their same-sex attraction if they are gender non-conforming. There is a link in the research between childhood gender non-conformity and later same-sex attraction. There is also a risk that parents and other responsible adults will not be able to have open conversations that help people feel comfortable with their gender non-conformity, their same-sex attraction and so on.

There is also a problem with the definitions, in that the Bill does not distinguish between adults and children. Some of the terms in the definitions seem much more appropriate to adults than to children. For example, “psychological or emotional pressure” has not been defined. It would be very difficult to separate ordinary parenting from that. Any parent will be aware that psychological or emotional pressure is part and parcel of family life. Parents make mistakes. Sometimes it is not always appropriate, and sometimes it is appropriate, and pressure needs to be used to support children’s long-term wellbeing and development.

On the definition of transgender identity, and particularly the point about transsexualism and not identifying solely as male or female, or not as male or female, those seem to be quite adult concepts. A child who identifies as not male or female potentially encapsulates ongoing changes in identity. Anyone who has spent time with a small child knows that they regularly identify as strange objects. Obviously, that would not be the intention of the Bill, but because the definitions have been so widely drawn, it could potentially draw in those examples.

Matilda Gosling: Sexual orientation is measurable by behaviour, attraction and identity, whereas transgender identity is only about a sense of who you are, for which you cannot have an objective measure. It relies entirely on self-report. Sex—as in biological, binary sex—is measurable and immutable, whereas identity is constantly shifting. You cannot say for certain whether, for example, a 13-year-old who might identify as the opposite sex has an ongoing state of gender identity that harm would be baked into, if people were to have conversations about that identity.

Lord Scriven: Thank you, that has been really helpful. I am a non-lawyer, and I hear that the existing law, with the definition that you have said, would deal with conversion practices in terms of psychological damage, economic pressure, etc. Based on your understanding of existing family law, I will give you two scenarios, and you can tell me whether you feel the existing law would or would not catch them.

Let us say parents subject their 14-year-old gay son to repeated deliverance sessions, sleep deprivation and isolation from friends. Would that be a criminal act? At the moment, would family law deal with that?

Sarah Branson: Yes. That would constitute, in my view, significant harm attributable to the care provided by the parents—that certainly not being what is reasonable to expect a parent to give. That would therefore trigger the court to have jurisdiction to make care orders, which could remove that child, or supervision orders to advise that a system befriend the family.

It would not give the local authority the ability to apply for protective orders, which are contained within the schedule to the Act because, at the moment, local authorities are not able to apply for non-molestation orders, prohibited steps orders or any section 8 orders. Care and supervision orders are the range of powers available to the court. That would fall under the category of significant harm and therefore give jurisdiction to the court to intervene.

Lord Scriven: My second scenario is that parents plan to send their 15-year-old child abroad for a conversion programme. Would the existing law be able to deal with that, or is there a gap in the law?

Sarah Branson: That is an interesting one. I have spent quite a lot of time looking at case law and the way that the courts have approached it. There is very little to help us. What we do not have within the existing family law framework are injunctions to prevent certain practices. A local authority has the ability to intervene and remove the child into foster care, if the test for significant harm or the likelihood of significant harm is met, or to support them through supervision orders.

If the policy decision that those practices need to be outlawed is made, there is a gap around the preventative order that we see in the schedule to the Act. I do not believe that exists, but we do have the inherent jurisdiction of the High Court. The High Court steps in when there is no legislation, like in deprivation of liberty cases. Parliament has not legislated on that yet—maybe you can help us out with that—but the High Court does intervene and uses its inherent jurisdiction to prevent significant harm.

I have not seen any examples of that. I have found two quite interesting High Court cases. They are different on the facts. Injunctions could theoretically be made, but Parliament has not told the courts what to do, and since we are engaged in this process, we can say there is a gap there.

Matilda Gosling: Okay. If a vexatious complaint were made, my understanding is that a conversion practice protection order allows anyone with the court’s leave to make such a complaint. I think there is a risk on several fronts. One is that a parent will self-censor—again, I am talking about gender identity specifically. On the second front, a child could potentially threaten a parent into complying with a particular framing of the world from the child’s perspective.
