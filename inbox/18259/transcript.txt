Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Dr Andrew Tickell and Dr Conor McCormick.

Chair: Good morning and welcome to this meeting of the Scottish Affairs Committee. Today we will be taking oral evidence for the first time as part of our inquiry into the dual role of Scotland’s Lord Advocate. Before we begin, I would like to briefly set out the Committee's approach to this inquiry. The Committee has very narrow terms of reference and will focus specifically on appraising the case for and against splitting the dual role and exploring different options for reform.

The inquiry therefore will not touch on many of the wider issues associated with the recent work of the Lord Advocate or the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service. In particular, we will not discuss individual charging decisions. These are properly a matter for the Crown Office to undertake independently. There are also a number of ongoing legal matters that are off-limits, and in some cases may be sub judice. I will maintain order in line with the Committee’s agreed terms of reference this morning and in any future sessions considering this matter. I will also put on record—not just for the benefit of today’s witnesses but for future witnesses and members of the public following our work—that that is the case.

Having got the formalities out of the way, could I now welcome our witnesses and ask them to introduce themselves and their role? I will start with you, Dr Tickell.

Dr Tickell: Good morning, everybody. I am a senior lecturer in law at Glasgow Caledonian University.

Dr McCormick: Good morning, everyone. I am a reader in the Law School at Queen’s University Belfast.

Chair: As a Committee, we are familiar with the reasons and the historic nature of the role of the Lord Advocate and why it is a dual role at the moment. I do not really want to go into the historical origins of that arrangement, but can I ask you if there were any discussions around the time of devolution about whether the dual role should be retained or considered in a different way?

Dr Tickell: That is an interesting question. I do not actually know the answer to that question. That is not a good start. Certainly, if you look at the Scotland Act 1998 and what became the foundational documents of devolution, it notably did not attempt to codify what the roles of the Lord Advocate are, above and beyond saying that the role as public prosecutor is carved out and protected.

It was clearly felt that perhaps given how many different responsibilities—some of them quite eccentric—have accrued to that great office of state over the years, we should just fold this office into this new devolution framework. It was felt that we should really focus on the big change around creating a Scottish Parliament, rather than trying to revisit at the same time the responsibilities and roles of an ancient Law Officer of the state who does many, many different things.

Chair: Certainly, as someone who followed the debates around that time very closely, I do not actually remember it being discussed. Then again it probably was not something I was personally looking out for.

Dr McCormick: I have read through the reports of the time and there were certainly debates centred more around the way in which the Lord Advocate’s responsibilities should be divided between the UK Government and the devolved institutions. In that context there was quite a heated debate and a vote around whether the Solicitor General should be the devolved version of the main Law Officer, and the Lord Advocate be retained as the UK Government’s primary legal adviser and all the consequences that would flow from that.

From my memory of it, there was not much attention around the dual role as it is understood here, as between the independent prosecutorial role and the legal advisory role. I also remember reading some archival papers relating more to the Advocate General for Scotland, where there certainly was consideration given to whether that role should be held by just an official; somebody non-politically active. That was roundly dismissed by the Government of the day. However, I cannot remember any specific coverage of that.

John Cooper: We are where we are with the Scotland Act. As we know, it is a fine piece of machinery; it is a beautiful Swiss watch. As time has gone on, has this dual role proven to be helpful? Are there advantages to it? Is there a case to be made for keeping it? Obviously, the headline is the idea that we should get rid of this, but are there some advantages to it?

Dr Tickell: As you know, I am in favour of splitting the role, so I see the disadvantages as tending to outweigh the advantages. It certainly brings into the heart of the Scottish Government a Law Officer who is concerned about guiding Ministers on what the rule of law is. Previous Lord Advocates, such as Elish Angiolini, have highlighted the importance as head of the Prosecution Service of being able to make the arguments in Cabinet, in Government, and for resourcing, for example. We know that decisions of the Scottish Parliament around what you can criminalise and what offences should be looked at more carefully will often have resource implications in terms of the number of cases being indicted in the High Court, for example.

Previous Lord Advocates have said that it was helpful to be able to be in the room, not just as an official but as part of the Government, in effect. That enabled them to make the case directly to the First Minister and the Cabinet Secretary for Finance. We need these resources. That seems to have been the key benefit that previous incumbents have identified.

John Cooper: Has there been an argument for the Lord Advocate to sit in Cabinet to help shape policy?

Dr Tickell: That is an advantage for any Law Officer, frankly, being in the room. As you said, devolution is a Swiss watch, and a Swiss watch is not known for being a simple operation. Devolution began in quite a complicated way and has only grown more complicated as successive reforms have been made to the Scotland Act. With any given policy issue, whether you are a campaigner or a politician trying to sponsor a proposal through Holyrood, you have to begin with a much more legalistic set of questions than meet this Parliament in legislating. You cannot just say, “We are sovereign and can pass any legislation you want.” The question is, is this reserved? Can we modify this provision?

Increasingly, devolution has grown more complicated. The case for having a Law Officer in the room to be able to say to Scottish Ministers, “We can do this, we can’t do that. Here’s why,” has always been important but it has become more and more important as devolution has become more complex.

John Cooper: The question then would be whether that should be the Lord Advocate. Obviously, the Scottish Government need good legal advice; we all do. The argument moves on to whether that should be the Lord Advocate. We are not going to solve that one today, necessarily. Dr McCormick, do you have any thoughts? Do you think there is a good case to be made for keeping the arrangements as they currently stand?

Dr McCormick: Focusing on the advantages and leaving aside the points that Dr Tickell has already made, I would probably make the additional point that in any aspect of the constitution, if you have somebody who is wearing two hats, they are capable of being viewed as having either a conflicting set of roles or a synergistic set of roles. I am thinking specifically of the idea that if somebody is known to have to make prosecutorial decisions independently, that will naturally reinforce the independence with which they are viewed when giving legal advice, and vice versa.

The same is true in other contexts. If you are a councillor working at council level and you have to decide planning applications or licensing decisions, you have to decide that impartially. That should not necessarily lead to doubt being cast on your ability to represent your party’s interests in other capacities. The two roles issue can be viewed either way. It is important to think about it from a potentially positive perspective because that is your question, but it can actually be viewed in other ways too.

Susan Murray: Can you take us to the disadvantages and the main risks of the dual role, please?

Dr Tickell: Yes, absolutely. In terms of recent debates around this role in Scotland, the question about perceptions of bias is absolutely critical here. Is it compatible for the same person to be the Government’s chief legal adviser and to be the chief prosecutor in the country? That goes to the nub of the question. Without suggesting any impropriety we can take that general formulation of public law from Porter v. Magill of would a reasonable person looking at this conceive there was a real possibility of bias? The answer is empirically yes, because we are sitting here talking about it and have been for some time.

This is not a novel controversy in terms of the roles of the Lord Advocate. There have been ongoing controversies—as we were discussing outside—about Law Officers’ roles where they are partly in Government and partly outside it. There are inherent tensions in any system we adopt. In terms of public confidence in the process, there are clear challenges with seeing that the Lord Advocate is sitting around the Cabinet table with the Scottish Ministers and quite separately taking decisions on whether individuals should be prosecuted. That cannot be addressed except by removing one of the roles from the Lord Advocate, which I would suggest would be the legal advisory role in terms of Ministers.

Dr McCormick: The second disadvantage that is commonly referred to is workload: the idea that there is now such a significant number of prosecutorial functions to be discharged even within that side of the office. That is to be discharged alongside the very complex understanding of constitutional law and any other form of legal advice that might be requested in the advisory role. I have heard that mentioned quite frequently.

I am not persuaded by it because you are effectively talking about a ministerial, top-of-the-pyramid figure who can be asked to give advice when required. There are over 80 advocate deputes on the prosecution side. There are many professional legal advisers on the other side of the House. It is those complex and difficult questions that ultimately reach the Law Officers. Workload does not really persuade me, nor does the perception problem because the same disadvantage can be levelled at anybody wearing two hats.

Susan Murray: Can I ask you both if the fact that the Lord Advocate is a Scottish Minister specifically undermines the independence of the system of public prosecutions?

Dr Tickell: We need to distinguish between roles and membership of the Scottish Ministers because they are not quite the same thing. That might seem like a fairly wafer-thin distinction, but I am not sure that it is. The perception challenge facing the office at the moment is about roles, not necessarily about being ex officio a Scottish Minister.

In fact, I would argue that we should keep that approach. If we reformed this particular office, it would make sense for the Lord Advocate, focused primarily on their criminal functions, to retain that accountability to Parliament and to be participating as part of the wider Scottish Ministers. It is a question of roles. Are the roles compatible? In a sense whether they are a member of the Scottish Ministers is a distinct question.

Douglas McAllister: Do you think that the example of the consideration by the Scottish Government in relation to the safer drug consumption rooms maybe helps demonstrate why there is a need for that dual role of the Lord Advocate? Both as the chief legal adviser in relation to drug policy and as the chief prosecutor, she has to take the decision in relation to relaxing whether someone is prosecuted around that zone for possession of drugs. Do you think that helps the public understand that there is no bias there but perhaps an enabler in relation to Government policy?

Dr Tickell: That is absolutely right. This really gets overlooked, which is one of the reasons I highlighted it in my written submission. The Lord Advocate at the moment, and any previous Lord Advocate, takes politically significant decisions. If you asked the Lord Advocate whether they were being political, they might say no because these are people who do not see themselves as politicians. They see themselves as primarily lawyers. Let us use the softer language of policy. Is it a major policy decision to say it is not in the public interest to prosecute people in safer drug consumption rooms? Absolutely it is. Is it a major policy decision for the Lord Advocate to systematically start appealing against effectively lenient sentences in rape cases? Absolutely it is.

In terms of the political fray, for example, we have seen Lord Advocates coming into Parliament to advocate for particular legislation. When Frank Mulholland was Lord Advocate, he strongly supported the offensive behaviour at football legislation, which has since been repealed. Dorothy Bain went into Parliament more recently to talk about her support for the Victims Bill, which passed Parliament in 2025. I am not saying that is inappropriate. In fact, I am saying the opposite. These are political interventions, policy interventions. Therefore, it is one of the reasons we should see the Lord Advocate not as a party political person necessarily but as an inherently political role, which quite appropriately has political accountability mechanisms associated with it.

Dave Doogan: Dr Tickell, we should not see the Lord Advocate primarily as a political person. You make an interesting distinction between politics and policy, one of which I would argue is much more legitimate than the other for the Lord Advocate. Should we not see the Lord Advocate as emphatically not a political person? They are in Cabinet and they are a Minister, but they are not a politician.

Is it part of this housekeeping review, if you want to call it that, that there may be advantages to having that distinct ministerial capacity as a non-politician in Cabinet—leaving aside the chief prosecutor—in terms of legal advice? Is that inherently advantageous, neutral or disadvantageous? How important is it to be very clear that, no matter who the Lord Advocate happens to be at the time, they are about as far away from a politician as it is possible to be, with the exception of being in Government?

Dr Tickell: Historically that is not true, as we know. For example, going back to the history of the early phases of devolution, Lord Hardie as Lord Advocate played a significant role in steering the Scotland Act through in 1998. Admittedly, in recent times, the appointments have tended to be not obviously partisan or political party members of one kind or the other.

It depends on your approach to the word “politics”. I have a fairly robust approach to what counts as politics. Taking decisions of the kind I outlined a moment ago is political, even if they are not coming from an entrenched party political point of view. That is one of the inherent tensions in this whole debate. Very often, people are relying on rather stark and not particularly persuasive distinctions between, effectively, officials who are political eunuchs at one stand and then at the other, we have all these terrible politicians being political over here. In fact it is more nuanced than that as far as I am concerned.

Equally, under the Scotland Act you could be a politician. If John Swinney had thought that one of his colleagues was an excellent fit to be Solicitor General or Lord Advocate, there is nothing prohibiting them from being appointed to that role at this stage, except perhaps for public opinion, public reactions and so forth. We need to unpick the idea of what counts as political. I am not suggesting that Lord Advocates are the same as a Cabinet Secretary advocating for a Bill. Clearly they are not, because they are Law Officers. Law Officers are creatures of the law and creatures adjacent to politics, and have to be loyal to both.

I would say one final thought on that. I am always very struck by something I heard many years ago from Dominic Grieve, a former Attorney General. He was giving a lecture that I attended. He made the point that when he was sitting around the Cabinet table talking to his colleagues about a policy that the Government of the day wished to adopt, he thought it was terrifically useful for the Attorney General to be able to say, “I am a fellow traveller. I am a Conservative MP. I am not against you. This isn’t for legal reasons as a way of stymying and blocking a policy. I’m on your side, and I can help you solve problems.”

When we think about that other dimension of the Law Officer’s role, that is something we do not currently have in Scotland because we have slightly more apolitical Lord Advocates. It could be useful for the Government of the day to have a more political one, as some evidence before the inquiry has highlighted.

Dr McCormick: May I make another related point? Another way of framing that would be to talk about neutrality, which is the way you see the same debate conducted when you are talking about lawyers outside politics. This is an age-old Law Officer debate that I associate mostly with a great article by Neil Walker, a famous Scottish professor. In that he makes the point that, whether you are talking about the Lord Advocate or any Law Officer in their capacity as a prosecutorial officer or a legal adviser, you run into the same debate. To what extent are they some totally impartial, neutral applicator, an automaton? To what extent can they advance the client’s interests?

In fact, lawyers can be criticised just as strongly for advancing their client’s interests too much, but that is not a legitimate criticism of a lawyer any more than it is a legitimate criticism of a politician. It is helpful to bear that in mind because, ultimately, what I see happening all the time in debates with regard to many Law Officers is people losing sight of that debate. Here we have a discussion about splitting those two roles, but you would still be retaining the same tension in both versions. You would still have the people who want to have somebody really neutral and not so interested in advancing the client’s interests, in the way that Dominic Grieve was discussing, and then the other who is all for advancing the client’s interests to the maximum extent. The client in this case being the Government.

Chair: Does it therefore come down to finding what the system is that avoids that perception as much as possible—I take your point about lawyers always having that dilemma—doing whatever it is that needs to be done to make it clear what those roles actually are and how they are separated? I do not want to prejudge the Committee’s decisions. I certainly have not come to a conclusion personally, but maybe that is one of the arguments for splitting the roles so that you can have that avoidance of a perception. It may not work, but what I am trying to say is, are we really trying to address a problem of perception rather than actuality?

Dr McCormick: I feel sure that is the case. I am just sceptical about perception alone being enough of a justification to dismantle a centuries-old office. I view this as taking a scalpel instead of a sledgehammer to the problem. If there are particular issues to be remedied, by all means tackle them. However, I just cannot imagine that separating the prosecutorial role from the legal advisory role would necessarily prevent these debates from unfolding because those would be reframed against the purely legal adviser person. You are being too partisan; you are interpreting the law too favourably towards your client in a way that the courts might not, or vice versa.

That is where safeguards become all important, of course. I have always thought the Scottish model was the best of those in operation in the UK because of the clarity with which the two roles are distinguished. Certain functions are statutorily protected and must be discharged independently. Others do not have to be. That is all quite clearly set out. If anybody falls foul of those safeguards, they can be judicially reviewed, among other things.

Lillian Jones: To both gentlemen, what safeguards currently exist to protect the independence of Scotland’s system of public prosecutions from political interference? Are those safeguards strong enough?

Dr Tickell: The starting point for this is the Scotland Act, which sets out specific provisions that protect the Lord Advocate’s reserved functions, which are effectively those functions that she exercises independently of any other person. The principle that any decision making on prosecutions is independent of any other person is entrenched in the Scotland Act. MSPs could not pass legislation to change that provision as things stand. That is a strong statutory guarantee that the Lord Advocate effectively is not subject to the general principles of collective Government responsibility when it comes to decision making on prosecutions in particular. That is an important statutory safeguard.

The Crown Office has a range of internal processes when it tries to deal with some trickier types of cases, such as questions of conflict of interest and prior connections with cases. Those are not on a statutory basis. They could be, as the Law Society of Scotland suggests. You could have a more robust framework in a statutory sense for what the Lord Advocate should do if she is effectively compromised out of a case.

Perhaps more prominently than the political examples we have looked at recently are cases where Lord Advocates have been involved in previous representation of clients in a case, which are much more common than cases involving prosecutions. We have some recent examples, such as the malicious prosecution cases involving Rangers football club. That was a case where the Lord Advocate decided she could not be involved. Similarly we had the Sheku Bayoh inquiry, which is ongoing, into his death in police custody. Again, because of her prior connections to that controversy, Dorothy Bain decided she could not be involved in that process. There is a basic statutory framework but there are other internal safeguards and processes that the Crown Office has put in place.

Dr McCormick: Yes, there are so many. There are so many legal and political safeguards. As Dr Tickell has said, the Scotland Act is the right place to start. You have the statutory protection around the functions that are reserved, such as the prosecutorial functions and the investigation of death functions. Beyond that, as I am sure you know, there are also provisions that exempt the Lord Advocate in the discharge of those functions from collective responsibility, which is reinforced by the ministerial code. Beyond that, in the discharge of all functions you have things such as the Human Rights Act 1998 and the Scotland Act’s own compatibility functions but they do not apply in every circumstance.

What does that mean in terms of a safeguard in real terms? As I just said, it means you can judicially review the Lord Advocate in the event that you suspect or have evidence to show a decision has not been exercised with true independence. There is no such case. As Dr Tickell has just said, there are many cases that have been taken on other grounds. Not for a breach of the statutory independence provision but for compatibility with human rights or on grounds of a malicious prosecution. That includes prosecutorial functions.

You have the assisted dying policy guidance being judicially reviewed for its compatibility with article 8. You have a refusal to institute fatal accident inquiries into infected blood scandal victims, which the courts then required. There is accountability and a safeguard working well. The Lord Advocate says, “No, I’m not going to require that.” The courts look at it through the lens of human rights and say, “No, you must.”

There are safeguards that can be turned to in the legal sphere, and the same is obviously true of the political sphere. That is the great advantage of this model, to my mind. In respect of the Rangers case, for instance, you could reflect on the level of damages awarded by the court. You could also reflect on the fact that the Lord Advocate attended Parliament and apologised. There was that political responsibility, and the two worked together. I had better stop there, but there are substantial safeguards in place.

Douglas Lumsden: I just want to remind everyone that I was an MSP earlier in this Session. Dr Tickell, you mentioned the Lord Advocate recusing herself. What would that actually mean in practice? Would that mean that she did not take any part in the process? Would it also mean that she would not be kept updated on anything that was taking place during those cases?

Dr Tickell: In terms of how this actually works in practice, this has not been very publicly accessible until fairly recently. The Crown Office published in August of this year two pieces of guidance on how it approaches these recusal processes. My understanding is that effectively the Lord Advocate would devolve the decision making to Crown Counsel—a senior lawyer within the Crown Office—who would take all decisions independently of the Law Officers in cases, say, involving a politician or where they are personally compromised out of being involved in the case.

From developments, we know that this does not mean the Lord Advocate does not know anything about these cases. We know from the controversial communications to John Swinney that she had an awareness that Peter Murrell had been indicted, for example. That was a factual statement the Lord Advocate gave the First Minister. There is clearly some awareness on the part of Law Officers about what is going on. I am not sure whether that is necessarily sinister in terms of the fact that they still have overall responsibility for their ministerial office.

Douglas Lumsden: This is where the perception might come from, where the ordinary man on the street thinks, “Hold on, this doesn’t seem a bit right.” The Lord Advocate sits in Cabinet with someone and is not taking part in the decision-making process but she is aware of what is going on. There could be a perception that that information is being fed to the rest of the Government.

Dr Tickell: That absolutely would be an understandable perception that people might have. For many people, when they think about the law, with a capital T and a capital L, they think about criminal law. Looked at in a dim light and from a relatively underinformed perspective, you might think that the Lord Advocate is at the same time the prosecutor who is involved in a case and your defence agent. That is not really what is going on in these cases, but people can understandably see it in that way.

There has clearly been controversy about whether Dorothy Bain was correct to share those factual updates with the First Minister on two occasions. Reference has been made to 30 other examples over the history of devolution where the Law Officers have shared information on cases, from the Lockerbie case to the naked rambler. I found it rather remarkable that it merited political scrutiny.

The argument in defence of it is that it informs the Government about a high-profile case that they are likely to be asked about. In this case it represented a merely factual update after the event where an indictment had already been served. Some evidence before the Committee suggests that that is entirely above board. It is merely factual updating of the Government of the day, as the Lord Advocate might do to the Prime Minister and has done in the past when we have had criminal cases connected with people associated with the Prime Minister.

Equally, if this is a communications problem—that is a big part of it—it is not satisfactory that we are getting this published only in 2026. These conflicts of interest are not new. In my experience dealing with the Crown Office in general, as journalistically adjacent, sometimes it can feel very inscrutable in terms of its internal processes. I welcome publishing a clearer route to decision making around this in August 2026, but it comes rather late in what has been a fairly long-standing tension since devolution or predating devolution.

Douglas Lumsden: If the Lord Advocate knew that the then First Minister and her husband were going to be arrested, would she feel concerns about the running of the country and that she might have to tell that person what might be coming?

Dr Tickell: Is that a hypothetical question?

Douglas Lumsden: Exactly, yes.

Dr Tickell: It is a hypothetical question. I do not know whether the Crown Office would consider that to be an appropriate thing to disclose to the First Minister. Certainly in terms of what it has published in 2026, it has set out a framework that is clearly informed by the data protection framework, but is there a legitimate aim in making a disclosure? Is it proportionate for them to disclose this information to Scottish Ministers in the public interest? In terms of the role of the police in leading inquiries, it seems more likely than not that the Lord Advocate would not be aware that that was going to happen in most cases. Therefore the hypothetical might not arise.

Douglas Lumsden: You have covered some of this already, and you mentioned the potential benefits of a political appointment. You have covered that with Dominic Grieve’s examples in the past. Are there any other benefits of having a political person in that role?

Dr Tickell: It gives the Scottish Government the choice. It gives them the choice to have someone who is a fellow traveller, whether that is in terms of party politics or just general attitudes. That probably informed Alex Salmond’s decision to retain Elish Angiolini as Lord Advocate. I do not know what her views are on the constitution, but there was a sense that she was broadly in alignment with the social outlook of the Administration. Therefore, they were not particularly losing anything. We have not had an example in recent times of a politician being appointed to these roles, but it raises the question of why we should prohibit it. Is there any reason to prohibit having someone who is involved or connected through politics uniquely in Holyrood?

You see it now in litigation strategies. I suggest that the Lord Advocate has four roles rather than just two, but one of them is leading the Scottish Government’s approach to litigation involving Scottish Ministers. That can involve significant strategic calls. For example, as we sit here, over in the Supreme Court across the square they are hearing argument about the Ammori appeal. The Attorney General will have no doubt made significant tactical decisions about where to prioritise the case under legal advice.

It goes back to that issue of how legal advice is not simply an inert, “Here’s the law. I’m giving it to you, go away and think about it.” It is problem-solving and considering the ambiguities. The law is not two plus two equals four. It often deals with concepts that are open to interpretation, such as proportionality. It often deals with different and difficult choices about what arguments to run in court. It seems to me that it is perfectly reasonable to have an Attorney General who represents what the Government of the day think is the best approach to arguing that case in the public interest.

Douglas Lumsden: If the dual role was retained and they continued to serve as prosecutors, should they be allowed to be MSPs?

Dr Tickell: For me, it would be strange if you appointed an MSP to that role in the current political context.

Douglas Lumsden: You can just now, can you not?

Dr Tickell: Yes. There is no prohibition on it, and no expectation that they would or would not be an MSP. If we are concerned about wider questions of perception, making that someone who is not a Member of the Scottish Parliament would be consistent with saying, “These are reserved prosecutorial functions that are connected with Government, accountable to the Scottish Parliament and in a sense to one side of the general fray of politics.”

Dr McCormick: Briefly on your first question about recusal, it is worth making the point that the same issue can arise in practically any legal context. If you work in a set of chambers in England for instance, you might find yourself having to recuse yourself from working in the same building as teams that are acting against you. The importance of setting up professional firewalls is a matter of professional ethics to me, which exists in and out of Government. I just wanted to make that point briefly.

On whether having a political Law Officer model is a good thing, on top of what Dr Tickell has already said, it might be worth reminding everyone that under the current arrangements at least the Lord Advocate is still subject to collective responsibility in the discharge of their ministerial functions: running the offices, handling parliamentary questions, and upholding the Government’s policy when not giving legal advice. That is important to bear in mind too. Having someone with the same outlook is obviously important in that context sometimes.

Douglas Lumsden: Does that mean that the Lord Advocate could not properly recuse themselves because they still have to be kept updated on things that are taking place within the legal framework?

Dr McCormick: Yes, that is right. It is about the nature of the communication at the end of the day. It is purely informational; there are no performative signals or instructions. It is the same kind of communication that any member of the public would be entitled to have if they made the right inquiries and had the knowledge to know which questions to ask.

Douglas Lumsden: That is the problem. The general public are not getting the same information as the Lord Advocate. It opens up the perception that that information could then be going to other places. That is a perception of course.

Dave Doogan: Dr McCormick, we heard just recently from Dr Tickell that over the course of devolution we have had somewhere in the region of 30 disclosures. Is that right?

Dr Tickell: That is the number in the public domain.

Dave Doogan: There have been some 30 disclosures by Lord Advocates to various First Ministers over the years. There is no suggestion that that is inappropriate, that the disclosures were inappropriate and not in the furtherance of some legitimate interest, public or otherwise. In so far as there is a latent concern about the dual role and the ability of the Lord Advocate to give perhaps privileged information to a Minister or First Minister, in the history of devolution, is it your understanding that there has been such a controversial disclosure? Are these 30-odd disclosures much of a muchness and actually the routine business of being Lord Advocate?

Dr McCormick: I am not sure that I am the best person to answer that particular question, only because I know as much as you do in relation to the information that has been made available. Truthfully, there is not a lot more I can say on that particular one.

Dr Tickell: There are probably at least two different ways this has proven controversial here, one of which is the point that Dorothy Bain had recused herself from involvement in this case. It goes to what Douglas Lumsden was just asking me: what does that mean? Some people might take that to mean that she does not know anything about it, and therefore another person is entirely responsible for it and she is excusing herself from the whole process. To some people, this might seem incompatible with that.

It certainly is the case that the Crown Office has made these disclosures in a range of different cases over the years, predominantly in high-profile cases that Ministers are likely to be asked about. The case we are talking about is certainly one of those. It was very likely when Peter Murrell was indicted that the First Minister and others would be asked about it.

The second rationale given by the Crown Office for doing this is to remind politicians of their responsibilities under the Contempt of Court Act 1981, in particular not to say anything about a case that may give rise to substantive prejudice or impediment to the course of justice in active criminal proceedings. One might hope that politicians would all retain some sense of the proprieties of that but maybe it is incorrect to assume that politicians would necessarily know that they should not be wildly speculating about such cases.

John Cooper: Can I just ask a quick question on that point?

Chair: Just a second, Mr Cooper. Mr Doogan is still asking his question.
