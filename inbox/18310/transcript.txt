Examination of Witnesses

Witnesses: Baroness Cass and Professor Gillian Leng.

Chair: This afternoon, we are holding an oral evidence session on the draft Conversion Practices Bill. This Joint Committee has been appointed to consider and report on the Government’s draft Bill, which sets out provisions to prevent and criminalise abusive and harmful conversion practices. We offer our thanks to our witnesses for attending this session. I am sure that I do not need to remind everybody that we are dealing with very sensitive matters, and we appreciate that this can be a challenging area, especially for our witnesses.

I will begin by asking our two witnesses to introduce themselves. Baroness Cass, would you be kind enough to introduce yourself, please?

Baroness Cass: I am Hilary Cass. I was the chair of the independent review of gender identity services for children and young people, which I foolishly allowed to become eponymously named after me. I need to declare that I am now an independent adviser to DHSC on gender services for children and young people.

Chair: Thank you. Professor Leng?

Professor Leng: Thank you for inviting me. I am the president of the Royal Society of Medicine, a multi-specialty body, and the fairly new president of the Medical Protection Society, a defence body. I also come with 20 years of experience at NICE, developing guidance or guidelines for the NHS. I therefore come with breadth but not depth in these subject areas.

Chair: May I begin by putting a fairly general question to you, Baroness Cass? You have welcomed the draft Bill; I think you said that, compared with previous drafts, it gives a clearer definition of what conversion practices are, but also of what they are not. Do you think that the Bill’s definition of abusive conversion practices is clear enough to do what you have been saying?

Baroness Cass: No, I am afraid I don’t. I should clarify that. I met with Liv Bailey when she was Minister, and she explained to me the principles of how the Bill would operate. It was encouraging that there was indeed more clarity about what it would cover and would not cover, but I had not actually seen the Bill, and I said that I could not give a statement until I had. Unfortunately, I was not sent the Bill until about 15 minutes before they wanted the press comment, so I gave a statement on the principles that I had been told about, but said that I was looking forward to pre-leg scrutiny, because clearly I needed to read the Bill.

Having obviously now spent a long time thinking about it, I unfortunately do not think that it is anywhere near tight enough to avoid serious unintended consequences.

Chair: Others will be questioning you in more detail on this, but could you summarise very briefly where you see the weaknesses?

Baroness Cass: Yes. Although there is a health carve-out, which I did welcome, the problem is in the definition of what it means for a person to fall “far below the standards reasonably expected of a person in their position.” At the moment, we do not have clear standards. Moreover, standards are highly contested in this area, and there are many who disagree strongly with the approach recommended in my review.

The jeopardy, particularly for healthcare professionals, is that it is very easy for a young person who is understandably distressed after what is inevitably a painful discussion—possibly angry and upset that they have not, for example, got on to the medication pathway that they want—to feel, and report, that the professional has behaved in what they deem to be a very negative way.

The further challenge is that unfortunately there are also malign actors—I have seen social media quotes from them—looking to exploit young people who may feel that they have had an adverse experience, in order to pursue some of the people working in this field. That is a significant problem. Many people are being called conversion therapists: myself, the right honourable Wes Streeting, clinicians working in the new gender services and other clinicians you have heard from on this Committee, who are just doing careful, exploratory work with teenagers. That is one whole area.

My second concern relates to parents. I know that there is absolutely no intention for them to be caught up in this, but that is unavoidably a potential risk of the way in which the Bill is framed. Things like not agreeing to change your child’s pronouns, or restricting money if you think they are going to use it to get a breast binder or hormones—all those things could fall within the reach of this Bill. Those are significant worries, I think.

Lord Isaac: Thank you, Baroness Cass; that is really helpful. May I start by asking whether you still stand by paragraph 11.5 of your report, which says that “no LGBTQ+ group should be subjected to conversion practice”?

Baroness Cass: I absolutely do. Moreover, throughout most of the review, we had a statement to that effect on my website, saying that those practices are abhorrent and are not something that anyone would condone. I have obviously thought about this for a very long time. I have racked my brain as to how, and have spoken to Ministers in the previous Administration and this one as to how one might enact a Bill that protects the people it is supposed to protect but does not accidentally entrap those you do not want it to.

Baroness Brown of Silvertown: May I ask a follow-up? Baroness, are you aware of any of the other legislation passed by other legislators in this area? Do you think any of those make a better Bill than the one that is in front of you?

Baroness Cass: Yes. In fact, the GMC, which you will hear from next, has done a much more thorough review of legislation in other jurisdictions, but the problem is that it is all relatively new. I understand that no cases have been pursued yet, and I also do not know whether it has had a chilling effect or not, so I am not sure that I can say anything very useful on that.

Lord Isaac: Can I come back to the very helpful amendment that you sponsored to the Health Bill? I think it perhaps addresses some of the concerns that you have identified this afternoon and a way forward, and I am quite keen to explore some of that.

Over the past few weeks, we have been looking at questions of what is or is not a health professional. In your amendment, you very helpfully talk about somebody who is registered but also somebody who meets the standards reasonably expected of a person in that position. It would be good to hear your view on that.

The second area for us has been exploratory conversations that take place in a therapeutic or medical context. You very helpfully talk about how those conversations could take place exploring the person’s distress but without presuming any particular outcome. We have been asking questions about affirmative therapy. Could you help us with those two areas? That will certainly expedite some of the questions that we have this afternoon.

Baroness Cass: Yes. This was an attempt—it was obviously a probing amendment—to see whether one could tighten this up, and I have shared it with the clinicians in the new services. Suffice it to say that there are four new hubs, and it is worth saying where they are. In London, that involves Great Ormond Street, Evelina, which is Guy’s and Tommy’s, and the Maudsley. In the north of the country, it is Liverpool—Alder Hey—and Manchester. Then there are Bristol and Cambridge, which has just come on. You can see those are very impressive children’s hospital centres. They have responded as a group with their concerns, and many of them have phoned me individually and I have shared this with them, but that has not really allayed their concern that you could still be the subject of a vexatious complaint. As you know with these things, the issue is not so much whether you are found guilty; it is the whole process of the inquiry. As colleagues behind me from the GMC will know, there are people who commit suicide while awaiting the outcome of a GMC referral. The chilling effect is not theoretical; it is real, and it is here today.

Lord Isaac: Do you think your amendments go some way to address those concerns?

Baroness Cass: I am trying to be helpful to this Bill, obviously, but it is still not allaying people’s anxieties that they could still be taken through a process, even if they are exonerated, or a private prosecution. The problem about this area is that it is hard to recruit to, and it is hard to retain staff. It is a sort of sellers’ market if you are CAMHS professional—as you know, we are very short of them—so why would you work in this when you could work somewhere else?

Lord Isaac: I certainly understand that, but can I ask one final question? You gave some examples earlier of anxieties and the chilling effect. Those examples still have to meet the test of being abusive and causing serious harm. Do you think that all the examples that you gave meet that threshold?

Baroness Cass: Staff have given me some real, practical examples. A mother says in relation to a child who is a biological male and now a trans female that the father said, “I couldn’t have tolerated him being gay. I’d have beaten it out of him.” If you have a discussion with that young person about whether things might have been different if they had not grown up in that environment, that could be very triggering and traumatising for the child. It may meet the threshold of impacting their functional—a lot of the children and young people being seen are not functioning very well anyway. Those are the sorts of judgments that—

Lord Isaac: Lots of statements are being made. You have made the point about the debate being weaponised—

Baroness Cass: On both sides.

Lord Isaac: On various sides. Do you agree that some of those do not meet the threshold?

Baroness Cass: I think the problem is not whether something would meet the threshold, but whether somebody would still report the individual, with everything that goes with that—which is highly problematic.

Lord Isaac: I understand.

Chair: There will be questions from Baroness Browning and then Olivia Bailey. Professor Leng, I am very sorry that you have been overlooked so far. May I ask the two questioners to put their questions and then come back to you for any comment? Is that all right?

Professor Leng indicated assent.

Baroness Browning: I should perhaps declare that Baroness Cass and I know each other, but not in this context. We have regular conversations, mainly about autism—

Baroness Cass: And vaccinations.

Baroness Browning: Yes. I declare that now.

The Cass review, on page 150, talks about exploratory therapy. That has come up in the evidence we have taken, particularly as far as clinicians and professionals are concerned. But your report stated: “The role of psychological therapies…has been overshadowed by an unhelpfully polarised debate around conversion practices. Terms such as ‘affirmative’”—which we have heard a lot about—“and ‘exploratory’ approaches have been weaponised”. Now that you have read the Bill and seen the language used in it and the explanatory notes on it, are you still concerned about that?

Baroness Cass: Yes, I am still concerned, because unfortunately the polarisation is worse now, I think, than when I first came out with my report. We had a honeymoon period after my interim report, when things settled down a bit, and then the environment became more hostile, on all sides. It is obviously profoundly unhelpful having—I am not sure whether I am allowed to say this in a Committee—President Trump, on the other side of the pond, being very aggressive towards trans people. That pushes the polarisation even more.

It is very clear that there are still malign actors on both sides, but there are those who feel that the exploratory approach that I describe in my report is conversion therapy. That is what they label it as. There is definitely a wish to push back on the changes that have come about because of my review, and this is one way to potentially undermine it. That is troublesome. I do not know how we get out of that problem.

Olivia Bailey: I should declare just for the record that—as you have mentioned, Baroness Cass—we met when I was a Minister, during the creation of this legislation.

I want to return to some of the themes that Lord Isaac has explored with you. In particular, you said that your concerns about the Bill were, on the healthcare exemption—please correct me if I get any of this wrong—that there is not enough clarity on what below an acceptable level is and you fear that people will be subjected to vexatious complaints. Then, on parents, you mentioned some examples, such as a parent not using pronouns or not being willing to buy a breast binder, as reasons why they might be affected by this legislation. Subject to further clarification from Lord Isaac, we have then established that you accept that people’s perception of the law is not the same as what is actually in the law.

If you accept, as you just have, that you think there should be a ban on abusive conversion therapy, would clarity on both those points for parents and healthcare providers satisfy you? For example, for healthcare providers, guidance on what is below an acceptable level could be useful, and perhaps some sort of provision around protecting against vexatious complaints. Would that satisfy you and enable you to continue to support a ban on the abuse of LGBT people?

Baroness Cass: The devil is in the detail; I would need to see the text of it. Let me give you an example of what happened to a parent. I ran listening sessions through my review, so anyone could talk to me if they had what I considered to be skin in the game. In other words, they had to be a young person, a young adult, a family or carer, or a professional working in some respect in the field, which could have been health, education or social care, but I was not prepared to hear from people who came from a purely ideological stance.

This is one of many stories, but a mother was in an abusive relationship with her husband. The daughter was going through anorexia and then had some medically unexplained symptoms. The mother and father broke up, and the father left the family home. The daughter then began identifying as trans male. The father, who was still in contact, was initially hugely negative about that. The mother tried to be supportive but was doing what I spoke about—not using the other name and not using the pronouns that the young person wanted. The father, who was still very angry with his wife, or was very acrimonious, reported her to social services for not supporting this young person’s gender transition. At the time she spoke to me, she was 11 months into an investigation by social services, and the younger brother had developed bed-wetting and was no longer going to school.

I think that was the father being vexatious, but he could now, on this, just as easily report her to the police, for example. People who are angry have the potential to do the wrong thing, and it is about how we try to guard against that. Believe me, as I said, I have been trying to think of forms of words on this whole issue for a number of years, but the devil is always in the detail.

Chair: I think the question, if I can put it this way, is whether the Bill would be improved if there were a provision for statutory guidance. Obviously the devil is in the detail, but we do not have that in the Bill at the moment. Would the Bill be improved if it were there?

Baroness Cass: Potentially, yes. As I say, I cannot think how you would frame it, but one would have to look at it. There is always room for improvement. When I spoke to the current Minister, he was really clear that everyone is looking to improve the Bill, but it is very hard to prejudge changes that we have not yet seen.

Chair: Yes, of course. Professor Leng, do you have any comment to make on what you have heard so far?

Professor Leng: I think it is really important to remember that the scenarios we are probably considering are not people who are absolutely clear about what they want; it is those who are distressed and unhappy and need to have open, honest conversations in the healthcare scenario, so let’s focus on that. We therefore need clinicians who feel absolutely empowered to have open, honest conversations, where they are asking questions, they are safe to explore and they are not directing an outcome.

We already see a lot of defensive medicine being practised in this country. Sadly, it has changed a lot since I qualified. I worry that the breadth of this definition at the moment encompasses a conversation that might, albeit that it was exploratory, upset someone because of the circumstance that they are in.

I think we will see clinicians shying away from having those conversations that people need, and—knowing the way the world works these days—people will go online to find answers. We know there are abusive practices happening online, and it may well make that scenario worse. I am not sure whether the Bill is intended to encapsulate what happens online, but that is a place where abusive conversion practices are more likely to happen than in healthcare, which is a highly regulated environment.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: A lot of what I was going to ask has already been covered, but I am very struck by the language that you have been using: “weaponising”, “hostile”, “malign actors”. Just to summarise what I think you have said: in this field, even if we say that statutory guidance must be given, there will be a real chilling effect from the very fact of passing legislation. Is that your real worry?

Baroness Cass: Yes, I am afraid so. This is such an extraordinary area that during the course of my review I could not get people on opposite ends of the spectrum into a room together; it just was not possible. There is so much anger and distrust on both sides that it is not feasible. Dr Hutchinson, whom you have heard from before, has had to have security with her when she has given talks. That is not normal, in any area of medicine.

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: No. Are you also concerned that it might have a chilling effect not just on the provision of healthcare but on whistleblowing, which was relied on in the Tavistock case, for example?

Baroness Cass: Yes, and I am sure members of the Committee will be aware of what happened in Sarah Redvers’s case in Brighton. Are people aware of that?

Baroness Prentis of Banbury: Could you summarise quickly?

Baroness Cass: Yes. This was a GP called Sam Hall, who was giving out both puberty blockers and masculinising and feminising hormones in a way which an investigation has demonstrated to have been extremely dangerous. Dr Redvers was the safeguarding GP for the area. People came to her with concerns. She raised those concerns—

Chair: May I interrupt you? Is this sub judice at the moment?

Baroness Cass: What I am saying is in the public domain.

Chair: It is in the public domain—okay. We have to be very careful not to comment on something that is sub judice, so do be careful with what you are saying.

Baroness Cass: Okay. Anyway, the upshot was that Dr Redvers ended up having to leave her job, and the current case in point is an employment tribunal that has been brought by Dr Redvers as the whistleblower.

Chair: We must leave it there, I think. I call Baroness Browning—sorry, Baroness Brown.

Baroness Brown of Silvertown: Brown, yes. I did not get the extra “ing”: we obviously couldn’t afford it.

Can I ask whether you think there are any risks in our not passing something now that criminalises conversion therapy? Do you feel that we would leave people unprotected? Having gone this far, as long as the Bill tightens up on the areas that you have spoken about, do you feel that it might be worrisome not to proceed to the end and pass it?

Baroness Cass: Yes. This is the game that is medicine as well: what is the greater risk, and how do you evaluate it? Medicine is a game of risk reduction. We can never eliminate risk, so the question is whether the risks are greater from passing the Bill or from not passing it. It is a really difficult one to call.

I think the other thing is that although this is a Bill that covers the whole LGBT community, the complexities are greater for trans-identifying children and young people than for LGB people. It is that particular part of it that is so complex. We know that some children with gender dysphoria or gender incongruence or who are trans-identifying—however you want to term it—will continue to be gender-incongruent into adulthood. For them, a medical and/or surgical transition may be the best outcome. But we know that many young people express psychological and physical distress through gender dysphoria and that if you prematurely medicalise them, that is a huge price to pay; whereas, whatever the risks of medical treatment, if you have a long-standing trans identity, the risks and side effects are trivial compared with the pain of not being able to live in your identified gender. That is what we are balancing. It is much more complicated for that group of young people.

Danny Beales: Thank you, Baroness Cass, for your evidence. You are describing the challenges that people currently experience in a contested space and a number of the issues that health professionals are already weighing up and experiencing. Is it your view that any Bill including healthcare professionals that does not have a kind of universal opt-out would cause concerns? Are you suggesting that there needs to just be a universal exclusion for healthcare professionals from criminalisation through conversion practices?

Baroness Cass: Well, that is one way forward, which would also have pros and cons. We would still have the question of what constitutes a healthcare professional. The other way of doing it would be to accredit centres or organisations. So I don’t know. Nothing is without risk, is it?

Lord Isaac: Baroness Cass, I thought your amendment was very helpful, because in it you tackle some of these things. I can see that you are trying to put forward various ideas. The carve-out that you talk about does not apply to conduct that involves physical force, threats or coercion. I think that that allays some of the fears that we are talking about today. I thought that was helpful. Do you have any further reflections on that?

Baroness Cass: Yes, that was what I was trying to do, although plainly those things are always going to be inappropriate under any circumstance.

Chair: I will call Naz Shah next. After she has put her question, I am going to ask you, Professor Leng, whether you have any comments. I am very conscious that you have not been contributing very much, but it is not because we do not want to hear from you. I will ask Nas Shah to put her question first, and then we will come back to you.

Naz Shah: My question is to both of you. The Cass report says: “Parents and families need support and advice about how best to support their children in a balanced and nonjudgemental way.” I take it that we all agree with that. Do you believe that this Bill would help to achieve that? Specifically, how concerned are you about the prospect of private legal action under the draft Bill? Do you think that there should be any changes there?

Baroness Cass: Yes, I do think that private legal action is a risk, because it is not at all clear how that bar would be set. Obviously it will be difficult enough going through the CPS, but if you can go direct to a private prosecution, I think that that is a further risk.

On your point about parents getting balanced advice, the challenge that parents face is that at the moment they still do not get very rapid access to medical care, so they are having to make complex and difficult judgments. As the Bill currently stands, it is very hard for parents to avoid potentially falling foul of its provisions. Lord Macdonald has already raised the point about it being unclear to parents whether or not they are crossing a line.

Professor Leng: You asked Baroness Cass whether there should be a universal opt-out for healthcare. We agree that there is a balance of risks here. My view is that the risk is probably greater that people will not get the care and support that they need, unless there is a universal opt-out, say for regulated healthcare settings. For me, that would probably make sense. We know what has happened, dare I mention it, in relation to people who are reaching the end of life and are struggling to have conversations with healthcare practitioners about not wanting to eat or drink, say: the clinician will just shut down that conversation because they are afraid of being caught up in helping somebody to die. We have seen that happen elsewhere—that is why I am bringing in that example—and I fear it would happen here.

Regardless of that, I think a good guideline would really help to reassure practitioners. We know that that happens in other areas, and that if they are required to defend their practice, it really helps if they have followed a guideline and have had clear training and accreditation in the field. Regardless of whether there is a universal opt-out, those professional guidelines would really help.

Chair: What do you mean by “universal opt-out”?

Professor Leng: At the moment, the draft Bill says that it “is not a conversion practice unless the person acts in a way that falls far below the standards reasonably expected of a person in their position.”

Chair: So it would be qualified by a reference to the standard. I wondered whether you were just saying that they should be completely exempt. That would be taking things long-distance. Are you suggesting that?

Professor Leng: We need to bear it in mind that we have to think about what problem we are intending to resolve. Most of the problems here are not in the healthcare setting; it does happen sometimes, but it is rare and rogue, and there are already ways of dealing with healthcare professionals who cross that line. Conversion practices are banned, if you like—I am using that word informally—by NHS England, and are not recommended by the colleges. It is not the setting that is most at risk here, by any means. That is why I mentioned the balance of risk: I expect that having it in the Bill will increase the risk to the people who need help. That is what we need to think about and keep at the centre.

Naz Shah: My final question is to you in particular, Baroness Cass. In response to Lord Hope, you said that the definition was not clear enough. There has been plenty of media coverage—it was even in yesterday’s report—saying that you supported the draft Bill originally. Given what you said earlier, I want to be really clear. I know we all want to end abusive, coercive conversion practice. Do you support this Bill as drafted?

Baroness Cass: No. As I said, I was trying to be as helpful as I could to the then Minister. I had said that I would not comment until I had seen it. It was sent to me an hour before they wanted a press release, and 15 minutes before I walked on a platform to give a talk, so I tried to give quotes based on the principles that I had been told about, but without seeing the Bill as drafted. Maybe I should not have done that, but, as I said, I am supportive of trying to do something about conversion practices.

None the less, having seen the Bill, having spoken to the many families who have emailed me and having spoken to my colleagues, I cannot support the Bill in its current form.

Sir Jeremy Wright: I wonder whether it might be helpful to unpack the structure of the Bill a little. We have talked about the healthcare exemption. The stage at which the Bill asks us to consider whether a conversion practice is happening is the point at which the healthcare exemption operates, is it not? At that point, if you meet the requirements of that part of the Bill, you are lifted out of consideration under any further stages.

I think what you are saying to us is that you are concerned that the Bill is drafted to require that, in order to benefit from the healthcare exemption, someone must not be acting “in a way that falls far below the standards reasonably expected of a person in their position.” You are concerned that that might be problematic. Can we park that for a minute and think about the other stages? If we go to the last of them—the question of whether “serious harm” or “serious alarm or distress” is caused—I think, Baroness Cass, you were saying earlier that that is not a high enough hurdle. I see that you are nodding, Professor Leng; I will give you the chance to agree with that, if you would like, but I will take the nod as assent.

The other hurdle is that the behaviour is abusive. If you do not qualify for protection under the healthcare exemption, because it is considered that your actions fall far below the standards expected of someone in your position—if the Chair lets me, I will come back to some of those questions in a minute—you then face the question whether your behaviour was abusive, in the terms of the Act. Did it cause alarm, distress or harm? You have said already that you do not think that the last of those is a big enough hurdle. Do you think that abusiveness is a big enough hurdle, as it is defined in the Bill at the moment?

Baroness Cass: I am not clear how abuse, in this setting, is defined. It is defined as the serious harm, which is alarm, distress and so on. It does not seem to be defined separately from that.

The other thing that one has to be really sensitive to is that there are two people in the room: the clinician and the young person. When people walk out of a room, they have a very different perception of what happened. We have all the same issues as in rape, for example, about what intent is. I think it is very difficult to be absolutely clear about whether what the clinician actually said has caused that distress, or whether it has thrown up for the young person some kind of deep-seated distress and they therefore perceive that that was the fault of the therapist. Does that make sense?

Sir Jeremy Wright: It does, and of course the distress does not have to be intentional, as the Bill is drafted.

Baroness Cass: No.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Would it help if it did have to be intentional, or at least in circumstances where the person causing the distress should have realised that they would do so?

Baroness Cass: Realising that a discussion is going to be painful is probably not a reason why a healthcare professional does not have that conversation. Otherwise, you cannot make progress in trying to address some things. If a young person, for example, has been sexually abused, any discussion about it is going to be incredibly triggering, but you cannot therapeutically work through it without any reference to it. That is the problem.

I am not suggesting this as a solution, but maybe we need to find some way to think very specifically about young gender-questioning people, because that is the whole thrust of the problem. When you evolve your sexual orientation—some people are very clear about their sexual orientation from very young; for some people it takes longer and more experimentation—it is not something that can be a way that you feel purely as a result of a whole series of other, kind of, traumas. For example, I spoke frequently to parents where the young person had gone through a significant mental breakdown, was on the autistic spectrum, had gone through eating disorders, had gone through cutting or had gone through medically unexplained symptoms and then presented with gender distress. All those things were a function of something else that was causing that young person to be very distressed. That is a very different situation from that of somebody who works their sexual orientation out over a period.

We are treating as a stable belief something that is both developmental and, in some cases, part of a completely different presentation. That is what makes this so difficult. I am not sure if I am being clear about that.

Sir Jeremy Wright: No, you are. I think that what you are saying is that it is intrinsically distressing to discuss, perhaps seriously so.

Baroness Cass indicated assent.

Sir Jeremy Wright: Professor Leng, do you have anything to add?

Professor Leng: I would just echo that these things are intrinsically difficult to discuss, and intrinsically difficult for parents to discuss. They are also upset. I have two friends whose daughters have transitioned from male to female, and I saw through them how they actually were going through a grieving process.

Going back to what we need here, we need supportive environments where all concerned can have sensitive conversations. I like evidence; you can tell that from my background. You look for reviews in this field, and you get reviews of things like aversion therapy, hypnosis, spinal cord cauterisation—all sorts of awful things—and those things are absolutely banned. But we have to be very careful when it comes into the context of having conversations in a healthcare supportive environment, because that is not what we want to criminalise; that is what we want to support.

Baroness Cass: I can read you a quote from a parent, if that is helpful. This is from somebody whose daughter identified as transgender and then gradually desisted over time. She said, “We all experienced significant distress and were impaired in our day-to-day functioning. It’s a very stressful experience to have a beloved child suddenly insist you call them a different name and use different pronouns, when they themselves are self-harming and pleading for hormones, binders and breast surgery.” This young lady then overdosed and the parents were investigated by social services. She said, “I felt bullied and experienced the encounters with education and healthcare providers as the psychological abduction of my child.” Yet some years later, when the young person desisted, she said that what she saw as her mother not understanding her and causing her pain and distress, she now sees as having protected her.

Of course, there are many cases that will come out differently, and the young person may go on to have a stable trans identity and a very successful life as a trans adult, but these are the ones that are the challenge.
