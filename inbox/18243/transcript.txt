Examination of witnesses

Michael Shanks and Bill Jones.

The Chair: Good morning, and welcome to the Lords Environment and Climate Change Committee. Today is the final session of our inquiry into Dunkelflaute weather events and the challenges that they present to the Government’s ambition to deliver the clean power action plan by 2030.

I would like to warmly welcome the right honourable Michael Shanks MP, Minister for Energy, together with his DESNZ colleague Bill Jones, deputy director for future energy security. Thank you very much for making the time to be with us today.

Before we start, may I remind everyone that the session is webcast live on Parliament TV and that a transcript will be taken and made public. Witnesses will have an opportunity to review the script and, if necessary, make very minor amendments. Members are reminded that they should declare any relevant interests the first time they speak. I will take the opportunity here to say that I am a director of Peers for the Planet.

We are a little bit late in starting, so we will move straight to the first question. Minister, in written evidence from DESNZ, we are told that the majority of capacity procured through the capacity market can be expected to generate or manage demand throughout a Dunkelflaute period, in combination with other non-capacity-market eligible capacity. In that statement, we can infer that the Government have some idea of how they are defining a Dunkelflaute period. Could you share that definition with us?

Michael Shanks: Good morning. It is good to be at this end of the building. Thank you for inviting me along. You have had some very detailed evidence. I read the session that you had with the Met Office, for example, on scientific definitions, and there is a mixed picture of exactly how you would define it. We characterise it as a period of low wind and low sun, usually correlating with a winter period where there might be an increased expectation of demand.

In truth, it is just one of a number of weather and climate events that the National Energy System Operator has to consider in its planning for the system. Although we recognise that it is an event that requires thinking on how the system will run, it is part of a long list of other things in which the capacity market exists to make sure that we are constantly able to meet the demand that the system needs.

The Chair: What demand are you meeting?

Michael Shanks: Every single day, we have to meet the peak demand on the system.

The Chair: Let us just talk about these high-risk periods of Dunkelflaute. We really want to try to understand what level of capacity you think we need. You seem to say here that you understand fully that you can meet those risks, yet we do not understand what those risks are. We have had lots of evidence to suggest that this uncertainty is not helpful to people trying to make decisions in mitigating that risk.

Michael Shanks: It is one of a number of challenges. The system operates in a business-as-usual way when weather events are as predicted, but, on any given day, the weather can change. Gas terminals could be offline for any particular reason. There are a whole series of things that mean that the system operates in different ways every single day. The capacity market exists to make sure that we have the capacity that we can deploy whenever there are differences in generation or, indeed, peaks in demand that perhaps are not the normal demand that the system would come under.

Although I completely understand why, especially in an inquiry focused on Dunkelflaute, you would want clear definitions of that, in truth I do not think that anyone has an absolutely clear definition. It is just one of many things that the system has to be able to respond to on any given day.

The Chair: Dr Emily Wallace from the Met Office stated that a three-week period of wind suppressed to 20% of its usual capacity would be an infrequent, one-in-40-year event. Is that something that the system, as it currently stands, will be able to meet?

Michael Shanks: Again, the system is set up to meet any strains that are put on it at any point in time. That is why we have redundancies built into it. There are a lot of different definitions in terms of how long a period of Dunkelflaute could last, what the impact would be in other parts of Europe in terms of the impact on interconnectors, and how other parts of the generation fleet can respond to it. Of course, we are not wholly reliant on renewables, so it is not entirely affected in that way.

There is, though, also a greater understanding of what the impact of climate change might be on Dunkelflaute in the future. There is more research being done on that at the moment, but, in truth, the way in which the Government consider the system is that it is one of many things that we have to consider in terms of the strain put on the system. The system has to be able to respond to that, but, equally, to other periods of outages. If we think of the summer that we have just had, significant heatwaves put pressure on the system as well. There is a whole range of things that we consider.

The Chair: It would really help to understand the additional work that the Government are undertaking to clarify the risk of climate change in this very uncertain series of events that lead to a Dunkelflaute. Can you shed some light on what extra work is being carried out?

Michael Shanks: We have an interest in it. NESO, which I know you have had evidence from, has said that it has an interest in further research, but this is work that is being taken on by academics and others looking into the longer-term trends in this. It is not work the Government are directly looking at, but we remain in close contact with the Met Office and with others to try to understand this.

Part of the modelling that NESO uses—and I know that you had the chief engineer here to give evidence to you—is looking backwards at all of the evidence of weather patterns and others that can help build the models for the future, but the system is also built in with redundancies, so that, if there are unexpected periods of Dunkelflaute or other weather events, it can respond to those in real time.

The Chair: What the Government are trying to achieve with the clean power action plan and delivering the volume of renewables that that will entail by 2030 is extremely ambitious and risky. Some of those risks are magnified by weather events such as this. We want to be absolutely certain that the Government recognise those extreme risks and are really putting their shoulder to the wheel to make sure that the lights do not go out. Can we be absolutely certain that the Government are fully cognisant of the risks and that the lights will stay on?

Michael Shanks: First of all, we have one of the most resilient energy systems anywhere in the world. We have had renewables on our system for decades now, and that resilience has not changed. I would challenge the idea that there is an extreme risk of deploying renewables. There are many countries around the world that have relied on renewables as a core part of their energy mix for a long time.

The Chair: There is an extreme risk when we have events such as these, which will test the system.

Michael Shanks: Testing the system is different from extreme risk. I would not characterise it in that way.

The Chair: I did not for one minute say that it is extremely risky to go for renewables. For the sake of clarity, I fully support the Government’s ambitions.

Michael Shanks: I am grateful for that. There is a mix of technologies. We were very clear that the decarbonisation of the power system by 2030, which is a hugely stretching ambition, was not just about deploying renewables. It was about building new nuclear far more quickly as a country than we have been able to in the past. It was also about bringing forward storage, which we have not kept up pace with. We have just announced the first long-duration energy storage in 40 years. We should have been building that alongside the renewables that we have been building so that we can store more of it. It is also about how we use the system much more smartly, so that we have flexibility built into it as well.

In terms of the risk point, though, to come to your question, this is not saying that one single technology should ever make up our energy mix. We do not hold that view at all. Gas will continue to play a role in a strategic capacity, and there is resilience built into the plans for clean power.

The Chair: Do you have anything to add, Bill Jones?

Bill Jones: No. I think that that sets out the prospect very clearly. We need a mix of technologies to address this challenge.

Lord Krebs: Thank you very much, Minister, for coming to see us. We had a session with Ofgem and NESO last week. Although this is an inquiry about keeping the lights on, I am afraid that Ofgem and NESO rather left us in the dark, in the sense that, although they said that the Government are due to make a decision on a pathway for the strategic energy plan by the end of the year, they were not able to shed any light on what things would look like.

They were not able to tell us which technology will be in the plan, what scale it will be, by when it will be ready, where it is going to be situated, or whatever infrastructure is required. I am hoping that you might be able to tell us a little bit more about this strategic spatial energy plan in terms of the technologies and the investment that will be required to deliver it.

Michael Shanks: I can give some context as to why we are going through the process of a strategic spatial energy plan, and outline the process going forward. The pathway that will be chosen will be consulted on. The alternative pathways that have been presented to Government will form part of the environmental assessment that will be published as well, so there will be transparency in what those pathway options are.

We co-commissioned, alongside the Scottish and Welsh Governments, the first ever strategic plan for the country, recognising that, while the renewables that we have built in the system are hugely welcome, we should have been building them more coherently, and we should have done that 20 years ago, or in any of the intervening years since, so that we are not paying huge costs to connect those projects after the fact. That is the purpose of the plan.

It is about a range of technologies, and some of the discussion on the strategic spatial energy plan has come to the view that we will make black and white choices about offshore wind or SMRs. That is not how we are going to reach a conclusion on this, but it is about saying that there is a pathway that involves either a significant amount of onshore solar and wind, or a significant amount of offshore, or a balance of the two, and other technologies built into that mix as well.

The decision will be made on the basis of the cost of the pathway, first and foremost, while also ensuring that we have the industrial advantage that comes from some of those opportunities, and that it strategically makes sense in terms of land use across the country as well.

Every single one of the pathways, as I hope would be taken as a given, conforms to our security of supply standards, so every single one will meet all the supply needs of the country as well as the legally binding carbon budgets of the country. All six follow that pathway. The choices are individually within that in terms of what the mix of technologies will be.

Lord Krebs: Thank you for that. Is there an urgency in your mind about this? There needs to be some long-term investment by the private sector, and possibly by the Government as well. While there is uncertainty, people are not going to invest in particular technologies. What is your comment about the urgency to get this resolved so that people can get on with delivering the infrastructure that is needed?

Michael Shanks: It is a hugely important point. The reason why we announced the clean power action plan two and a half years ago was partly to give that confidence to industry that, after a failed offshore wind auction under the last Government, we were committed to this, so there could be confidence in that investment. That has happened. We have seen more than £110 billion of private investment in clean power since we came into government. The long-term future, I understand, is really important, not least because we want to build the supply chains here, which requires that pipeline of projects to work.

At the same time, though, we want to make sure that we get this right, because the danger to date has been quite an ad hoc approach to building renewables across the country. I am a Scottish Member of Parliament. I live next to two of Europe’s biggest onshore wind farms. They are fantastic pieces of infrastructure, but, if we were logically designing a system, we may not have placed quite so many wind farms quite so far away from centres of demand. The whole point of this plan is to look more coherently at the opportunity across the country.

The pace point is absolutely crucial, but it is equally about making sure that we get it right for this first plan. It will then be an iterative process. Although this takes us to 2050 in broad terms, it is not a one plan in 2027 that we never revise. It clearly will be revised regularly, but it gives us some indication to allow us to then do the centralised strategic network plan, or CSNP—there are always acronyms in this business—which is how we try to bring down the cost of building network infrastructure so that it is more coherently built to meet the future pathways of our energy system.

Lord Krebs: If I am in charge of a company that is providing one of the technologies that could support ultra-long-duration energy storage, when will I know that I can press go and that you have given the clear signal that this is a technology that the Government will adopt?

Michael Shanks: In terms of the timeline for the SSEP, the Secretary of State will make a decision in the coming months, which will be consulted on early in 2027. We want to do that in partnership with the Scottish and Welsh Governments, which we co-convened this with. That process will take us to some point in 2027, and the final decision will be at the end of 2027. In the intervening time, we are continuing to do everything that we can around individual technologies. The energy system is not going to stop for a year.

On long-duration energy storage, gas is probably what you are referencing in that specific example. We have said that we will respond to the consultation on security of supply for gas early next year to give some confidence to the gas storage question. For example, on long-duration electricity storage, Ofgem will soon announce its final decisions on almost seven gigawatts of long-duration storage. All of that will continue. This is about the longer-term future pathways for energy in the country.

The Chair: I wonder if I can press on the evidence that we heard from Ofgem and NESO last week. They were unable to confirm which technologies would be in the SSEP. However, Julian Leslie from NESO mentioned that it was an iterative plan that would be updated every three years to account for technical innovation. This would suggest that there may be an emphasis on proven technologies in the first instance. That is really the question that I want to put to you. Will that be the case? Is it that proven technologies, such as grid-scale batteries, pump storage or hydro interconnectors, have greater certainty already?

Michael Shanks: Yes, in some ways, but we also are supportive of technologies that are coming forward and that need additional support. For example, there will be a choice to be made about the amount of floating offshore wind that we think will be in a future pathway, but there is no doubt that, as a country, we want to see some floating offshore wind, so we will work on a way to support the industrial opportunity around that. Established technologies are clearly crucial to make sure that we have confidence that things will be built, but also that the cost curve is at the lower end, because affordability is the Government’s number one priority.

In terms of storage, the pathways will make an overall choice around how much long-duration storage we need. They will not go into the detail of the technologies, because that is the process that Ofgem is running in the cap and floor scheme.

The Chair: Long duration is different from ultra-long duration. The plan would be updated every three years. Will it be the case that technologies such as hydrogen storage, carbon capture and storage or compressed air energy storage will be having to wait for three-year periods?

Michael Shanks: No. This is where I just want to add a bit of clarification about the strategic spatial energy plan. This is a reflection on the past two years in which this work has been under way. The sense has been given that it is going to be an extremely definitive plan to 2050, written in black and white, that never changes, and that nothing that is not on it will be delivered. That is not how the SSEP will work.

It is to give us a broad design for what the future system looks like, so that we can build the grid that is most relevant to meet that demand. Projects will come forward. If we think of nuclear, we are really interested in advanced nuclear projects from the private sector. It is not for Government to decide whether there is private investment interested in those projects. It is our job to judge the safety and the planning, and then to allow them to carry on. Whether those are in the SSEP or not, we will continue to support them, and, likewise, emerging technologies that come out in long‑duration and ultra-long-duration storage, which is a hugely interesting area. If we can crack the science and the development behind them, we should be deploying as much as we can.

The Chair: You say that you have received the final pathways options report from NESO and are in the process of selecting one of them. How many pathways are there? What are the methodology and assumptions that lie behind them?

Michael Shanks: There are six pathways that have been presented to the department. The methodology document is publicly available, including all of the detail in the consultation exercises that NESO has gone through. There is a significant amount of information within all of that.

What NESO has presented to us is its view, as the system operator, of a variety of pathways, their cost calculations, and the impacts on land use, for example. It is for Government to then take that information and decide on the wider impacts. There are questions about how much land use we want for onshore solar, for example, versus offshore wind. That is a political choice. There are questions about supply chain benefits that might come from offshore wind versus solar that could be built in the UK, with jobs and economic opportunity. There is a question about cost in terms of how much we are willing to pay versus all the other trade-offs.

Those are all political choices. As the Prime Minister has emphasised recently, Government are in charge of energy policy in the country. The role of NESO and Ofgem is to advise and to regulate, but Government will decide what the future system looks like in the UK.

The Chair: When will we have greater clarity on the SSEP? All the evidence that we have heard so far says that everyone is operating in the dark, as Lord Krebs has said. When will we have greater clarity? Are investors and stakeholders expected to wait until the end of next year?

Michael Shanks: No. Everyone will see publicly the pathway that the Secretary of State has chosen early next year. The expectation is that that will be in the first quarter of next year. In the environmental assessment that goes with that, the other pathways will also become clear, so there will be transparency on the alternative options. It is for NESO to do that consultation, but the Government will have an explanation that goes alongside that as to why we have chosen that pathway. Importantly, there is then the opportunity for industry and the public to add their views to this, and we will come to a final decision next year.

I would just reiterate the point on investor certainty, which is so important. This is not a binary choice that we are making. We have to build an energy system with a mix of technologies. We are open to investment in a whole range of areas, and we continue to work right across industry to make those projects happen.

Lord Redwood: Minister, I welcome the Chancellor’s recent statement that it makes no sense to keep adding renewables if the grid capacity is not there to deliver it to customers. I welcome your hint today that we already have an imbalance problem, with an excess of wind power in Scotland, and cost and supply difficulties resulting.

I wondered if you could just give us some background on where you and the department are in solving that grid blockage problem and how much delay and extra cost is going to be entailed in putting in synchronous condensers and grid-forming inverters, for example, and the megalink needed to Scotland with pylon and cable capacity.

Michael Shanks: It is a really important question. It is the most pressing thing in my in-tray as a Minister at the moment. The grid is hugely resilient but is not where it should be. If we are honest, we should have woken up decades ago to the fact that the grid was not being invested in at the scale that it should have been.

That is not just because we are moving to renewables. There are hugely important economic growth opportunities on the demand side that cannot connect and are given dates a decade in advance, which is an absolute nightmare for any investor confidence in the UK. It is becoming increasingly complex to manage the grid, because we are trying to upgrade grid that was largely built in the 1950s and 1960s, alongside connecting many more things into it.

It is a collective failure of Governments over decades to build what is probably the most important national asset that we have as a country. The grid is a phenomenal piece of engineering, but it has been largely ignored for decades, and that is a failure.

We are now trying to grip two things that are crucial. One is the connections reform process, which has been painful but really important. When we came into government, more than 700 gigawatts was waiting to connect to the national grid, which was an absurd amount of projects that had just been allowed to grow and grow. We have reformed that queue and cleared out 300 gigawatts. That allows projects that are ready and strategically aligned to connect. That is part of the solution.

The bigger thing is upgrading the grid itself, which will be a huge piece of investment. We have been really open with people. If you think back to the Budget last year, we took £150 of costs away from bills and into the Treasury. People did not see £150 come off their bills, because we had to invest in the grid.

That was a politically difficult thing to do, frankly, when we would have liked to bring people’s bills down, but, if we do not fix the grid now, we will end up, a decade or more from now, in an even worse position than we are now, losing out on economic opportunities, but, crucially, spending billions of pounds on constraining wind and turning up gas, which is a disgraceful way for us to be operating a system. We need to build the grid. We need to invest now. We recognise that that comes with a cost, but there is, as with all of this, an even greater cost if we do not act now.

Lord Redwood: Can you give us any indication on cost on bills in 2030 and on delays in all this?

Michael Shanks: I cannot give you an exact figure to 2030. Ofgem will be able to give you its modelling. The reason that it is a balancing act here is that there is an investment cost to build the grid. For example, if we build Sea Link and Norwich to Tilbury, it will come at a significant cost to consumers, but the cost-benefit will outweigh the investment cost in building it, because of the constraints that we will reduce to bring cleaner, cheaper power to people’s homes. There is a trade-off with short-term investment in order to get to a reduction in constrained costs to bring down people’s bills.

Lord Redwood: Thank you very much. Can I give you my apologies? I have to disappear early because of the change of date, which would not fit in my diary, but thank you for coming.

Baroness Whitaker: Good morning, and thank you for coming in. Minister, you referred to the capacity market. We have heard that it does not adequately recognise or reward duration and availability of assets to deal with Dunkelflaute. Looking at your Clean Power 2030 Action Plan, you are clearly contemplating reforms, so will they take account of the security of supply benefits that ultra-long-duration storage and low-carbon dispatchable power could deliver, as well as enable innovation? In that connection, could you tell us what is happening with the Marine Energy Taskforce? What does it think should happen to the market?

Michael Shanks: Great. There are lots of really good questions in that. I might pass to Bill in a moment, who is Mr Capacity Market in the department and will speak specifically about that point.

First, we absolutely want to try to get to a place where the capacity market is using low-carbon options as much as possible, but the critical thing about the capacity market is dispatchability. Security of supply will always be the number one aim. We said that, even in the clean power scenario, gas would continue to play a role as a strategic capacity on the system because we recognise its inherent ability to be dispatchable, but the gas fleet is ageing, and so we want to see other technologies come forward.

Long-duration storage is a really critical part of that, but we have not been building it for a very long time. Some of the battery projects that are coming forward are now long-duration. They may have quicker build times. We are also keen to build more pumped hydro storage, but that is not going to happen overnight.

I will pass to Bill in a second on the capacity market, but just on the last point, I set up the Marine Energy Taskforce because I felt from industry that they were not getting a fair hearing from Government over many years. It is a technology that has been proven in many other countries, but is extremely expensive to build.

I wanted to try to get the evidence together to justify tidal or wave technologies that are more expensive as a capital outlay, but, if we look at countries around the world that have them, are often on the system after 70 or 80 years and continuing to generate day after day. I visited a number of hydro projects in the Scottish Highlands that are still generating with much the same technology as they were built with when we electrified the Highlands.

I am interested in the technology, but I have been really open with industry that the cost is prohibitive at the moment. Although we have supported some tidal projects to get to scale, we are going to have to work on how we bring down the cost of the capital outlay to begin with, and I am open to working with industry on that.

Baroness Whitaker: It is the mechanism to get these prototypes up for scaling that seems not to be sufficiently available, so I do not know whether you have any thoughts about that. Perhaps your office could let us know which other countries have managed marine energy.

Michael Shanks: I will. I know of a particularly good project in France, for example, which has been running for almost 18 years. We are supporting the EMEC in Orkney, for example, which is doing a lot of work on wave generation, but you are right that the scale-up point is the challenge. The technology is there at an innovation level, and we know that it works as a way of generating electricity, but scaling it up is a challenge. Tidal energy comes with issues in that, although it is a constant source of energy, it does not necessarily correlate with peak demand, so you still need storage attached to it. There are trade-offs, but I am very interested in looking at it as a technology. I do not know if Bill wants to add anything on the capacity market.

Baroness Whitaker: Could he add in, just finally, whether the Marine Energy Taskforce is preparing a report? I did hear that it was.

Michael Shanks: That is more me again. Yes, it is. I would need to check. I think it had its final meeting and is now drafting the report, but there may be one further meeting to happen. I am happy to write to the committee.

Bill Jones: The capacity market is GB’s primary mechanism for ensuring security of supply. It runs on a technology-neutral basis, where it seeks to buy the capacity that we need, whatever technology that might be, to ensure security of supply according to metrics that NESO develops as the system operator.

It is not the job of the capacity market to bring through new technology or more low-carbon technology, or things like such as that. It is about ensuring security of supply. The sorts of things that the Minister was talking about are where you bring forward new technologies. They are bespoke support mechanisms to develop a technology or to help it overcome barriers.

Baroness Whitaker: By that, do you mean contracts for difference?

Bill Jones: That could be one mechanism. For example, that is what is used in the context of offshore wind. It could be things such as business models to support hydrogen to power. There are a number of ways in which we address the needs of different technologies to try to bring if forward as a competitive technology in an auction process, which is how the capacity market runs. The capacity market does not decide which technology gets agreements under the capacity market. It is about who bids in and who is able to offer capacity that they can deliver to when required to meet a system stress event.

There is quite a detailed, complicated process that we go through in terms of annual auctions to make sure that we have enough capacity on the system to meet those requirements. In the context of Dunkelflaute, we are thinking about whether the capacity market delivers what I was going to call the types of technology, but which can respond to that long-duration event rather than, as it has traditionally been set up, to meet peak requirements.

This device was set up in 2014. We had, basically, a coal and gas system, so we did not have to worry about Dunkelflaute because the technologies that came through were able to do both: meet peak demand and also meet the Dunkelflaute challenge. Now, with a more renewables-based system, where we are facing a bigger challenge in the context of Dunkelflaute, we are going to have to think about whether the capacity market brings forward the right sort of technology that can respond to those sorts of events. We are thinking about that and contemplating how we might reform the capacity market to be able to meet those challenges.

Baroness Whitaker: We would love to see how you are going to reform it when you have decided, if that is within our timescale.

Bill Jones: We are actively thinking about it now. The nature of the capacity market process requires quite a lot of consultation. We need to get the views of industry that can contribute, and all that stuff. It is not a quick fix, but we are actively thinking about it. We will be thinking about bringing forward proposals for reform next year, which might be a process that takes a couple of years to develop. The other thing to remember is that it then takes three to four years before you see those new technologies come through, because the auctions for capacity are looking that far ahead.

Baroness Whitaker: Thank you very much. We will have to manage to postpone any Dunkelflaute.

Bill Jones: Dunkelflaute is already part of what NESO buys against, so it is not like it is a new challenge. It is one that we can see becoming more of a challenge because of the nature of the system, but it is not new. NESO projects its challenge in terms of how much capacity it thinks it needs to secure the system, and has been doing so for many years. It is a constant, iterative process, so each year it thinks about what the new challenges are and how it weighs what technologies can contribute, as well as what challenges it faces in terms of Dunkelflaute rather than peak demand. It is an ongoing process.

Baroness Rafferty: My question is on the auctions that you have referenced. What exactly are these? Is that the best mechanism to use to engage and secure supply?

Michael Shanks: From a policy point of view, there are two auctions. One is four years ahead, which allows us to give that longer-term procurement, and one comes a year ahead of when we expect the demand to come on, or when it should be available. It is a well-used process, if we think of the CfD auctions, for example, to try to identify the projects that come forward but at a price that is good for consumers.

Ultimately, to qualify for the capacity market, you have to meet all of the requirements for the system, so that everyone that participates is suitable for our needs. It then comes down to the cost that we can procure it at, and we have found that the auction is an effective way of doing that, not least because the generators bidding into it do not have any idea of what the alternative pricing structures are for others. It allows some competition within that and for us to be able to identify the cheapest price possible for consumers.

We keep the rules of the auction under review. We made some changes last year to the capacity market. We continue to do that to make sure that it works for consumers, and crucially, more than any other part of the energy system, the capacity market is about us always being able to meet the needs of the system.

The Chair: You will be looking at reforming the capacity market, you say. Can I ask over what timeframe?

Michael Shanks: We are open to looking at what shape the capacity market should take in the future, recognising that this mix of technologies is changing right now. We are not actively in a period of consultation on it, so we are looking at the policy development at the moment.

The Chair: The reason why I ask is that we have written evidence from the Renewables Investors Group. One of the issues that it raises is that, “For storage, the de-rating factor rises with the number of hours a site can run, but levels off at about 95-96% at around 9.5 hours. Storage that can run for longer gets no extra credit”. That does not reflect reality. Will the capacity market be reformed to reflect ultra-long-duration storage realities?
