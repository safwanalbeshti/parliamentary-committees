Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Fintan Slye and Kayte O’Neill.

Q1 Chair: Welcome to this morning’s session of the Energy Security and Net Zero Committee. We have a one-off session on the work of NESO and we welcome Kayte O’Neill, the chief operating officer and Fintan Slye, the chief executive. Fintan, I believe you would like to make some opening remarks so over to you please.

Fintan Slye: Thank you very much, Chair, and thank you for inviting us to the committee today. We appreciate the opportunity to provide evidence. I would like to start by acknowledging the publication last week of the independent report into whistleblower allegations made against NESO. While this investigation by Eversheds Sutherland (International) LLP did not substantiate any of the four allegations made against NESO, it did identify other organisational and cultural issues where we need to improve. The report made seven specific recommendations, all of which have been accepted by the board; acting on those findings and delivering those recommendations is a priority for me, the executive and the full board.

Last week also saw the interim report of the Ofgem-commissioned review into system and market operations during the extreme heat at the end of June. I do not want to prejudge the final report findings, which will come out later this year, but we know from the interim report that it was an exceptional period of extreme weather that placed significant stress on the electricity system in Great Britain, as well as on electricity systems right across Europe. Despite this, our operations and control room teams did a phenomenal job; no customer demand was disconnected and the frequency remained within required statutory limits at all times. I understand there is significant interest in these processes, and I am sure we will speak about them this morning. The board and executive team take all these matters very seriously.

Alongside these though, it is important to highlight key areas where we have made significant progress and delivered major outcomes since we were last in front of this Committee. We have advanced the once-in-a-generation reformation of the connections queue to our electricity system, and we have made significant progress around strategic energy planning, submitting the strategic spatial energy plan pathways to the Secretary of State and standing up the regional energy strategic planning process. We continue to operate one of the most reliable electricity systems in the world and have saved consumers over £1.2 billion in constraint costs over the last number of years.

Undoubtedly, we still have a huge amount of work to do and lessons to learn, but we will continue to make progress. I am absolutely confident that NESO, and most importantly, the great people who work for us tirelessly day in day out, will continue to deliver a secure electricity system today and help design a resilient energy system for tomorrow. In doing all this we will keep the interests of consumers absolutely front of mind, as it is for them that the system must deliver in the long run.

Q2 Chair: Thank you very much for your opening comments, which prompts a number of questions on its own. Given your admissions around the organisational and cultural challenges NESO faces, should you and your chair be significantly more visible in explaining publicly what NESO is doing, what your priorities are, and why things are happening? Do you agree that is one of the key lessons here, and do you intend to make such a change?

Fintan Slye: There is certainly a point around making sure that the work that NESO does is explained in a really transparent, objective and open way. We are a relatively young organisation, having only existed for just over two years, and we do not yet have the recognition that other organisations have.

Throughout the summer we were cautious not to prejudice the outcome of those two reviews by speaking too publicly about them. However, only two weeks ago I was interviewed on stage at the Energy UK industry conference. Also, I was interviewed by Rachel Millard from the Financial Times at the Financial Times conference, and I recently attended the Public Accounts Committee with colleagues from Ofgem and DESNZ. I would say increasing our visibility, presence and public explanation of our role is absolutely something we are looking to do.

Q3 Chair: I am not convinced it sounded as though you thought that was necessarily your role.

Fintan Slye: As chief executive that is absolutely my role, but this is a big organisation and I have colleagues, such as Kayte and others on the executive team, who all need to be out there representing us. However, as the chief executive of the organisation I take responsibility for that.

Q4 Chair: It has been described to me that the challenges of the energy transition are that the electrification of system means more grid generation and storage—the fastest change in a generation—all while keeping consumer bills down, bringing communities with us, and keeping our climate commitments. Assuming you accept that definition of the challenge, how well is NESO performing against that metric?

Fintan Slye: The challenge for the energy industry, and indeed for NESO, is to balance those three things: security of energy supply, affordability for consumers, and the transition to a sustainable energy system.

This is an energy transition across the industry. There are bits of it that we are leading on and bits of it that we are participating in. However, one of the big transitions that we are part of delivering is reformation of the connections queue; that is a once-in-a-generation change in how we think about connecting to the electricity system. We have progressed that; it is now progressing to plan, and people are getting the connection offers that they need to connect.

Q5 Chair: The engineers say it is an engineering challenge and if they are given the appropriate challenge they will meet it. Do you agree with that?

Fintan Slye: In terms of managing the increased complexity—

Q6 Chair: Yes, in bringing those three elements along together. The engineers say it is feasible; they just need to be tasked with doing so. Do you agree with that?

Fintan Slye: Absolutely, and that is what the engineers in NESO are doing in terms of trying to ensure that we keep the system secure while managing the increased complexity as we move from a fossil fuel-based system to a more renewable-based system. It is undoubtedly more complex. However, we have brilliant engineers right across the energy system who are absolutely dedicated to making sure that we marry those three things up.

Q7 Chair: One of the criticisms we hear—it came out somewhat in the reporting over the summer—is that, for some reason, there is resistance or delay in using the latest technology: for example, digitisation of the grid is where we get efficiencies from and it enables better use of demand flexibility. Is that a fair criticism: that NESO is not embracing technology and the use of AI as fast as it could?

Kayte O’Neill: Actually technology and the use of AI is hugely important to us in a number of places; we use it ourselves in our ways of working in the organisation and in the control room, but also in how we think about the value of data, digitalisation, AI, and technology enhancements across the sector, where our control room and our teams are connecting with market providers and market participants and the work that they are doing. I would say we have actually seen some really great progress.

We are nearing completion of a very significant technology change out programme in the control room. We have introduced new systems and new processes for colleagues, giving them much better visibility of what is going on in the system and much greater ability to optimise. That is within our own control room day to day as we balance the system.

We have also taken on a real leadership role in engaging with the industry on the value of being able to share data and information. We want to be able to do that in a real, standardised way, creating the potential for that data to be used, to think about how we plan the system for the very long term, how we operate markets to deliver what the control room needs, and then to be able to make use of that.

Q8 Chair: Thank you for that. I was looking at a pilot example that goes to what you were saying, Fintan, about curtailment costs. It is a pilot example of a heavy user of electricity being paid to use electricity during periods where you would otherwise be paying to curtail wind farm production. They have set up the system and storage to benefit from it, and yet they are not being allowed to use it because NESO never gives them the consent to do so.

Allied to that, it got me thinking: if this is something that NESO is looking at, why is it not being rolled out more quickly? Why are we not already there with encouraging heavy energy users more widely to benefit from this option of storing electricity, rather than paying to switch it off?

Fintan Slye: Obviously I do not know the specifics of the example that you quote, but there are maybe two things to highlight. First, the use of flexibility in a widespread sense, down to household level, was something that NESO pioneered through the ESO previously with the demand flexibility scheme. We have recently run a really significant further innovation trial with partners called CrowdFlex around how we build out and expand that.

On the subject of industrial and commercial consumers and how they do that, one part of that is what signals NESO gives; the other part of it is how the market rewards that type of flexibility. Really, NESO should just be stepping in as the residual balancer in the market, when in the first instance the market should resolve that. What we need to look at is how we make sure that the right signals and market incentives are there, so that the type of situation that you describe, where an industrial customer that has some storage assets and maybe has some flexible load can operate in a way where the value that they deliver back to the system is recognised, either in cheaper electricity, or a payment stream for that flexibility. We are working with Alexon as the market facilitator on that, and the flexibility commissioner who has been appointed, to look at all the mechanisms we can use to achieve that.

We are hugely supportive of enabling demand flexibility. We see it as part of the long-term solution. It was one of the first things listed in our clean power advice to Government back in 2024 around how to do that.

Q9 Chair: I suppose my question is, why are we not further advanced with it? You said this was something that happened before NESO came into existence, which was more than two years ago. When you were describing it, you also said that you recognise this is technologically feasible. It goes to my previous question about whether NESO is using technology in as advanced a way as it could; that is what is being said to me. Is that a fair criticism?

Fintan Slye: I do not think it is NESO’s use or lack thereof of technology that is impeding the rollout, or the adoption and payment for flexibility in the commercial and industrial space that you are talking about. What we have not seen is the market arrangements developed to a point that makes it a commercially attractive proposition for that individual.

Q10 Chair: What needs to happen for those market arrangements be in place?

Fintan Slye: Government published, I think it was last year, the clean flexibility road map. This is a road map setting out across industry participants, the regulator, ourselves and the market facilitator, the things that need to be done across the different sections of the market to enable that flexibility to come on stream. All parties are working to try to progress that as quickly as possible.

Q11 Chair: When is that supposed to happen?

Fintan Slye: That is a road map of increasingly opening the market and getting rid of the multiplicity of barriers over time—

Q12 Chair: We do not have a timeline; we do not have dates on the different stages?

Fintan Slye: Sorry, there is a timeline within that; I do not have it a hand but we can absolutely share it with you. Apologies.

Chair: That will be helpful. Thank you.

Q13 Melanie Onn: Connections reform, strategic planning, control room activity, grid build out and policy advice are all things that fall underneath NESO responsibilities. Is that too much to do? Are there competing priorities within that?

Fintan Slye: As with anything in the energy space, there are competing parties and competing tensions. In fact, as we talked about earlier, the energy system is ultimately about how you balance those elements of the trilemma. It is a huge role and set of responsibilities that we have been given, and we have looked to scale the organisation very significantly since its inception in October 2024. We started with just over 1,000 people, we are nearly at 3,000 people now, so we are really scaling up the resources and the capabilities we have in the organisation across all dimensions.

I accept it is a really broad remit, but one of the benefits that NESO brings is that you have all those elements within one organisation and therefore can see the trade-offs between them and take that whole system perspective, such that you can focus on getting the right answer for consumers and for the system.

Q14 Melanie Onn: You do not seem to be managing that very well in terms of the levels of expectation and delivery. You have admitted yourself that you knew about issues in the control room but failed to take action. You have said that there was lots of change happening in the organisation, yet you are used to working in large organisations. Would it be better if there was someone who was in a position to deal with this, or someone who has more experience with change management, if you are not able to take on board significant concerns that an investigation has revealed exposes deep cultural issues?

Fintan Slye: We take the findings of the Eversheds investigation incredibly seriously. The organisation has been on a journey since it was established at the end of 2024. We have grown the organisation significantly and have delivered some incredibly big and important things for the energy system and for the country over that period. What the report showed is that there are some areas where we need to focus and improve. As the chief executive, I absolutely take responsibility for making sure that we do that; that the organisation grows and that we build the capability within it to address those issues so that we can continue to deliver for society and the economy.

Q15 Melanie Onn: Do you think you are going to be in a position to aid in delivering clean power by 2030?

Fintan Slye: Absolutely. As I say, the organisation continues to deliver across the really broad remit that you outlined earlier.

Q16 Melanie Onn: Do you think 2030 is still achievable?

Fintan Slye: In terms of the achievability of 2030, when we first set out our advice to Government, the term we used was that it was a Herculean task. It requires all parts of the system to do things at a pace and in a way that has not been done previously, and that—

Q17 Melanie Onn: Well, we know that all parts of the system are not doing that because in your organisation bits of it have failed.

Fintan Slye: Our view remains that hitting 20230 is an incredibly challenging target. In terms of the pieces of the plan that we are responsible for, we are absolutely focused on making sure that we deliver those parts. However, it is an energy system wide, indeed economy wide, change. We do not hold or control all the levers necessary to hit 2030, but we are absolutely committed to working with industry partners, Government and the regulator to play our part and make sure our bits of it are delivered

Q18 Melanie Onn: What do you feel are the biggest risks to delivering clean power by 2030?

Fintan Slye: At the moment, it is infrastructure delivery. If you think about the scale of the transformation that Clean Power 2030 needs, on both the generation side and the network side, these are really big, hairy infrastructure projects. Delivering those within the timeline is probably the greatest: will the renewable generation come on stream, and will the network build be delivered in line with the expected timelines? Within that there are obviously a series of specific risks for each of those categories, but in a macro sense, I would say that infrastructure delivery is probably the biggest risk.

Chair: We welcome Catherine Fookes to the Committee for her first session.

Q19 Catherine Fookes: Thank you, Chair, and thank you to our witnesses for appearing for us today. Why do you think people blew the whistle if there was not a problem?

Fintan Slye: Reflecting on the report and what it said, it did not substantiate the four specific allegations. However, what it did find was that there was a set of other cultural issues within the organisation that we needed to look at, and we are completely committed to doing that. One of the reflections—as you would expect—that we looked at our whistleblowing and speak up policy to see if it was fit for purpose, and whether it had all the elements in it that it should for an organisation such as us ours. My reflection is that while it does, and it did, we need to go further as an organisation and be best in class at this. We need to ensure that we have a culture in the organisation that encourages people to use both the internal channels, and indeed the other external channels: for example, Ofgem has whistle-blowing facilities as well.

We are also in the process of engaging with both the trade unions, which have really great examples of where this works well in other organisations, and with external experts to make sure that our policy is not just what it needs to be but that we actually have the right culture. We want people’s lived experience in the organisation to be that they feel free to raise things, whether that is within their teams or anonymously.

One of the things I did take some comfort from in the report—I put my hands up and acknowledge there was difficult reading in there—was that people actually felt quite comfortable raising issues with their managers and within their teams. But we were not really good at all at providing a feedback loop to say to them, “This is what we have done with your concern.” You may have raised a concern and a team somewhere else in the organisation would have dealt with it, but we were not as good as we should have been at closing the feedback loop so those individuals who raised it could see how it was resolved.

Q20 Catherine Fookes: Even so, those people obviously raised concerns in your organisation and felt they were not being dealt and therefore they had to whistleblow. What are you actually doing to instil a culture where next time we have an issue, like we did in June 2026, you ensure that people do not have to go outside the organisation, but that you deal with their concerns and that the culture of honesty within the organisation actually changes and delivers?

Fintan Slye: That is a programme of work that we are setting out to do, to absolutely make sure that we have that in place and that we have a best-in-class, best practice approach.

Q21 Catherine Fookes: What changes have you already made to make that happen?

Fintan Slye: Very specifically in terms of the things that we have done, we have set up an engineering forum run by our chief engineer. This provides a place where any concerns from the operational teams or control room teams can be raised. Those things are then explicitly triaged and dealt with, with a feedback loop back to that individual. That is one very specific thing that we have done.

We have also established a new operability committee at the highest level of the executive team, which includes senior managers as well. That enables any of those issues that are coming up through the organisation to be dealt with in a very explicit way at the highest level, making sure those concerns are addressed to the extent they need to be. It is cross-functional to ensure we join up all the bits of the business, and that committee will provide updates on a regular basis into the board, to make sure that we have closed those issues off. As I say, one of the issues was that this feedback loop was not there, so we have done that.

We are also talking to the organisation in a really open way about this, trying to engage both individuals and cohorts of teams across the organisation. We are looking to engage with the trade unions to get their advice and input on how to do this. We are trying to move on multiple fronts to address this and to do it in a really open way with the organisation and our people.

Kayte O’Neill: The big thing for me is that we recognise that trust with our colleagues is crucial. One of the things for me from the report is that if there is a single individual in the organisation who feels that they do not trust that we will take their concern seriously, then that is one too many.

The proof will be in the pudding. They need to see us behave differently and they need to see that change flow through. Hopefully, we will have that trust that we already have in vast ways across the organisation. Again, for any individual to have to go outside and use a whistle blowing channel because they felt they were not being listened to is one too many, so we need to address that.

Q22 Catherine Fookes: How are you dealing with the high levels of operational stress that the report found your colleagues in the control room were going through?

Kayte O’Neill: Again, this is something we take really seriously. The control room teams are very well trained. It is an environment where they thrive and where they do the amazing work that they are there to do. However, they are very open in talking to us about where that feels challenging and where they are looking to us to provide support.

We have some really practical measures on the ground: for example, if they come off shift and feel like they need to talk to someone, or even vent about what it is like to operate the system in sometimes very challenging circumstances, they have the opportunity to do that. We also have really good team structures and a great leadership team in and around the control room, who are continually having that dialogue with colleagues, ensuring they get the support they need.

The forums that Fintan mentioned will allow us to identify where they think there are specific things we can do to help. A good example of that is that we know that a lack of system visibility can make its operation very challenging. They talked to us about that and we are now looking at how to provide better visibility and better situational awareness in the control room as a direct outcome of having those really open conversations.

Q23 Chair: What I do not understand from listening to your answers, is why it took a whistleblower and an independent review for you to recognise these cultural problems.

Fintan Slye: There was already a huge amount of work under way to address those issues. The review, because of its nature and being a public report, put a very fine point on it. We are a relatively young organisation that has been growing significantly, and where there were cultural issues, we were looking to proactively address them through all this. Obviously having those very specific seven recommendations in the report, which the board have accepted in full, provides a focus for delivering on them.

Q24 Chair: You are not the first large organisation to face cultural issues, but the safety-critical nature of your organisation puts it on a different level. You described the process, but there is a very big difference between people in theory being able to engage with senior managers to raise any concerns and, in practice, having the confidence and—Kayte used the word trust, which is a really important word—trusting that they will be listened to, and not just listened to but respected and not suffer adverse consequences as a result. How are you making sure that this is a success?

Fintan Slye: We are acutely conscious of exactly the things that you say because it is the lived experience of colleagues that matters. The set of processes are important and necessary, but not sufficient on their own. We need to ensure that people feel heard. As I say, one of the key things that came through from the report was that the people interviewed indicated they were relatively comfortable raising concerns with their managers. What they felt was missing was that the feedback loop was not closed. That will have a knock-on consequence. The next time they may not raise an issue at all if they feel it was not heard or acted on: even if it was somewhere in the organisation but we had not closed that feedback loop. Therefore, we are looking to put in those behavioural changes at an executive level to improve how we engage with the teams, but also those processes to ensure those feedback loops are really clear and closed.

Q25 Chair: You have talked about your executive-level engagement a few times now; does this mean that you and your executive team are going to be directly talking to engineers and people at all levels of the organisation to build those relationships, and to demonstrate by leading that is the culture change you want?

Fintan Slye: Absolutely, we will be out there doing exactly that. It is something I do all the time: I go into the control room to talk to the teams and it is something we do around the organisation. However, it is clear we need to do more of that and be better at it. When things come up in those conversations, we need to ensure there is actually a follow-through and response back to the individual who raised it. That is really important and those are the things that will change the culture of the organisation and indeed the lived experience of the people who work for us.

Fintan Slye: I suppose when I read the report it—

Q27 Graeme Downie: Sorry; before you read the report you did not believe there was a disconnect between the senior leadership team and the control room?

Q30 Graeme Downie: What reflections did that lead to? Was it perhaps that the steps you had been taking for the initial two years were not working in terms of identifying that disconnect?

Q31 Graeme Downie: When was that put in place?

Fintan Slye: No, and we are continuously looking at how we can improve engagement with colleagues right across the organisation. Fintan Slye: We have the report and its findings, so that is a set of actions and recommendations that we will now take forward and deal with. As I say, we do extensive surveying of our staff on an anonymous basis that allows them to provide feedback to the executive team and provide their perspectives on the organisation—

Q34 Graeme Downie: Well, clearly, they have not been effective up to the point of the report.

Fintan Slye: The report identified a specific issue of a disconnect between the control room teams and executive management. I have such high regard for the brilliant people in there and the job they do. Q35 Graeme Downie: Is it not eerily obvious from the last two years that it is a critical part of the organisation that has to be engaged with differently? Yet it does not sound like that was something that was happening as much as it should have been.

Fintan Slye: What the report has shown is that a disconnect developed, and that is something that we are now working very actively and are very seized of the need to address.

Q100 Sir Christopher Chope: The NAO has said that you are; you have a role in scrutinising the costs. Do you accept that? The National Audit Office has said that you have such a role.

Fintan Slye: Yes.

Q101 Sir Christopher Chope: What is going to be the cost of balancing the grid in the current financial year?

Fintan Slye: In the current financial year, it will be about £3 billion.

Q102 Sir Christopher Chope: It will be £3 billion. What do you expect it to be in 2030?

Fintan Slye: Our estimate is about £8 billion in 2029-30.

Q103 Sir Christopher Chope: It will be £8 billion?

Fintan Slye: Yes, it comes down very significantly after that.

Q104 Sir Christopher Chope: Let us put this in context. Today the Chancellor of the Exchequer is going to make a big announcement about removing standing charges from gas bills. There are 23 million users of gas and they are going to have these standing charges removed. The cost calculated, just over £100 per year per user, is about £2.5 billion. The Chancellor is going to make a big thing about £2.5 billion being saved from bills for the coming year, or in 2027, and yet you are forecasting that between this year and 2030 there is going to be an increase of about £5 billion in the costs of balancing the grid, which are four square put upon the consumer, are they not, the user of the electricity?

Fintan Slye: Yes, and those numbers and projections are something that we have highlighted for a number of years now. We are absolutely committed to making sure that they are as low as possible. We recognise the costs today are a huge burden on consumers. You are absolutely correct; they flow through to bills. There are two things to note. One is that in the medium term, the thing that will get constraint and balancing costs down is the delivery of new network, and we are working with all the transmission companies, Government, and Ofgem to get network delivered as quickly as possible. That is the thing that ultimately brings those constraint costs down.

Q105 Sir Christopher Chope: These balancing costs are a stealth tax on consumers. They are contributing to the cost of living crisis. I was looking at the figures and, back in 2013-14, the balancing costs were only £1 billion. You are now complacently saying, “Well we may be able to limit it to about £8 billion by 2030.” This is unacceptable. Am I right in saying that, if we did not have this obsession with net zero and clean energy by 2030, these costs would be significantly reduced and in fact could be put back to the level that they were in 2013-14 and thereby save consumers in the order of £7 billion a year?

Fintan Slye: Firstly, to clarify, there is nothing complacent in how we view that level of costs and those levels of costs flowing through to consumers’ bills. We are absolutely doing everything we can to try to minimise those costs. They are the costs that arise due to the actions that are necessary to ensure security of supply in the control room. We have been flagging for a while now that they are due to rise. Some of that increase this year is down to the increase in the wholesale gas price because some actions we take are to turn up gas generation in parts of the country, and if that is significantly more expensive then that flows through to balancing costs. Notwithstanding that the key to getting constraint costs down—we share absolutely the concern about that level of cost—is the delivery of network infrastructure. In the short term, we are working with the transmission companies to maximise what can be got out of the assets that are already there using things such as dynamic line rating where, in real time, you can push more power through lines.

Q106 Sir Christopher Chope: When are we going to get these balancing costs back to where they were in 2013-14 of £1 billion a year?

Fintan Slye: They drop significantly in the very early 2030s. Our projections are that they drop significantly, and that is in line with the delivery programme by the network companies.

Q107 Sir Christopher Chope: To £1 billion a year?

Fintan Slye: Our projection is they come back down to £2 billion a year but I will have to come back to you on it. We publish an estimate of these. I will have to come back to you on the number but I think it is £2 billion.

Q108 Sir Christopher Chope: People complain about the cost of net zero policies and the insistence on the 2030 target for so-called clean electricity. What I am saying is that these constraint costs or balancing charges imposed upon the consumer are a direct consequence of that insane policy, are they not?

Fintan Slye: The biggest driver of the level of constraint costs is a mismatch in infrastructure and delivery times between generation and transmission, with the generation connecting before the transmission infrastructure can be built. We have seen transmission infrastructure take longer. It is also important to point out that constraint costs are not the sum total of the benefits or dis-benefits—let’s not use slang, the costs to consumers. When you connect this generation earlier, it actually displaces gas and brings down the wholesale price of electricity so the £8 billion is too much. It is too high. We are focused on getting it down but I would not want you to walk out of here with the impression that delivery of the renewable infrastructure early is just a cost. There is also a benefit to it that accrues to customers through lower wholesale prices.

Q109 Sir Christopher Chope: We have yet to see any reduction in electricity prices. Our electricity prices are the highest in Europe, if not in the developed world. When are we going to get electricity prices down to the same level as gas prices are at the moment?

Fintan Slye: Hopefully soon, but it is the very high gas price that is one of the key drivers of high electricity prices.

Q110 Sir Christopher Chope: Gas is still cheaper than electricity, is it not?

Fintan Slye: You have the spark gap, which is the difference between them, but high gas prices play an important role in feeding through to the high electricity price that we have. One of the benefits of a transition away from gas power generation to nuclear renewables and so on is that you are less reliant in your electricity system and therefore the price is less than the price of gas.

Q111 Sir Christopher Chope: There is a difference between nuclear, which is constant, and renewables, which are variable. What you have to do is use public money, taxpayers’ money, consumers’ money, in order to try to enable the grid coming from the electricity to accommodate surges in power from windmills and solar. If we did not invest so much in Chinese windmills and solar panels, the costs to you of balancing the system would be far lower, would they not?

Fintan Slye: Without going into the different origins of location of technology, it is really important that you have a diverse portfolio of generation. It provides greater resilience for the country as a whole to have a diverse portfolio. When looking at the costs that consumers face, we should absolutely factor in constraint costs and we should focus on getting them down, but we also need to look at the whole-system costs that consumers face, because consumers pay all the costs within our energy system. It is incumbent on us as NESO, on the energy industry, to make sure we are trying to get to the lowest cost system. One of the ways to think about it is we will never, and probably should never, get to a situation where constraint costs are zero. If you get to that point, you have probably overbuilt your network, such that never for one hour on one day is there a constraint. There is an optimal.

Q112 Sir Christopher Chope: All I am asking, on behalf of consumers, is for a reduction in these enormously high costs. There was a day earlier on this month when £47 million was spent in a day on balancing costs; that is a direct cost upon consumers of electricity. This is intolerable. You seem to not recognise the need to try to prevent these increases taking place. You are already three times what it was in 2013-14; you are projecting to go up another £5 billion a year beyond that. Why are you not taking immediate action to level that off so we do not go beyond say £3.5 billion a year? Limit it for the future.

Fintan Slye: The levers to do that are not within NESO’s control.

Q113 Sir Christopher Chope: Are they within the control of the Government?

Fintan Slye: They are a function of, as I said, the main driver of the constraint costs and driving them up to the numbers we talked about.

Q114 Sir Christopher Chope: I must finish but these are a direct consequence of misguided Government policy, are they not?

Fintan Slye: Constraint costs add to consumers’ bills. I would not want anyone to leave here with the impression that we were doing anything other than trying to minimise them, but it is important to recognise that constraint costs are one part of the set of costs that consumers pay. When you bring generation on, for example, ahead of the transmission infrastructure, that can give rise to constraint costs. It can also reduce the wholesale price of electricity. What I am saying is that we should all be focused, and we are focused, on trying to ensure that consumers’ bills are as low as possible. Energy prices are too high and we need to pull—to use your language—every lever that we have available to us to make sure that we are doing that. We are absolutely focused on trying to get constraint costs as low as they can be using the things that are within our control and, where they are outside our control, trying to use the influence that we have to do it.

Q115 Sir Christopher Chope: You will be judged on the results.

Fintan Slye: Always.

Fintan Slye: I suppose that is possible; to be honest, that is why I always ring the PSM before I walk into the control room, so I am not doing it unannounced. I say, “How are things going in the system? Is it all right if I drop in and have a quick chat?” I try to be as open and engaging as I can be with staff right across the business.

For a control engineer in any part of the business, it can be intimidating if the CEO wanders over and says, “How are you doing today? What’s going on?” We are acutely conscious of that and I try to make sure that I do it in a really thoughtful way. I know it is a trivial example, but as I said, I call the PSM before I walk into the control room.

Q117 Claire Young: I am just wondering about your awareness of how you are coming across given what we have read in the press, but I will move on. Kayte O’Neill: We can think about flexibility in two parts: demand-side flexibility and supply-side flexibility. We have already talked a bit this morning about demand-side flexibility, whether that is in consumers’ homes or businesses, and we are making really good progress in that space.

Q118 Claire Young: What evidence do you have that you are making good progress?

Kayte O’Neill: We set ourselves targets for the amount of flexibility that we would want to see coming through and at the moment we are meeting—in some cases exceeding—those targets. We have worked really hard with industry to put those targets in place and make them very transparent and industry is holding us to account to make progress. As Fintan said earlier, many parts of the Clean Power 2030 action plan required really significant change. Growth in flexibility was one and the supply-side flexibility is a really important part of that.

It is largely going to be policy-driven as to where the different sources of flexibility come forward. That is not to say that in the meantime, if some big sources of flexibility do not come forward, we have an insecure or inoperable system. As we said we are confident that there is movement between the different pathways, and that if we have less flexibility we have to account for that elsewhere. When we were creating the clean power advice we started with, “What is the maximum flexibility we can get on the system?” That minimises what else you need. We take it very seriously in that sense.

Q119 Claire Young: Do you think you have all the levers that you need?

Kayte O’Neill: On the demand side, some things that we have already talked about continue to be important. We know from talking to the market that it still sees barriers to getting in. We continue to push on all those fronts. As I say we are making good progress, but ultimately there will be decisions for others in the energy system to make.

Q120 Claire Young: If you cannot achieve the flexibility have you worked out what you are going to do instead?

Kayte O’Neill: Through all our strategic planning we are looking at how we maintain a secure and resilient system. We do it for every time horizon from real time—this minute, the next hour—through to the medium and long term.

Q121 Claire Young: I would like to come back to you, Mr Slye, and broaden this out a little. Fintan Slye: No, I do not think so. What we need to do though is explain better what the roles and remits are, in particular the dividing line between us, Government/DESNZ and Ofgem. Q122 Claire Young: Do you not think that perhaps it would be better to split up operational day-to-day from the looking to the future and the more research and policy development side of it?

Fintan Slye: I do not. A benefit of having that long-term strategic planning in the same organisation as the operational side of the system is that, in developing those long-term plans, you can ensure that, when the situation arrives in the control room, you have factored all those things into those long-term plans in whatever the time horizon is that you needed to. In a lot of cases, by the time it gets to the control room the set of things that they can do shrinks down as you get closer to real time.

You have to make sure that the toolbox you give them in the room on the day is sufficient to deal with anything that can happen on the system. That is what we are focused on. Q123 Claire Young: How do you ensure that you have achieved that? Absence of it going wrong just proves that the circumstances have not arisen yet; it does not prove that you have it right. Particularly given all the issues raised in the report how do you have confidence that you have given them the right toolbox?

Fintan Slye: There are a number of mechanisms by which we provide assurance. First, we do this very publicly by publishing an operability strategy report that sets out how we see the key challenges facing the system as it evolves and what we are doing to address them and we run a set of industry engagements to talk to those and get feedback on it. Secondly, we work very closely with international system operators in particular those who face similar challenges or whose systems are going through broadly similar transitions as we are. We share best practice and experience, and challenge each other on what is happening there.

Thirdly, we have an engineering advisory committee with external experts, academics, international system operators and so on that provide a level of challenge and assurance over what we are doing. As we talked about earlier we also have the new operability committee in place, which ensures that those forward-looking concerns by the control room are fed into this. We adopt a multi-layered approach because it is so important that we are thinking about these things right out into the future.

An example would be that as the level of inertia goes down in the system inherently, with the closure of some fossil fuel stations—for example the closure of coal off the system—we put in place a programme many years ahead of time to go out and effectively purchase inertia as a service from the market. That required us to run a tender and people then had to build assets and deliver them onto the system. That is how we think about those things and think about them ahead of time.
