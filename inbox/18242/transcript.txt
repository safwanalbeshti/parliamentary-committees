Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Yvette Cooper MP, Samantha Jones and Sir Jim Mackey.

Chair: Welcome to this session of the Health and Social Care Committee, where we will be looking at the work of the Department. It is a delight to welcome the new Secretary of State, Yvette Cooper—thank you for coming—and our dear friends, now, Sir Jim Mackey and Sam Jones. Thank you so much for joining us.

I will get right into it. Secretary of State, you have set out your priorities: maternity is one, and early years and children is the other. We have our 10-year plan, and we also have a Prime Minister who has put a lot of stock in social care. Are you making any tweaks to the 10-year plan to reflect your priorities, and what are they?

Yvette Cooper: Thank you for having me, Chair. It is actually 25 years since I last gave evidence to a Health Committee, so thank you very much for having me back. It is a huge privilege for me to be back in the Health Department as Health Secretary, and to be covering social care at a time when it is such a priority for the Prime Minister, with his strong commitment to building a national care service. Of course, we have been reviewing all of the work around the 10-year plan and all the work of the Department over the past few months.

What I would say is that the commitment to improving the performance of the NHS, and the turnaround that we need after the deterioration in performance under previous Governments, but also through covid, is crucial. The three shifts set out in the NHS plan—to prevention, to community services and to digital—all remain as important as ever, but you are right that we are adding to that both the commitments to the national care service and the priority that I am giving to maternity and child health, because I just think that that was getting lost and we were not putting enough of a priority on it.

Chair: What is concretely changing as a result of that?

Yvette Cooper: As a result of our focus on maternity and child health? Obviously, the maternity taskforce is under way at the moment, and we expect to publish an action plan—it will be interim, in terms of the things that we do—before Christmas, and a new modern services framework, with the national standards around maternity and neonatal care, in the middle of next year.

Interestingly, when I checked what I had given evidence on 25 years ago, the last time I gave evidence, it was on national service frameworks, which have since been cancelled. Establishing that modern services framework of standards will be important.

The other thing that I am seeking to do is bring back the Sure Start principles and the Sure Start approach to maternity and the early years. That is why we have announced the roll-out of the Healthy Babies programme—really, Sure Start health services—to all the family hubs across the country. Those are things that are either being strengthened or are different.

Chair: On social care, it was music to our ears as a Committee when you talked about the cost of inaction while making the case for how we are going to pay for it. There are two sides to this. Obviously, there is a huge range—between £3.5 billion and £17 billion—for what some form of national care service might cost. Our understanding is that your best estimate is from the Health Foundation. Has any other work been done on the different possible models? When Casey reports, the hope is that we can just get on with it, so I am trusting that you are preparing for this.

Yvette Cooper: As you say, Baroness Casey is doing the commission and consultations, hearing from the public and looking at what the long-term plan for a national care service looks like. We are approaching it almost from the other end, looking at what the building blocks are—what are the things that we need to do? What are the step-by-step measures that we can take? That means the workforce, but it also means looking at how we better integrate health and social care—for example, around dementia and frailty. We are looking at what the steps are, and we are also working very closely with Baroness Casey’s commission so that we can anticipate the direction of travel.

Chair: There was going to be an interim report soon, but now that the final report will be published next year, that is not happening. Are you saying that you are basically doing what the interim report would have said? I am struck by the fact that if we wait until next year, everything that we could have implemented from an interim report will be delayed. What is your take on that?

Yvette Cooper: We have a small ministerial group that meets, and meets with Louise Casey at intervals throughout, so that we can get updates on the direction of travel and do some of the preparation work. Louise will decide herself what further reports or interim arrangements she wants between now and the summer; that is for the commission.

Let me list some of the building blocks that we are looking at. One is the workforce strategy, and I can say more about that if you want me to. We are also looking at how you better combine health and social care on prevention and preventing frail elderly people going into hospital in the first place; how we improve the discharge processes, which I think have gone backwards since 25 years ago; progress around things like dementia; and some of the issues around palliative care and end-of-life care, which is an area where social care and healthcare already work closely together but not in the way all of us want them to, as part of a new modern service framework. Ally McGovern is also looking in some detail at the structures we have in place at the moment, the way in which commissioning works and what improvements we can make step by step. We are looking at building blocks step by step, while Louise works through the vision of a national care service that the Prime Minister wants to see.

Chair: Would you write to us with more detail on those buckets?

Yvette Cooper: Sure, I would be happy to.

Chair: We would love to see it, but we are probably not going to have enough time to delve into the detail today.

Let me now turn to the change in pharmaceutical pricing, also known as the UK-US pharmaceutical deal. This Committee has been concerned about the lack of transparency in the impact assessment, what it is going to affect downstream in the NHS, what the money might be spent on and the sorts of drugs that we might end up having to commission as a result. Regardless of whether one feels it is a good or bad idea—there is a range of views—the lack of transparency has really concerned us. You might be aware that the National Audit Office announced just yesterday that it is starting a workstream on this. Secretary of State, bluntly, why should it take the NAO to get the transparency that this Committee and Parliament have been wanting from the Department?

Yvette Cooper: Let me give a bit of overview, and then Sam may want to come in on more of the detail.

Chair: Briefly—it is about the transparency point, not the wider issue. I appreciate you were not Secretary of State, but we have covered this at length before. You are a new Secretary of State. Do you think it is right that it takes the NAO to give the level of transparency that should be offered to Parliament as a matter of course? That is the question.

Yvette Cooper: As a former Select Committee Chair, I think the NAO always plays a crucial role across a whole series of areas. It will always have a role to play in these processes. I know there is interest from a series of Select Committees on this issue, and we therefore welcome that as a process.

The big conclusions of the agreement are the increase in spending on innovative medicines over the next 10 years and the impact that that has on long-term investment in the UK—that all clear as part of the agreement. So, too, are the immediate changes on the NICE guidance that we brought in in the spring. As a consequence of those changes we have had 10 new crucial drugs granted NICE approvals for the NHS, including Enhertu, which make a big difference for patients and families across the country. On some of the clear, crucial building-block decisions that are part of that, there is a lot of transparency, as there always is around the NICE processes. Perhaps Sam wants to come in with more of the detail.

Chair: I am sorry, Secretary of State. All you have done is repeat the press release. What I am looking for is the downstream effect of this deal on NHS budgets. Sam Jones, can you help us?

Samantha Jones: Thank you to you and the Secretary of State. This is the subject of a number of discussions, as you are obviously aware, on the release of the commercial information associated with it, and there have been initial conversations with the NAO. I know you appreciate, because we discussed it before, that some of the more technical aspects and pilots currently under way as a result of the arrangement are currently being worked through, including what the impact is on drugs that we would not have received or drugs that we might have received. That in itself is informing the specific cost of the deal over the SR period, which has been set out. To your original question and the commitment to transparency, we are working through the commercial arrangements on how best we do that on a cross-Government deal with a number of different Select Committees and the different Departments involved.

Chair: Let’s move on. Danny Beales is next.

Danny Beales: Good morning, Secretary of State. The Committee has looked in many of our inquiries at the challenge of moving services from sickness to prevention. It is an ongoing theme that we have often returned to. So we were quite excited to see the introduction of PDEL in the budget. For those watching and wondering, I understand PDEL refers to a prevention-based budget line. Do you envisage PDEL will be a ring-fenced element of spend? How do you envisage it working in practice?

Yvette Cooper: As you can imagine, our public health teams were extremely excited by the Treasury announcement about PDEL. We think a lot of our investment across not just public health but across the NHS has benefits in terms of keeping people healthy and in work. A lot of those treatment budgets have a consequence in helping people work as well as in the direct prevention. We do not know yet how this is going to play out. I think these will end up being the crucial decisions for the spending review and ultimately decisions for the Treasury. We are already looking at, from our budget point of view, what would count in that way. To be honest, we are already doing this, anyway, because one of the challenges for us is how you shift to neighbourhood health.

Danny Beales: If we are doing it, anyway, why would we need this? What value would it add?

Yvette Cooper: As a former Treasury Minister, I would say the benefit of this is keeping that focus and discipline around the things that you invest in now that have the long-term benefits. Those are the areas that always come under shortest-term pressure. At the moment there are challenges with it. We know that, for example, if you can keep frail elderly people out of hospital, that is much more effective for the patients and also for the NHS as a whole, instead of having costly NHS acute care. Later on, once somebody has become more ill, you will save money.

That is hard, because of the current ways in which the NHS works, but we are trying to achieve that. Having that PDEL process could help with the discipline of doing that.

Danny Beales: Do you envisage it including primary prevention, secondary prevention, technology or anything else? Most things can technically be linked to some sort of prevention. How rigorous will your definition of prevention spending be?

Yvette Cooper: Mine would be quite broad; we do not yet know what the Treasury’s will be. Primary prevention clearly is, and I strongly think that secondary prevention is. I also think that a lot of our treatment services have an impact, in saving money elsewhere. Mental health is a really good example, as we know that a lot of young people who are not in work could benefit from mental health support.

Danny Beales: Will it require any shift in spend, or will it be just a recategorization of existing spending? Will it just shine a light on what is already happening, or will it actually lead to a shift in activity?

Yvette Cooper: We need to make the shift, regardless of how the Treasury categorises the money. That shift is part of our 10-year plan, so we will need to do that. I hope that the Treasury and the PDEL process will support us and will strengthen that in an effective way.

Danny Beales: Will there be any protection around the PDEL? As you know, capital budgets have been raided time and again in the NHS: we may know we need to fix the roof, but suddenly, we will raid that fund to prop up A&Es in winter crisis months. That happened time and again under the previous Government, and it is increasingly concerning, given that with the DIP, we are looking at capital budgets again. Will this be the same case of saying, “We really value prevention,” until we do not because we need to prop up A&Es again?

Yvette Cooper: You are right that there is always pressure, and there can be short-term pressures on some of the prevention budgets. I think the Treasury is trying to achieve greater protection for prevention. The only area on which I would disagree with you is on the DIP, because ultimately, the funding for the defence investment programme is about prevention, as it is about our national security and safety. Of course, that means all Government Departments doing their bit, but that is a shift from one area of investment and capital to another to keep the country safe.

Danny Beales: Another area that often struggles to be prioritised is mental health. In May, the Government committed to a mental health strategy. Obviously, we do not have that strategy yet, so although I may encourage you to talk about what may be in it, I appreciate that you might not be able to go into detail. More generally, what do you hope the strategy will achieve, and what will mark it as successful? What will it add, beyond what is in the modern service framework being developed and the community mental health framework that already exists?

Yvette Cooper: The mental health strategy has to bring all of this together. In Peter Fonagy’s review into prevalence, we saw the real, significant increase in distress in mental health, particularly among younger people. With common mental health conditions such as anxiety and depression, we want to expand and do more on talking therapies, which can be effective. We also know that there are areas where we have real problems with waiting lists. So many people could benefit from much earlier intervention, rather than letting that distress worsen, become embedded and have a huge impact on people’s lives later on.

The challenge for us is how to get earlier intervention. That means working and rolling out our new mental health teams in schools, but it also means doing more on community services. In the summer, I announced new community walk-in services for mental health, located on high streets and in communities. Being able to do more of that kind of early intervention will be hugely important.

Danny Beales: On the issue of waiting times, which you mentioned, in July, 1.71 million people were waiting for first or second referrals, and 82% said that their mental health deteriorated while they were waiting. We as MPs will all have seen this in our constituencies. I have seen young people struggle to access CAMHS—until they are suicidal, they probably will not get access—and parents who are deeply distressed about the situation that their child is in. Will the strategy actually grasp the nettle on this, and have clearer standards, as there is an absence of any clear standard or target for the system, as well as commitments on waiting times for mental health care?

Yvette Cooper: The mental health strategy needs to look at all these issues. Obviously, I cannot pre-empt where the strategy gets to. On the waiting lists picture, there are some areas where waiting lists are very short, but there are others, including some children’s ADHD and autism assessments, for example, that are very long. You saw some of the things that Peter Fonagy’s review said about those, as well as some of the problems with independent sector assessments. In other areas, the talking therapies are proving to be positive and effective, as are some of the community services. What need to pull all that together in a strategy. We are absolutely going to have to look at waiting lists and waiting times as part of the strategy. That is clear.

Chair: Is that a commitment—that there will be waiting times?

Yvette Cooper: I am not going to spell out what we will say in the mental health strategy. I can say that we will have to look at waiting times and waiting lists as part of the strategy, but they are different for different bits of the system. People often talk about overall mental health waiting lists. Actually, they are very different. The most acute waiting times for some of the most serious things, like eating disorders and so on, have already come down very substantially, so we can intervene quickly, but we have to look at these across the board.

Danny Beales: I am not going to claim that every aspect of the system may need waiting times, but there is clearly an absence of any rigorous standard in mental health, even for, as you say, serious mental health, as well as for CAMHS. Do you envisage there being some rigorous standard or target in the strategy, or a commitment to develop one?

Yvette Cooper: I totally understand why you are pressing me on this. I am just going to say that I am not going to pre-empt the strategy, because that is work that we still need to do and announce in the normal way. I will come back to you as soon as I can on that.

Chair: Can you give us a timeline?

Yvette Cooper: Our plan is to produce the mental health strategy before the end of the year. We are also working in combination with the response to Alan Milburn’s review on NEETs, so we may end up doing things on slightly different timescales for different bits of the system, depending on the timetable of the cross-Government work on that.

Danny Beales: Investment is often crucial in driving decisions. I think most ICBs met the mental health investment standard in ’24-25. It has driven increasing spending on mental health services, which had been neglected. This has been an ongoing discussion year after year; the Committee has talked to the various Secretaries of State and asked them to commit to keeping the standard. Eventually, and fortunately, it has been kept. Should we have a long-term commitment, rather than this year-after-year discussion, to the mental health investment standard? Is the strategy going to speak to this point?

Yvette Cooper: Again, all these things need to be part of the strategy. As you know, we are attempting to do multi-year planning for the NHS. Of course, when there are continuing financial pressures, that has to be kept under review, but we are attempting to do more long-term planning and have more long-term ability and stability for the service across the board.

Danny Beales: Will that stability involve investment in mental health services keeping pace with general NHS spending? It is great to have certainty, but if the certainty is that you are going to have declining funding, that is not great news for the service. Will there be a commitment to ongoing and increasing funding?

Yvette Cooper: I do not think any Secretary of State is going to have any choice but to continue to ensure proper investment in mental health services, because the scale of need and the impact that it is having, including on the labour market, as well as on the distress for families, is significant.

There is a big issue for us about how we look more at prevention. We cannot simply think about mental health services’ response to the increase in anxiety and depression among our young people. We need to look at the causes, whether those are around social media or the pressure on young people’s lives, and at what early interventions should take place. I would go right back to the early years. Part of the reason I feel so strongly about Sure Start is that, if you look at the evaluation of it, it led to improvements in mental health, and you see those benefits a decade later, in the teenage years. When I talk about early intervention, I would go right back to those very first early years.

Danny Beales: Finally, do you recognise that the mental health investment standard is the reason why mental health spending has started to increase and the cuts those services previously saw have not taken place? Do you accept that, without it, areas are likely to again start to reduce—or not increase—spending on mental health services?

Yvette Cooper: It is right for us to be clear to ICBs about the importance of mental health funding. I think that is really important. There will be different ways to look at how we ensure that there is proper investment in mental health in the future. We are also interested in devolution, particularly around the response to young people who are out of employment and training and around mayoral strategic authorities being able to respond and bring services together in their local areas. That might be another way to strengthen the priority for mental health.

Danny Beales: Just to finish off, prevention is a priority and a shift that we want to see. Mental health is underfunded, and we want to see a shift. But there is no commitment to actually protect funding for PDEL or for mental health. Is that where we are?

Yvette Cooper: PDEL will be an issue for the spending review, and I expect us to have a strong focus on that. What we do have a commitment on is to increase our focus on prevention and to make sure that we can do that as part of the 10-year plan. That is our overarching commitment: we have to increase prevention. Of course, that means you have to look at where you invest, but we will certainly do that, even separate from whatever happens on PDEL.

Chair: We have a lot to cover, so let’s move on to Dr Allison Gardner.

Dr Gardner: Can I just pick up a couple of things on mental health and how it impacts our A&Es? I know of cases where people have arrived at A&E with extreme psychosis and have either been discharged from A&E because they could not get the assessment they needed, or spent several days waiting to have that assessment. We are meant to have 24/7 mental health liaison teams attached to A&Es. Has any assessment been made of how effectively those are working and whether that mental health support is happening when people are in crisis?

Yvette Cooper: I will turn to Sir Jim on the detail of that. One of the things we are trying to do—you are right that we do need that specialist assessment. That has an impact not just in terms of getting that patient to the right care, but also on other patients and staff. For someone who is in a mental health crisis, A&E is often really not the right place to be, certainly for long periods of time.

The other thing we are trying to do is invest in, effectively, separate mental health crisis A&E centres, so urgent care. One of the things we announced in the summer, as part of the £300 million investment, was new, separate mental health emergency care centres, so that you can have much more appropriate support for people who are going into mental health crisis.

Sir Jim Mackey: Just to add to that—we covered this a bit on Monday with the PAC—I think everyone would agree that the assessment service you have described is really valuable and really effective, but the whole system has to work together. Once the assessment is done, there has to be capacity and provision for the individual involved. That is usually where the service gets let down: there is not enough capacity there to move that person into a more appropriate setting. Colleagues are working on that around the country.

Dr Gardner: I will come back to that working-together issue in a second, when I talk about ICBs.

Another issue for you to consider for the mental health strategy is that, when people have long-term mental health issues, they can do talking therapies, which, I agree, have great benefits. But when they hit a crisis situation and move into crisis care, often they come out of those talking therapies and have their very short-term crisis support, and then they are back at the bottom of the waiting list, waiting to go back to those therapies. Are you integrating these things, so that people have continuous support going through? That is really important with the mental health strategy. We have to accept that people will go into crisis and will require additional support, but they cannot lose pre-existing support.

Sir Jim Mackey: Absolutely. When I talk to NHS leaders, they see that as an issue and are trying to deal with it. They are also trying to build out the neighbourhood mental health services. Importantly, it looks to me at times like we have services that are built for very discrete definitions—we have discussed this here before, I think—and sometimes packages stop when something else starts, but people are moving in and out of different levels of escalation. People are trying to address that, but it requires an awful lot of things to change at the same time.

Yvette Cooper: The centre I went to in Sheffield is one of our first community-based mental health services. What they were doing was bringing together all the different mental health teams, because you have multiple different teams that might deal with, or provide support in, different circumstances. Having them co-located, changing the way in which health services work so that, as Sir Jim said, they are based around the patient rather than in different silos, has to be what we try to do on this.

Dr Gardner: I am going to jump around, so apologies. I am going to jump to a question about section 75 agreements and the ICB reorganisations. Section 75 of the National Health Service Act 2006 relates to the duty of co-operation or collaboration between NHS providers. A strong recommendation came from this Committee that that should be extended to local authorities, particularly to get that wraparound care. I know from my own experience running a Monkey Dust—synthetic cathinones—taskforce, because we have a big issue with a particular type of drug in Stoke-on-Trent, that our very good drug and alcohol service, as a third-sector provider that has been tendered by the local council, has found trying to get that co-operation with the mental health service and all the other services really challenging. A section 75 would help to make sure that that happens. Where that happens, it works. I understand that the call to do that and extend section 75 agreements to local authorities was rejected. Why was that, and will you think about it again, because I know it will make a difference?

Chair: To be clear, the recommendation was to “review” section 75 arrangements, because they can happen with local authorities but too often do not. That was rejected by the Department, which was a real blow to the Committee, because we see it as a really important way of solving some of these issues.

Sir Jim Mackey: The mechanisms are great where there is agreement locally to use them. I have experienced that in my trust life. Really effective section 75 agreements have tied people together where there was a mutual will to do that. The issue is that you cannot mandate it everywhere. You cannot force people to do it. There has to be a local will. There has to be a purpose around what they are trying to solve.

Dr Gardner: Why not, though? Am I being naive? Why can’t we mandate it?

Sir Jim Mackey: Everybody can do it now. There are no barriers to people doing it. But you need the will of local partners, a plan, a focus and a purpose around what you are trying to achieve through the section 75.

Dr Gardner: What do I do when maybe that will isn’t quite there, because of all the competing pressures that these providers have? And what do I do when I am dealing with a third-sector provider of a service? I then go down the chain: “You’re not the council; you’re a third-sector provider, and we don’t do it with those.” You end up with this very diffuse field, where you have very good providers but they can’t or won’t work together, because of the pressures.

Yvette Cooper: I think this gets to the heart of the devolution question and the rewiring of the state that the Prime Minister has raised, and is one of the issues that is at the heart of looking, now, at how you do greater devolution. It has to be in a way that gets local organisations to properly work together around place, so that you don’t have that sense of, “Look, we have individual organisations doing their own thing, and based on different national requirements. It’s okay: we work in this silo; they work in that silo,” where you don’t get that partnership working. I think the biggest question that the Prime Minister is asking the whole of Government and these others, whether it be mayors or local government across the country, is, how do we rise to that challenge and get organisations working together?

You can look at whether there are legislative duties and responsibilities on people to work together, at different ways of organising how funding flows—for example, different kinds of requirements on pooled budgets—or at giving the mayoral strategic authority oversight of the whole thing, so that they can go in and bash heads together. Different things are being looked at. The Prime Minister has said we want the devolution White Paper to be published very soon, and a lot of that work is being led by No. 10 North, so I do not have a precise answer for you on this issue, other than to say that this is exactly the thing that the Prime Minister wants to solve, based on his experience in Manchester and seeing areas where people are able to bring things together.

Chair: On a point of clarification, when you say “being looked at”, the passive voice does a lot of work in that sentence. Who specifically is looking at it, and is the Department running its own workstream to look at section 75 arrangements?

Yvette Cooper: We are looking more broadly at devolution and what such arrangements should be, as part of work being led by No. 10 North. The reason I use the passive voice is that this is being led by No. 10 North, rather than the Department, but of course we are contributing to it. No. 10 North is looking at the legislative issues, and at the powers, responsibilities and budgets. We are, rightly, one part of that picture. The whole point is to make it a cross-Government focus on rewiring the state.

Sir Jim Mackey: May I add to that? Often with this sort of partnership thing, we default to the better care fund being the solution. It is generally the first tool we go to in the box. That is a useful challenge we should take away, to make sure that section 75s are appropriate—

Chair: That was a specific recommendation we made to you, so I am pleased that you are coming around.

Dr Gardner: Co-commissioning would really help, I have to say. You mentioned devolution, so I will again refer to my experience. The ICB in Staffordshire was due to merge with the ICB in Shropshire. We might have had queries as to whether that made sense for us, in terms of our health economy, particularly in North Staffordshire, but that was halted. So they lost about 50% of their staff and then, because we did not know what we were doing about devolution, they were merged. In Staffordshire, we have now been told that we are going to have a Staffordshire mayoral strategic authority, which is not coterminous with what was proposed for the ICB reorganisation. Likewise—this will be brought up by my colleagues—we still have the issue with NHS England and how we do the regional offices. Where are we at with the ICB mergers and the number of ICBs? Are you confident that the 50% cuts in ICBs will mean that they have the capacity needed to carry out all their functions and missions? Originally, they were going to have a whole load, but some have been taken off them. Where does the enforcement rule sit in there, because I am worried that that responsibility is being a bit diffused?

Yvette Cooper: The first thing is that we want to get to coterminosity. I think that having the same boundaries for different services is far more effective for local delivery of public services than not. That is being worked through at the moment, which is why we have paused some of the ICB work—in order to align with the mayors and the approach to strategic authorities across the rest of government. If we can get that prize and much better coterminosity, in some areas—depending on the local government geography—that might mean an ICB covering two areas. It might be different in different areas, because the point about having the ICBs was to have a big enough footprint to be able to do effective commissioning of services, but that is what we are currently working through. I think that, with boundary alignment, it just makes it so much harder for local agencies if they do not have that.

Dr Gardner: I would argue, though, that it gets challenging. Again, in Staffordshire, if you are in East Staffordshire, you will go over to the Derby hospitals; if you are South Staffordshire, you go into the Birmingham hospitals, obviously; and if you are North Staffordshire and indeed Cheshire, you come into the Royal Stoke. Boundaries are a little—

Yvette Cooper: Yes. It is harder because of patient referral patterns in the health service; it is not as simple as having the same historical local government patterns. That is exactly what we are having to work through, recognising that complexity. If organisations can work in partnership, it is much more effective, but you are totally right that the acute service pattern—

Dr Gardner: Do we have a timeline for when my poor ICB is going to get an answer?

Yvette Cooper: The next steps will be the devolution White Paper. Once we have the devolution White Paper, which has brought together the cross-government approach, we will set out the next steps.

Chair: Okay. I am sure we will come back to this.

Joe Robertson: I am going to stick with mental health and will refer to the Professor Fonagy independent review. In the broadest terms, are you satisfied that the money is in the NHS budget to deliver a better system for improving mental health and neurodevelopmental care, particularly in the community?

Yvette Cooper: We have a record £16.3 billion of investment going into NHS mental health spending now. I think we have to be able to use that investment as effectively as possible.
