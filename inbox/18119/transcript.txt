Witnesses: Chris Haslam and Vicky Philpott.

Q1 Chair: Welcome to this meeting of the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Select Committee. Today we have the first session of our inquiry into tourism. The first panel is Chris Haslam, the Chief Travel Writer of The Sunday Times, and Vicky Philpott, also known as VickyFlipFlop, who is a travel blogger and content creator. You are both very welcome. Thank you so much for joining us today. Before we begin, I remind members to declare any interests at the point that you ask your questions.

I will kick us off, starting with Chris. Do you think that tourists’ expectations and priorities have changed very much in recent years?

Chris Haslam: Generally speaking, all kinds of tourists, globally or just in the UK?

Chair: The UK.

Chris Haslam: Habits have changed. The 14-night holidays are a thing of the past. The average length of a holiday now is about 9.6 days. We are seeing people trading down. Expenditure and duration have been falling away ever since 2021 when tourism peaked post-pandemic. People are trading down—either spending less time in destinations or less time and staying in cheaper properties in destinations.

We have seen a move, across Europe and in the UK, from traditional accommodation—hotels, guesthouses, B&Bs—into short-term rentals, known as STRs, the Airbnb type thing. Within a very short time, that has grown to take probably 20% to 25% of the market. Hotels and traditional accommodation is now around 40% to 45%.

Let me just look at my notes to see what else has changed in the past few years. I have said about downsizing and shifting away. Funnily enough, something across Europe now that gives you an indication of how tight spending is and how little disposable income people have—it is not just the UK; I heard it in Spain as well—is people self-catering in hotels. Cleaning staff go into hotel rooms and find supermarket bags in the UK, in Spain, and last week I heard about it in Switzerland as well.

The general indication is that there is much less money around but the joke is that people, particularly the British, will put the holiday before the kidney replacement. They will always prioritise travel but they are having to cut their cloth to suit their budgets.

Q2 Chair: Vicky, what are you seeing? Are you noticing that people’s priorities for their travel are changing? Do they prioritise experiences over destinations? What are people prioritising when they go on their holidays?

Vicky Philpott: I think that as a priority they want a new experience that they can maybe put on their social media and they can talk about, something they have never done before. It is not really going to somewhere known and just lying on the beach. They want to be out doing things, entertaining the children with whatever they can, going to the coolest cafes and more interesting, adventurous travel.

Q3 Chair: To what extent do you think the UK destinations have responded to these changing priorities and, to Chris’s point, the changing finances?

Vicky Philpott: A lot of attractions and places are making them more visually interesting and having activations there where you can take Instagram photos and tag them, ways to share the experience easily and things that you have to do when you go to a destination. I think they are trying to add to the experiences there by creating Instagramable-visual things to do there.

Q4 Chair: Chris, expanding on what you said earlier about the pressures on finances and the changing patterns of how people are spending their money, how successfully do you think UK destinations have adapted to these changes?

Chris Haslam: I don’t think they have really adapted to it at all. They do not know whether this is a permanent change or a temporary change. Everyone is hoping for the best that people will choose more. Adding to what I said about changing habits, my area of expertise is the British coast, the UK seaside, and overnight stays in the British seaside have dropped by 18% in the past 10 years. Not only that, but the average duration of short breaks has gone from 3.3 days down to 2.9 days. I just wanted to add that in answer to your previous question.

On the industry’s preparedness for shorter durations, shorter stays, they are not ready for it, but I want to make clear that this is not uniquely British. This is exactly the same across Europe. There is an article in the Spanish trade press this morning talking about how hotels have been hit by the fact that people are spending so little money in hotels. They are not going to restaurants and spas. They are not buying all those extra things. They are not doing it in the UK either. The problem is if you are going to offer spas and 24-hour dining, you have to have the staff there, so you are not taking the money but you are still spending on the provision.

Q5 Chair: That sounds unsustainable in the long term. Good news for you: at least two Committee members represent coastal potential tourist destinations, Bayo and me, so we can liaise with you later.

Vicky, quite a lot of what you do talks about how families can still go away even though they might have financial constraints as the cost of living pressures are mounting up on them. What do you see that families prioritise when they are deciding whether a holiday offers good value for money?

Vicky Philpott: I think it is the overall experience and the cost of the overall experience. As Chris said, people are prioritising self-catering because obviously that can save a lot of money. Then there is a whole movement online of travel hacks and money-saving travel hacks, like booking a hotel room and making meals with your hotel kettle, and just ways that people can save on things like that so that they can spend it on experiences and getting out.

Chair: Thank you. Let me turn to my fellow coastal MP.

Q6 Mr Bayo Alaba: Good morning to you both. My first question is to both of you. What determines whether UK residents will choose domestic holiday destinations rather than overseas locations?

Chris Haslam: I will tell you what the facts are, what the statistics show. The average British family—and that is husband, wife and two kids—takes about three to four holidays a year. There will be the big summer trip, almost always the southern Med or maybe Florida or the US, but it is generally an overseas trip. They will also take another trip during the year, possibly at half-term, which is either going to be short-haul Europe or the UK coast. They will then take one or two further breaks during the year, which could be a weekend away in the UK or a city break. That is the general model at the moment. The average spend for all that, according to things like GfK and ABTA data, is about £7,500. I don’t believe that. I think it is a lot more than that now.

Going back to your question about what makes them choose, the one driver with the British holidaymaker is value: the money, the cost. The permanent constant problem with UK holidays is the high cost. For example, if you wanted to go away to a famous four star hotel in Cornwall at May half-term next year, that will cost you £1,020 per person B&B. You can have a week all-inclusive in the Algarve for £960 or a week all-inclusive in the Costa de Almería in Spain for £678. It is not competitive.

Bear in mind that I just said that was £1,020 per person B&B. If you add a modest £30 a day per person for lunch, dinner, drinks, ice creams, things like that, that takes the full price up to £1,200, so it is twice the price of going to Spain. That is one of the biggest drivers and it increasingly makes the glamorous side of the British coast, the north Cornish coast, the south Devon coast, more and more a middle-class destination.

Mr Bayo Alaba: You did not mention the Essex coast, but we will come to that in a second.

Chris Haslam: I can talk about that.

Q7 Mr Bayo Alaba: Vicky, would you like to add to that?

Vicky Philpott: I think the cost of holidays in school holidays is escalating out of control. A holiday point I was looking at was £700 for four nights at the start of May but then in the May half-term it was three and a half times as much at £2,386. That is just for basic lodging, no food or anything like that. That is making it totally prohibitive for people to go on holiday.

Q8 Mr Bayo Alaba: I represent Southend, a beautiful part of the country, very historic, but, as you mentioned, over a generation or so it has lost out to international travel. You mentioned that your experience and speciality is coastal communities. What should communities like Southend do to increase their appeal to the tourism market?

Chris Haslam: I am going to make a position statement here. I have travelled almost everywhere. I can’t tell you how many countries I have been to in my job, so I am in a position of authority when I say that the British coast is the most beautiful on earth, without doubt the most beautiful, and I have seen almost everywhere. I have not been to Chechnya but I do not think they have a coast.

The other thing you need to bear in mind is that the great British seaside is almost a figment of our imagination. If you look at the history of the British seaside, it was developed for short-term gain. You need a lot of money to maintain those massive Victorian buildings with all that wrought iron, all that decoration and grandeur. That money is not there and it never was there during the 12-week season. The season is longer now than it was at the height of the British seaside. It was never built to last and the idea that we go back to what we had is ludicrous.

That said, what can Southend do? I think there are two very different towns to look at: Bude in north Cornwall and Great Yarmouth in Norfolk. Great Yarmouth was a moribund classic Victorian resort with piers and all the attractions that you would expect, winter gardens, in a state of decay, near collapse. It was not quite up to HMO level but it was getting there. Bude, on the other hand, is a little country town on the north coast of Cornwall.

Great Yarmouth has benefited from external funding. First off, its council has gone for every grant it can get from the Government—the small town scheme, the community trust scheme, heritage funding. It has gone for everything, but it also has a big income stream from offshore energy, renewables. It has a lot of money and it is transforming that place. It is astonishing how it has grown in the past two years. That is one way that you can change fortune.

The cheaper way to change fortune is to engage the community, and Bude is the exemplar of that. If you have ever been on a British beach and seen the #2MinuteBeachClean stand, there will be a litter picker and some bags. That was an idea that came out of Bude. Bude is the only place, apart from South Shields—it is one of the two places in the country—that has a coastal parliament made up of citizens who make decisions about what their town needs to survive and thrive. That is all community engagement. It is almost the polar opposite to the Great Yarmouth model using external funding. It is from within and it has succeeded. I write The Sunday Times beach guide and it won beach of the year this year, and that is a tough competition that Southend has never won.

Q9 Mr Bayo Alaba: Yes, it is interesting. Southend is definitely one of the fastest growing seaside towns for day visitors but not overnight, which is something that we have touched on. This takes me to my question. Vicky, you were talking about the experience being very important, and that is quite big for my constituency of Southend because of its growing day visitor base. What should UK destinations do to increase the experience? You spoke about the visual elements. What else should they be doing to attract families?

Vicky Philpott: I have lived in Portsmouth for the last nine years. I moved there to be by the sea, because I am from the midlands. Something that Portsmouth does really well is to have so many events every weekend, especially through the summer. People are coming down to join in the music festivals, food festivals, car festivals. There is all sorts going on throughout the summer and I think that helps to bring in people. One of our main festivals is Victorious, and a lot of people come and are so impressed by Southsea and Portsmouth that they come back. I have evidence of this.

Events like that are important to bring people in because they might not necessarily have thought of Portsmouth or they might think of it in years gone by when it was not the most attractive place to go, but now they have done a lot work on the seafront and it is looking good. There is a lot to do there. The Portsmouth marine dockyard is the biggest attraction but then it is nice just being there. It is a very arty place, coffee shops, so people want to come back. After they have been to these festivals, they want to come back and see more.

Chair: And then pop over to Gosport, of course.

Q10 Mr Bayo Alaba: The Government are quite keen for regional mayors to have more say. There is talk about overnight visitor levies. What is your view, your position, on visitor levies for coastal communities, or even for any tourist attraction area?

Vicky Philpott: I think if you can directly see that it is benefiting where you are staying, then great, but if the money just disappears—maybe if there was a way for it to pay for something tangible that you can see and say, “This is what we got from the levy” rather than just saying it was distributed.

Chris Haslam: I don’t think I ever seen any evidence of tourism levies making the slightest difference to visitor intent. They come anyway. No one is not going to go to St Ives because they have to pay £5 extra a night. They will still go. If anything, tourism levies are way too low to make any difference and I think they could go a lot higher. You could probably start charging people to get into museums as well, because they are coming.

Q11 Mr Bayo Alaba: My last question is: if there was an opportunity for communities to have package offers—train, hotel accommodation and so on, maybe even into one or two attractions—is that something that communities or towns or cities could do to support their local communities? I know how that is administered could be a challenge but is it something that communities should be thinking about?

Vicky Philpott: It is an interesting idea. Every large city has something like the London Pass, so maybe they could work together and create something, maybe offer vouchers to help consumers. I think that would work.

Chris Haslam: I am interested that you say that because the systemic challenge faced by domestic travel in the UK is that it is up against a mature, efficient, highly polished industry that takes people, puts them on planes and delivers them by bus to the door of a hotel with a water park in it and it is all inclusive. That is the outbound travel agent, but if you look in the window of any travel agency in the UK and see how many UK offers are there, there might be a coach trip to go and see “Mamma Mia” but there is no equivalent. UK domestic is up against this huge machine of the travel industry. It does not have the equivalent.

That is the same in other countries, but in Spain, for example, Turespaña has been massively successful at promoting Spain to Spanish people. It created the España Verde campaign, which completely transformed Galicia, Asturias and the Basque Country and turned that into a massive tourist attraction. That is the equivalent of finding a new Cornwall in the UK. Andalucía is always popular with everybody but it had a campaign called Andalucía te quiere, bringing people from all over Spain.

I do not know if it is VisitEngland or VisitBritain—I do not know what they do. I do not hear from them ever, and I am in the industry. I think it is about stronger representation—if it is not going to come from the private sector, it will have to come from the public sector—of British tourism attractions to the British people, something like the English Tourist Board. That is my answer.

In answer to packages, yes, definitely. There is only one all-inclusive hotel in the UK. It is on the Norfolk coast or Suffolk coast, called Hopton’s, near Lowestoft. Packages is a very good idea. I do not know how it would work, though.

Q12 Jeff Smith: Good morning. Before I ask my questions, I was very interested in what you said, Chris, about the Bude and Great Yarmouth examples. Are there others that we can look at where they have managed the transition from the traditional pier arcade?

Chris Haslam: Amble in Northumberland was a derelict former coal port and the community took the harbour over and turned it into a fabulous tourist attraction. It started with a coffee shop and it is great now. If you get hold of old pictures of Amble from maybe 20 years ago and look at the high street going down to the harbour and now look at the way prosperity has radiated back up the high street, you see that it has gone from being pound shops to boutiques and there is a deli. It is almost like Southwold. Amble is a good one. Hastings is another good one. It turned a huge derelict department store into a community art centre. It puts on gigs there and there are craft breweries. Again, it is the community.

Bournemouth’s south beach is also a very good example. It was a neglected beach at the far end. Bournemouth is one of the most beautiful beaches in Europe if treated properly by those who look after it, the council. South beach renovated a patch of neglected beach. They put a double-decker bus down there and turned it into a café. They were holding classes for children, for veterans, for the lonely, for widows. It became a proper community asset, used by the whole community, and brought tourists into the beach.

Now the lease has run out and the council is saying, “You have to go. We are going to give someone else the job.” They do not mind that. They do not mind going if someone else comes straight in but it could take two years, at which time south beach just collapses again.

Chris Haslam: No.

Q14 Jeff Smith: Thank you. The questions I was going to ask you are about how people discover the places they choose to go on holiday. What has the biggest influence on a tourist’s choice of destination?

Vicky Philpott: I think it will be what they have seen on social media, places going viral. It used to be the Lonely Planet effect where Lonely Planet would mention something but now if it has gone on TikTok, everyone else starts going.

Chris Haslam: Definitely social media, definitely TikTok, also traditional media. Some 24% of our unique views on The Sunday Times are for UK destinations, which is extraordinary because we cover the entire world. There is a lot of interest from that, particularly in new hotels.

Q15 Jeff Smith: That is quite a change in just the very recent years from traditional travel journalism and official tourism promotion to social media. Should the UK, VisitBritain, just get loads of influencers in to promote the UK destinations?

Vicky Philpott: I think a lot of TikTok and Instagram is what has come naturally, not necessarily the tourist boards putting their information out. It is organic posts that randomly do really well rather than a targeted thing, but obviously they should do that as well. They do include influencers and invite influencers on trips and invite them to try new places out, but I think it is quite hard to make something go viral.

Q16 Jeff Smith: It seems that everything now is influenced by AI. Have you seen any influence from AI in the travel industry?

Vicky Philpott: I think it is quite negative.

Jeff Smith: How does it work?

Chris Haslam: I will give you a very quick example. A guy at the WTM two years ago told me to choose a random travel video from YouTube, and it turned out to be a German having a long weekend in London. We put that URL into an AI travel agent called Mindtrip and it scanned it and came back with exactly the same itinerary, where to stay, where to go, “Tower Bridge? This is the time to get there.” Not only that, going through the user’s Google records to find out what shops he goes to, what food he buys, it recommended restaurants and hotels. It was absolutely personalised.

That was terrifying to see but, that said, travel agents talk about it all the time. At every conference you go to, there is a module on AI: is it going to steal our jobs? They have either got their heads in the sand or they are being extremely sanguine but they are saying no because you are always going to need humans behind AI. They are seeing it as a tool that travel agents can use rather than something that would make travel agents redundant.

Q17 Jeff Smith: Do you think it is the latter?

Chris Haslam: When I first started in this industry 29 years ago, I was told to write an article called “The travel brochure is dead” and you still get travel brochures. They are still making the glossy brochures. No, I don’t think it will put travel agents out of business but I think they will have to adapt.

Q18 Jeff Smith: Vicky, do you use it? You probably use it in your writing.

Vicky Philpott: I have used it for research, but I am of the stance that people will always want people. I think that is why social media is so influential because people see actual people like them doing the trips that they want to do and then they can recreate it rather than just typing it in and an AI avatar telling you what to do. I would not follow that information. I would want a real person.

Q19 Jeff Smith: Do either of you know of any examples in other countries where they have successfully harnessed social media to promote their destinations?

Chris Haslam: Dubai. Remember during the pandemic? That backfired a little bit because no one else could travel. Malaysia is good at it. I do not think anyone else is good.

Jeff Smith: That is helpful. Thank you.

Q20 Chair: Chris, given the rise of creator-led content on travel, is there still a place for travel journalism and travel journalists like you? What do you bring to the table that would not be there otherwise?

Chris Haslam: We see a lot of suspicion and fatigue in our readers of the whole notion of influencers and of a certain amount of travel writing as well. If we think something is rubbish, we will say it is rubbish. That has changed. In the past, not so much at my newspaper but in other traditional media, there was the idea that if you can’t say anything nice about it, don’t write it, but that has changed now because the public need it.

Chair: Yes, you tell it like it is.

Chris Haslam: You have to tell it like it is.

Q27 Dr Rupa Huq: Is England not aggressively marketed? An aggressive marketing campaign would not be enough because the stuff is not up to scratch?

Chris Haslam: You are right: it is not quite up to scratch. It is very seasonal. A lot of our seaside towns are beautiful on a summer’s day, but as soon as the sun goes in in wintertime, they are just ghastly. We have an issue with the British seaside in that because it declined as an industry from the late 1960s onwards due to the rise of the package holiday, many of our seaside towns have become retirement homes because the property was cheap and people could move to the seaside; the British dream to retire to the seaside.

However, there is no youth there. The young people have had to move away to the cities. Bude is different because people always come back to Bude, but that is what makes it unique. Most places they leave, they do not come back. So you have a place that is effectively a retirement colony. The season is only 12 weeks long. The industry is all focused on making as much money as you can in the season and not investing.

If you think of those huge Victorian properties along the seafront in Bognor or Eastbourne: the cost of maintaining those is staggering. Who is going to take on a great big building and open it as a seaside hotel? In Cromer, Norfolk, we have the Hotel de Paris, opened in 1888 by a Parisian hotelier who swore the future was in the British seaside. It is now almost derelict.

The southern Med has serious issues going forward. Look at any climate science you like, but if you take World Weather Attribution or CRS, the prediction is that by the middle of this century one in two summers will be detrimental to health. We will have blisteringly hot summers detrimental to health, with a 50% frequency. It is supposed to be one in 10 now, although it is not.

We have already seen something called coolcations; everyone talks about coolcations now. They do not really exist in the UK yet, but the idea is that people flee the summer heat to the northern climes, to get away from the heat and enjoy it. We talk about people going to Finland, Lapland, but the UK is in the perfect position to grasp that opportunity and to profit from it. We have the seaside resorts; we have properties within the seaside resorts. When we see the migration of people from the south, people from Spain, Italy and Greece will be coming on holiday in the UK, Ireland, Netherlands, Scandinavia.
