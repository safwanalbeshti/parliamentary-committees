Examination of witness

Witness: Rt Hon James Wolffe.

Chair: Good afternoon, and welcome to this meeting of the Scottish Affairs Committee—our second meeting of the day. This afternoon we are resuming our inquiry into the dual role of the Lord Advocate. We are very pleased to have our witness here this afternoon. Would you like to introduce yourself and what you do?

James Wolffe: My name is James Wolffe. I was the Lord Advocate between 2016 and 2021. I currently have a variety of professional, charitable and other things that I do.

I need not tell you that the Lord Advocate is responsible for a number of functions fundamental to the maintenance of the rule of law in Scotland. Those functions could be distributed and organised in other ways. Various models are available and no institution should be immune from critical review. As Dr McCormick explained to you last week, all approaches to the organisation of the functions that in Scotland are held by the Lord Advocate involve trade-offs.

There are some significant strengths in the current system. If reform is to be proposed, we should be clear eyed about those strengths and the complexities and trade-offs that would be involved in moving to an alternative model. We should do it with absolute confidence that the current system for the investigation and prosecution of crime in Scotland is, has been and remains robustly independent of political interference. I accordingly welcome the opportunity that your inquiry offers to reflect on these issues and I welcome the commission that the Scottish Government has given to Lord Boyd of Duncansby to review the office.

As you know, Chair, I am giving evidence in an entirely personal capacity and there are some limitations on what I can say. The first is that I can only speak directly to my own time in office, and that is increasingly becoming historical. Like all professional lawyers, I am subject to obligations of confidentiality. I currently sit in a judicial capacity in both the UK and the Channel Islands, and you would not expect me to take a position on issues of live political controversy.

I have thought about the nature of this office. I have reflected on it in light of my own experience of occupying it for a period. I hope that there is plenty that I can say that will be helpful to you and your Committee as you deal with the important issues that are central to your remit.

Chair: Thank you very much. That is a very helpful introduction. Based on your time as Lord Advocate, Mr Wolffe, what are the main advantages of having the senior legal adviser to the Government and the prosecutor combined in the way that they are?

James Wolffe: It is perhaps worth stepping back and thinking about the constitutional place of the office holder. The Lord Advocate is appointed by the monarch on the recommendation of the First Minister, with the approval of the Scottish Parliament. As Lord Advocate, you immediately acquire the functions of that office, notably the responsibilities as head of the system of prosecution and investigation of deaths, and a raft of statutory functions that sit with the Lord Advocate and the Lord Advocate alone, which are not functions of the Scottish Ministers. The Lord Advocate also, by virtue of the Scotland Act, is a Scottish Minister and, under the ministerial code, is given functions as the Government’s senior law officer. I might come back to the distinction between law officer and legal adviser. Perhaps there is a subtlety there.

Also, it is possible for the Lord Advocate to exercise other functions of the Scottish Ministers. The most significant of those is the ministerial responsibility the Lord Advocate holds for the Civil Recovery Unit, the system of civil confiscation of assets, which is a Scottish Minister’s function, although certainly in my time—I think it is still the case—was given to the Lord Advocate in terms of ministerial oversight and accountability.

The starting point is that you inhabit this role as Lord Advocate. You have a number of really significant independent functions relating to the investigation and prosecution of crime and related issues, a number of functions within Government, and statutory functions under the Scotland Act, which are functions that sit only with the Lord Advocate and are of a constitutional nature. They are intended to be part of the complex series of checks and balances to make sure that legislation of the Scottish Parliament and acts of the Scottish Ministers are conducted within that competence.

In terms of the advantages of this combined set of functions, almost all of which are related in one way or another to the rule of law and the administration of justice in Scotland—they have a sort of common core—first, it secures ministerial oversight of the system of prosecution and investigation of deaths. It secures ministerial oversight that is accountable to the representatives of the people of Scotland through the Scottish Parliament. It maintains a strong voice for rule of law values within Government. I will take the first two and then I might say something about the third.

It is quite an important point—sometimes lost sight of—that the Lord Advocate has ministerial oversight of the system of prosecution and system of investigation of deaths in Scotland. It is not an administrative or management role; it is a ministerial role. The individuals appointed to the office, whatever their professional background, come into that as a ministerial role. They do not expect to stay there for ever. They exercise the kinds of functions that you would expect Ministers to exercise in terms of oversight of the system.

The second advantage is that it is a Minister who is accountable through the Scottish Parliament. You can have models for the prosecution of crime where the prosecutors are treated like judges in a siloed, institutional system and where the opportunity for accountability is correspondingly limited. That is a model that could be considered. If you are looking for advantages of our current model, it is that the Lord Advocate is accountable to the Scottish Parliament.

The reason why that is important is that, like most European systems, but quite differently from England and Wales, in Scotland all public prosecutions are undertaken by the system for which the Lord Advocate is constitutionally responsible. It is a total system, if I put it that way, with a single prosecutorial agency. Other public bodies cannot prosecute in Scotland. As you will know, private prosecutions are virtually unknown, for a variety of reasons, in Scotland. Equally, like most European systems, there is a very close working relationship between prosecutors and police, where the police ultimately respond and act under the directions of prosecutors.

The Lord Advocate is in a position, as the person with ministerial oversight for the whole system, to set the framework of policy within which individual decisions are made in relation to the investigation and prosecution of crime, and in particular to set prosecution policy within which individual prosecutors exercise their functions. Ultimately, the apex of that policy is the public Scottish prosecution code, which you can see.

The strength of that approach is that the Lord Advocate can take a view across the whole system, both horizontally and vertically, in terms of the policy framework. It is entirely right that someone exercising those functions should be accountable for their exercise and for the systems for which the individuals are responsible.

I do not need to tell you that there are a variety of mechanisms of accountability. In my own time, when there was a question asked of me, I attended at portfolio questions to answer questions in the Chamber of the Scottish Parliament. I responded to urgent questions when I had to go to Parliament to deal with those. I participated in debates about prosecutorial matters. Early in my time, the Justice Committee of the Scottish Parliament had an inquiry into the prosecution system and there was a debate following that. I made statements to Parliament about a variety of issues. The mechanisms of accountability are there.

My own view, based on my own experience in office, is that that accountability is not just about the asking of questions and the making of statements. It is the opportunity that it gives for parliamentarians to raise issues with an office holder who has ministerial responsibility for the system, who may then be expected to look into the issues that are raised.

An example in my own time was that parliamentarians raised issues in relation to the functioning of the system for investigation of deaths in Scotland and the time that those investigations took, among other things. I like to think I might have come across it spontaneously, but it was a response to interest from parliamentarians that led me to look closely at that part of the system. I was satisfied that, as it then operated, it was underfunded and not responding in the way the public should reasonably expect. I initiated a fairly radical reform of the way that the Crown exercised that function and secured the funding to resource that part of the Crown’s work. That is a practical example of the way that accountability can feed through into the functioning of the system.

I mentioned the budget. It is not a trivial point that the Lord Advocate negotiates the Crown’s budget. I formed a view, about a year into office, that the Crown was under-resourced and needed additional resources. There were a number of issues, including investigation of deaths, the management of serious and complex cases, and aspects of the way that sexual offences were being handled, that could only be addressed with additional resources. I made a case to Ministers for the additional resources, secured them and was able to engage in reform. That is a practical example of the way that the systems of ministerial responsibility and accountability interact. I can come back to the strong voice for the rule of law in government, if that would be helpful. I am conscious that I have been talking rather longer than perhaps you expected.

Chair: That is okay. It has been interesting. I would be interested to know whether the dual role is advantageous in terms of policy development, or whether you feel that that is something that is not helpful.

James Wolffe: In my own experience, there are two positive aspects of it. I should say that, in terms of the Lord Advocate’s policy-making role for systems for which the Lord Advocate is responsible, the Lord Advocate has a policy unit within the Crown Office, so has her own policy officials who assist her with that.

Because I was appointed on a non-party political basis, I took quite a firm view that, as a general proposition, policy matters were for political Ministers and the law was for me. The one exception to that is when you get to criminal justice policy, where the Lord Advocate has a prosecutorial interest. There is a departmental interest to contribute to the development of justice policy. By the nature of the office holders, they will be senior lawyers with experience of the system, who may well have something useful to say, assisted by their officials in Crown Office, on criminal justice policy. There is a closer engagement with the development of justice policy, and, in that role, there is scope for, in a sense, the prosecutorial and administrative justice view to be brought into the room.

It also goes the other way, but it goes the other way in a very subtle way. All systems that grapple with the problem that you are grappling with have to find a way to align prosecutorial decision making and prosecutorial policy with the general criminal justice policy of the Government of the day, who are accountable through the normal mechanisms. They have to do so in a way that defends the independence of decision making in individual cases.

If you look at continental systems, at least in some you have a very independent prosecutor role. You often have an aspect of the system where the Minister of Justice has powers to direct the chief prosecutor. That power of direction is the mechanism in those systems to ensure that there is a consistency between prosecutorial work and general criminal justice policy. For my own part, a system in this country where the Justice Minister gives directions to the Lord Advocate is certainly not one that I would be inclined to favour.

We have the Lord Advocate, who exercises those functions entirely independently and is in a position within Government to defend that independence. If any Minister ever wanted to interfere with prosecutorial decision making, I am confident that any Lord Advocate I have known—I have known Lord Advocates back to Lord Mackay of Clashfern—would have told them pretty robustly to get their tanks off the Lord Advocate’s lawn.

At the same time as having the independent prosecution of crime, the Lord Advocate is within Government and may well have been involved in discussions about criminal justice policy. While the Lord Advocate will, independently of any other person, make decisions about prosecutorial policy, thinking about what is right from a prosecution of crime point of view, they can be expected to at least have in mind the general criminal justice policy of the Government of which she or he is a member.

There is an interesting and quite subtle feature of our system that achieves those two things in a way that is different from other systems, which, having inhabited the role, I think has something to recommend it, recognising of course all the issues that you have been thinking about.

Kirsteen Sullivan: I was interested in your question, Chair, around policy development. Equally, I was very interested in your response of leaving policy to the politicians, apart from when it comes into the sphere of justice policy. I can see the logic in that.

I am wondering about where the Government decide on particular policies where there could perhaps be legal concerns. The one that springs to mind—apologies, I do not know whether you were there at the time, so I am sure you will tell me what you can and cannot answer—is the deposit return scheme. As it transpired, there were some legal issues around that. I am just using that as an example of where there was a distinct policy position, but perhaps some legal issues and concerns that had to be explored. If it was in another area but there were similar concerns, would that be an area where you would have expected to have input?

James Wolffe: Yes, absolutely. It might be helpful if I make a couple of general points. I will not speak to the individual case that you have mentioned.

Kirsteen Sullivan: It is an example.

James Wolffe: It is a useful example. My own view is that good policy making involves policy officials engaging with Government lawyers, because you can only make good policy within the framework of understanding what the relevant legal framework is. That is particularly important in a set of devolved institutions, where the powers of both the Government and the Parliament are constrained by the Scotland Act, but it is important for any area of policy development.

Policy officials have to understand what the state of the current law is. They have to work out whether the policy objective can be achieved without needing to change the law. If they need to change the law, they need to discuss with their lawyers what a change in the law would look like. Good policy development always involves interaction between policy officials and Government lawyers. The Scottish Government has a cadre of Government lawyers who fulfil that legal function.

If you look at the Scottish ministerial code, it tells you that the Lord Advocate, with the Solicitor General for Scotland, is responsible for the legal advice to Scottish Ministers. Internally, the law officers are fulfilling what is the classical law officer function in Government. You are taking ultimate responsibility for the legal position that the Government adopt and you are at the apex of the quite large bit of the machine that is thinking about law rather than policy. In most cases, that policy development work goes on in the normal way. It goes up to Ministers.

It might be that it throws up a particularly difficult issue, and that might well escalate to the law officers. The ministerial code gives some examples of cases where that might happen and the law officers would personally deal with it, with the support of an exceptional but very small group of lawyers, headed by the legal secretary to the Lord Advocate, who helps the Lord Advocate with that part of the role. Equally, if Government lawyers are concerned that their advice is not being listened to, they are free, and in fact the ministerial code requires them, to escalate the issue to the law officers.

Part of the law officer role within Government is to, so far as any system can, make sure that legal considerations are properly being taken into account in the policy-making process. Often when you are developing new policy you are at the edges of what is known about the law. You are often dealing with areas that have never been tested in court. There may be debates internally about just what the law is. Ultimately, if it is a serious issue, the law officers are, as it were, the last word within Government. Of course what the Government does ultimately can be tested in the courts.

Sometimes, as with any human system, we get it wrong. Sometimes, as I say, we are in areas where the law is genuinely doubtful and contestable. The law officers make their best judgment on the basis of their experience and the advice that they have as to where the boundaries are. History shows that it is a pretty robust system.

Sorry, that is quite a long answer to your question. One can focus on individual cases, but what is really important is that there is a whole machinery within Government to make sure, as the ministerial code requires, that all policy is properly informed by legal advice. The law officers sit at the apex of that and ultimately can intervene. They have the power to call in any particular issue if they think that it warrants their personal attention.

One really important aspect of the role, and it is one that needs to be given as much attention as the other, is the role that the law officers have in assisting the Government to live up to their ministerial code commitment to act lawfully. Part of the job of a law officer is to say, “No, Minister, you cannot do that”, if you have to. Within that boundary, it is for policy Ministers, who are accountable to Parliament and elected, to make the political judgments. If they can carry the democratic mandate of Parliament to support the policy, that is democracy in action and it is certainly not for a lawyer to get in the way of that, but, if it is a matter of law, it is for the law officers.

Kenneth Stevenson: Based on your experience as Lord Advocate, what are the main risks or disadvantages that come with the law officers having a dual role?

James Wolffe: The principal risk, and it perhaps is why we are here now, is the fact that the combination of functions, the way that they are exercised and the safeguards that are in place to ensure that particularly prosecution of crime goes ahead independently of political involvement and with integrity are not well understood. Those of us who have held the office probably have to take a responsibility if we have not always explained the role. It is not well understood and gives rise to legitimate questions about how it is that you can combine these functions in a way that maintains the independence of the prosecution system, and with integrity.

It is not helped by the necessary fact that the law officer role in Government is almost invisible, for very good reasons to do with professional confidentiality and the law officers’ convention, which are all there to try to promote good government. It is less visible than the prosecutorial function.

I have also wondered, as I have reflected on this, whether it goes to the ability to communicate the way the role fits together. It is a point that Malcolm McMillan makes in his report. You have the Crown Office, which is institutionally separate from Government and has its own website. You have the Government, which have a brief account of the law officers. Nowhere can you go and find the office of the Lord Advocate. Perhaps not just a website, but there is not, as it were, an independent office of the Lord Advocate reflecting the constitutional role, which I described at the outset, where you can immediately see, “That is how this senior lawyer is able to combine these various functions”. That might or might not answer the concerns that people have expressed publicly and the issues that people raise.

In other systems, steps have been taken to disaggregate those functions precisely because of the concerns. At the end of the day, I am keen that people understand that there are strengths in the current system, but, if the view is taken that there needs to be change, so be it. That is the main issue.

It is perhaps really important to communicate that, if the concern is about the independence of the prosecution of crime from political interference, this Committee should be confident that the prosecution of crime in Scotland is, has been and continues to be independent and undertaken with integrity. I can explain some of the safeguards that are in place that should give this Committee and others confidence about that, if that would be helpful.

Chair: We are going to come to that.

Kenneth Stevenson: You have answered the questions I had. I was going to ask about the perception problem. Then you have spoken about the other risks of the independence of the system of public prosecution, so I think you answered it all at once.

James Wolffe: Dr Tickell made a point about the sheer scale of the role. Having inhabited it, it is an extraordinary role with a number of different facets. I held it through both Brexit and covid. There are always large things that come along.

In each of those roles, the incumbent is supported by officials, Government lawyers, legal secretariat and senior prosecutors. As I said at the outset, it is a ministerial role where you are sitting at the apex of a system. It is not the biggest of these. If you look internationally, there are comparator roles where there are a wider range of functions. Curiously, it does not seem to depend on the size of the country whether you have a law officer with a lot of functions or a law officer with much narrower functions. It is a perfectly legitimate issue to raise, but the system is capable of supporting office holders to exercise all of those functions.

Dave Doogan: What do you understand are the principal motivations of those who would wish to see the dual role split?

James Wolffe: I do not think it is for me to speculate about motivations. As I said in my opening statement, it is a perfectly legitimate set of questions. If there is a public concern, it is right that the issues are answered and addressed. It is important that, in addressing those issues, all of us are mindful—and I am not going to say any more on this—that we have a responsibility to support the administration of justice. As I said at the outset, this is all about accountability. There are legitimate questions about the combination of functions. There are legitimate questions and legitimate requests for assurance that there is no interference with the prosecution of crime.

I suspect that you are going to hear from a number of law officers. I would be astonished if they did not all look you in the eye and say, “It never happened on my watch and I would not tolerate it if any Minister tried to”. It is perfectly legitimate to ask law officers to give that assurance and that confidence. It is important that the questions are asked in the right way.

Dave Doogan: The reason I ask is that, in the limited evidence that we have taken so far, we have heard very sincere academic reasons to split the role and, similarly, equally sincere academic views that it should remain as in the status quo. I guess one of the concerns must be, in critiquing legitimately the status quo and perhaps also in pursuit of a splitting of the role, what the unintended consequences would possibly be. You have been very clear about the operational independence of the prosecutorial function, so that is not a gain. You are not going to gain that, because it is already baked into the system with safeguards. What could the unintended consequences be?

James Wolffe: You are absolutely right. Hiving off the system of prosecution so that it is entirely institutionally independent, as you say, retains the independence that it already has, but without the voice within Government of a senior Minister who can draw the line and defend that independence.

I worry a bit about the law officer role within Government, because it is pretty invisible and not well understood. Perhaps I would say this as a senior lawyer. It is really important that policy is well informed by legal considerations. In the context of a devolved system where you are operating within legal limits set by this Parliament, it is really important that those limits are properly analysed and thought about, and that Ministers, as committed in the ministerial code, act lawfully at all times. That is actually quite an important rule of law thing: that the Government, of all of us, act lawfully.

The role of a law officer in Government is not just to give legal advice to your client. That is a really important role. You are giving legal advice, and your client can accept or reject it. If you are the law officer in Government, you have that slightly more elevated responsibility of helping Ministers to navigate this pretty difficult, contested legal landscape and to do so in a way that respects the rule of law and the limits set down by this Parliament.

One risk is that, if you are not really clear about the importance of that function in Government and if you do not replace the current law officers, or if you do not replace them with a law officer who can carry the same weight within Government, the law officer role becomes weakened in some way. I would not for a moment suggest that that necessarily happens, but it is the bit that I would worry about.

Dave Doogan: You have been quite clear—correct me if I am wrong—that you have a supreme belief in the institutional and legal safeguards that protect public prosecutions in Scotland from political interference from Scottish Ministers. In your time as Lord Advocate, you never experienced any political interference in discharging your responsibilities as head of the Crown. That being the case, while you were in post, how was that system of public prosecutions reinforced, or was it reinforced during the time that you were the Lord Advocate? Perhaps it was not necessary.

James Wolffe: I have a slightly broader view of the safeguards. Your academic commentators last week gave you some good evidence on this. I hope that I can run through my list. The first point is that, on legal safeguards, the functions sit with the Lord Advocate as the holder of that ancient office that has held these functions since the great reforming Parliament of 1567. As a matter of constitutional propriety and under the explicit terms of the Scotland Act, the Lord Advocate is required to exercise them independently of any other person. They are not ministerial functions, so Ministers have no role. That is legal safeguards.

There are political safeguards, because the law officers and Ministers are accountable to Parliament. Experience has shown that parliamentarians are very quick to ask questions if cases arise. One arose in my time and one arose more recently that I think has prompted your inquiry. Parliamentarians are very quick to ask questions and seek assurances about the integrity of the system. There is political accountability, which is one of the safeguards.

I cannot speak to what political Ministers were thinking about it, but my own experience was that political Ministers understood those limits. If they did not, part of my role as the Lord Advocate within Government would be to make sure that Ministers understood where the boundaries were. If there ever were political interference, the political consequences ought to be severe. The 1924 Labour Government fell in essence because of a scandal relating to alleged political interference in a prosecutorial decision of the Attorney General.

There are legal safeguards, political safeguards and personal safeguards. Anyone appointed to the role is going to be a senior lawyer who brings their own personal reputation, integrity and independence into the office. My two predecessors were very distinguished senior prosecutors who had both been Solicitor General. I had been dean of the Faculty of Advocates for two years before I was appointed. My successors are both very distinguished King’s counsel. There are personal safeguards in the individuals who are appointed.

There are institutional safeguards because the Lord Advocate does not act in isolation. My own experience is that the Crown Office is an institution with an immensely strong professional ethic and commitment to its independence and the integrity of its decision making. The Solicitor General and the Crown Agent both hold independent offices, the Crown Agent in particular with his own responsibilities for the service. The decision making, in most cases, is undertaken by advocate deputes, who are senior lawyers who are given a commission where they can exercise all the powers of the Lord Advocate.

In any serious case, there is likely to be more than one prosecutor involved. If one thinks about the Branchform case, for example, I do not have personal direct knowledge, but all the decision making in that case will have been undertaken by a senior advocate depute who has his own professional ethic and independence. The very fact that the Lord Advocate is fulfilling an important role, but is fulfilling a role within an organisation that has a set of institutional safeguards and is peopled by professional prosecutors who are used to taking hard and difficult decisions, because that is where the evidence takes them and not for any other reason, is an important safeguard.

Dave Doogan: As a senior legal practitioner prior to being Lord Advocate, then having been Lord Advocate and now back in the law, as the dual role exists just now, is there anywhere that, if it was in your gift, you could introduce an additional safeguard? If so, what would that be? In your professional view and experience, is there any need for any additional safeguards, given all that you have just said?

James Wolffe: So far as I can see, the issue is one of public confidence. It is really important that the system of investigation and prosecution of crime commands public confidence. If the current system cannot command public confidence, you would need to look to change it. As, I suppose, an acutely informed observer, having been in the role, I find it hard to see why there should be a loss of public confidence, but that is for others to—

Dave Doogan: Is that the difference between an institutional or operational failure and the perception of an operational or institutional failure? Those two things are not necessarily the same thing. It has to be seen. People have to see an institution within which they can have confidence. It does not matter if the organisation is working in an extraordinarily high‑performing and robust way. If people suspect it might not be, that is a communication issue.

James Wolffe: Yes. That goes back to the comment I made earlier. There may be something around the way these roles are combined and the way that is reflected institutionally and publicly that one could think quite creatively about.

One thing that I have thought about reflecting back is that there is super support on the Crown Office side and super support on the Government side, but there is limited crossing across that boundary. When an issue arose, or arises, that brings both into quite sharp focus, there is something about there not being a visible office of the Lord Advocate that is seen to be separate from Government, under which the Lord Advocate exercises the Crown Office roles and under which the Lord Advocate also exercises the Government roles. It would not require a large office, but it would visibly reflect the particular constitutional feature of the office.

If one were making that reform, it would have one other possibly advantageous feature. Because the Lord Advocate is responsible for reserved crime as well as devolved crime, so terrorism and firearms, and the Lord Advocate has functions for extradition, which is a reserved matter, the Lord Advocate has to have a relationship with UK Government.

I am speculating, but it might be helpful, in recognising, as it were, the totality of the role and the relationship that the Lord Advocate has to have with UK Government as well as with Scottish Government on the prosecutorial side, for there to be a more visible acknowledgement and to embrace the fact that the office combines all these functions and that these functions all tie together. They are all about maintaining the rule of law in Scotland.

Susan Murray: This is clarification, I think. You have just said that everything that the Lord Advocate does is subject to political scrutiny. Does that mean that the advice and guidance of the Lord Advocate is transparent and public, in order that it can be subject to political scrutiny? Are there times when that advice is private and therefore cannot be subject to political scrutiny?

James Wolffe: It is maybe important to separate out accountability and the requirement for the Lord Advocate to go to the Scottish Parliament, answer questions and explain the system, if they need to explain the system, and what the Lord Advocate can say on any individual case. I am going to separate out the Lord Advocate’s prosecutorial functions, where the Lord Advocate is constitutionally responsible to Parliament for those prosecutorial functions. For reasons that you will well understand, any limits are around the fact that you cannot disclose necessarily the inner workings of a particular investigation or prosecution. The Lord Advocate accounts to Parliament for the system and there are constraints on what you can say about individual cases.

On the Government side, it is different. On the Government side, most of what the Lord Advocate does is of the nature of legal advice. It is constrained by well-recognised professional obligations of confidentiality and constitutional conventions in terms of the law officer role in decision making. Generally speaking, on the Government side it will be a policy Minister who answers for the policy and all the implications of the policy, including the legal implications.

Just occasionally, the Lord Advocate may need to explain aspects of the role that can be explained publicly. One example in my time related to the Lord Advocate’s role in signing off on Government Bills that are introduced to the Scottish Parliament. There is a provision in the ministerial code to the effect that the Government may not introduce a Bill unless the Lord Advocate or one of the law officers has signed off that it is within competence. There was an issue in relation to the legal continuity Bill during the whole Brexit period, where the Presiding Officer took a particular view that the Bill was not within competence.

I went to the Scottish Parliament to give it reassurance that, notwithstanding the Presiding Officer’s view, I, as Lord Advocate, had considered the Bill. I explained to the Parliament why the Government’s view was that it was within competence. In the end, the issue was tested in the Supreme Court. With the exception of one clause, the judgment I made turned out to be correct, but it was a seriously difficult question. It could have gone either way. That was quite an unusual situation, where the law officer role in those competence issues came into sharp focus. I felt that the right thing to do was to go to the Parliament and answer for it.

Douglas McAllister: Freedom of information responses suggest that, as Lord Advocate, you attended the Scottish Cabinet more often than any other office holder before or since. You have mentioned Brexit and covid, which is perfectly understandable. James Wolffe: I do not think so. We are now going back historically. That was not really, so far as I remember, a feature at all. It is fair to say that I entirely recognise that I attended Cabinet meetings more often than—you will have the statistics.

Douglas McAllister: You attended more often than any other Lord Advocate before or after.

James Wolffe: That is interesting to know. I appreciate that it is an issue that people ask questions about. If I can give you an entirely personal view, I think that those who question the presence of the Lord Advocate at Cabinet may misunderstand. That may be an unfair characterisation, but I think the concerns around that reflect, potentially, a misunderstanding of the nature of Cabinet as the apex decision making body in Government.

Douglas McAllister: It was around the fact that you had attended more than any other Lord Advocate that I was focusing on.

James Wolffe: You are absolutely right. It was an extraordinary period. Part of the purpose of the Lord Advocate being present at Cabinet is because you are at the apex of this system of making sure that policy considerations are properly informed by legal advice. James Wolffe: It is not necessary for a law officer to have been a career prosecutor. It would be quite hard, but not impossible, to take on the role if you had never been a prosecutor. In Scotland, in our system, we have professional career prosecutors who spend the entirety, or a large part, of their legal career as prosecutors within the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service.

These days there are also some advocate deputes who see the role as part of a career in the prosecution service, including some really outstanding advocate deputes who were formerly procurators fiscal. In Scotland, one of the geniuses of our system is that the Lord Advocate can, in effect, clone herself by appointing advocate deputes. Every advocate depute has all the powers of the Lord Advocate and none of the accountability, because the accountability always sits with the Lord Advocate for what individual advocate deputes do. It is not uncommon in our system for a lawyer who has done other things to become an advocate depute for a period of time.

Thinking of my own career, I had done virtually no criminal law. I had done a lot of the kind of law where you talk to judges. I had done a lot of commercial work. I had done a lot of work for Scottish Government and other public authorities. In 2007, I decided to become an advocate depute and basically prosecute full time, which I did for about two and a half years. James Wolffe: It might be helpful if I explain the layout. The principal base of law officers is in Crown Office in Chambers Street in Edinburgh. There is a law officer suite on the first floor, where you have the wonderful private office, the Lord Advocate’s room and the Solicitor General’s room. It is fair to say that it is a very close working relationship because you are physically working next door to each other.

It depends on the way that the Lord Advocate of the day decides to approach the role, one would hope in discussion with the Solicitor General of the day.
