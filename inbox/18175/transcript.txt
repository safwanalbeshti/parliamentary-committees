Q2 Monica Harding: Hello, everyone. I will start with you, Abdouraouf. Can you set out the scale of humanitarian need across the Sahel?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: Thanks for the question. I will start by giving the statistics. It is about 20 million individuals trapped in forced displacement, 4 million refugees and asylum seekers, 14 million IDPs across the region, mainly from the central Sahel, coastal countries of Gulf of Guinea, Togo, Benin, Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana. The Lake Chad basin is where unfortunately we are seeing a warring trend because of the resumption of attack of non-state armed groups and the spill-over towards Cameroon, Niger, Chad—the whole Lake Chad basin region. Then, of course, countries like Chad; we are lucky to have the representative Patrice with us. Then, the Central African Republic region, as well as Congo-DRC, with the whole complexity that we all know when it comes to Congo-DRC: Ebola, malaria, cholera, as well as the conflict in the eastern parts of the country.

Out of those statistics, it is important to mention that it is about 82% of women and children trapped in that displacement. The average duration of displacement in the region is around 15 or 20 years. You will agree with me that this is an element that indicates that displacement in the region goes far beyond the humanitarian portfolio. The internal and external aspects make it very compelling: border intervention, social cohesion, the pressure on the local communities, pressure on the host Government and pressure on the whole asylum system. As we respond to the needs of this displaced population in the host communities, it is important to ensure that those member states keep their obligations when it comes to asylum and access to international protection.

This is maybe just another statistic, but there is a lot of struggle when it comes to young people’s access to education: 62% of the total population of refugee children of the age to go to school are out of school. This is a challenge. All this is happening in the context where we see political instability, pressure from the climate change effects and economic hardship and food insecurity. It is not easy in that region, if I can put it in one word.

Q3 Monica Harding: The figures that you are giving us are huge and astonishing. It is quite difficult, I think, to see it in any context. I guess my question to you is: where do you see this going?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: I think the trajectory will depend on how we act now. This is why I feel so privileged to be in front of this august Committee to thank FCDO and the UK for the support that they have given to that region, not only in Chad but beyond Chad. This is the moment. I feel like this is a tipping point where we all need to come together and understand the baseline, the data that we have, and the need for us to work hand in hand. We cannot continue to work in isolation, the humanitarian actors on one side, development entities on the other side, and Government trying whatever they can to respond to the needs. Local communities sometimes feel abandoned because they do not see the support that they require. The private sector also needs to understand that there is a space for them to be present in a practical manner to support all this effort.

To answer your question, it is a simple one, but difficult. If we do not act now, then unfortunately the situation will be a bit complicated; 90% of the displacement is happening within the region. You can see it through the IDP figure that I have just shared with you. We have few individuals who are compelled to go further away from the region. My fear is that if we do not act sufficiently to address the need, then we will see more and more people leaving these border areas, trying to reach places where they believe that they can get access to many other opportunities.

Q4 Monica Harding: Can I also put to you that we asked a senior director from the Foreign Office why there were cuts to aid and development in the Sahel. His answer to the question was, for example, the French have put in a lot of money to the Sahel for a long period of time and nothing has worked. It was almost as if to say nothing will ever work. My question to you is: what has not worked so far and what is different now? How should it work?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: I think if you look at the way that we were responding to the needs in the Sahel—let me just take Sahel, for instance, because I was UNHCR representative in Burkina Faso in 2021, so I saw it for myself. The level of resources that we had to deal with 1 million IDPs, when I compare it with the level of resources that we have been entrusted with in 2026, I think it is day and night. The numbers have increased, meaning that we cannot continue to only respond to the needs of those who are fleeing. It is important to look at how we stabilise. For us, maybe this is where we need to understand each other when it comes to stabilisation, working with local communities, bringing back agency to those communities, the displaced and the host communities. Sometimes we tend to forget that the first responders are the communities. How do we restore agency to those communities? How do we rebuild the local systems? Those are the things that I see today.

A month ago, I was in Mali and Burkina Faso, and I was pleased to see how communities themselves are reinventing new ways of addressing the challenges they are confronted with. We need to listen to the communities. This is why data is so important. This is one of the areas in which FCDO is supporting us a lot to understand through the data the need, the pattern, and see exactly how can we then influence the intervention. I think we cannot continue doing things that are not yielding results. This is a time where we need to reinvent a new way of engaging in the Sahel and across the region. Thanks a lot.

Q5 Monica Harding: Thank you. This is a question to you, Patrice. It is good to see you.

Patrice Ahouansou: It is good to see you again.

Monica Harding: How easy is regional co-ordination across the Sahelian states? How easy is regional co-ordination across the Sahel and across the various states?

Patrice Ahouansou: Thank you, Chair, and thank you, Madam, for this question. I will first of all start with the co‑ordination within Chad and then the co-ordination between Chad and the Sahel countries in terms of activities and cross-border response. Chad, as you know, is facing multiple crises: conflict in neighbouring countries, security conflict, armed conflict, security issues, climate crises, and food insecurity. I am sure Marc will talk more about food insecurity. Of course, all this triggers forced displacement.

What is important here is how this forced displacement is provoking human shock—let’s say three levels of shocks. We have the human shock, we have the demographic shocks, and we have also the territorial shocks. With the human shock, we are seeing refugees, IDPs, host communities in very dire situations, and women and girls paying a heavy price. Many of them, especially the children, are sacrificing their childhood because the circumstances of the conflict are putting them in a situation where they have to be head of household—a role they are not supposed to play at their age.

Demographic shock is because we are seeing refugees, IDPs and returnees representing the majority in most of the areas; for instance, the Sudanese refugees in the eastern part of Chad. One in three people in the eastern part of Chad is a refugee—one in three. This shows you the shock in terms of demography and also in terms of territory. The territorial setting that was pre-existing before the conflict has changed in a context where decentralisation has been actioned by the Chadian Government, but this needs to take into account the presence of the refugees in order to have a proper administrative set-up. These are the three levels of shocks.

Facing this, we need to co-ordinate better with the different neighbouring countries. For instance, co-ordination between Chad and Sudan is through cross-border activities to ensure that we are not only providing for refugees but the capacity is also extended to the internally displaced people within Sudan. The inaction in Sudan will trigger more displacement into Chad. That is why there is this co-ordination between the two teams, agencies, NGOs, as well as local authorities to make sure that the cross-border response can be provided. Between Chad, Cameroon and Central African Republic—

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: Nigeria.

Patrice Ahouansou: —also Nigeria. Between Chad and Nigeria, what we put in place is, first of all, thanks to FCDO, the protection monitoring, because once again it all comes down to data. To better co-ordinate, you need data to understand the trend of the movement and to understand also the cross-border movement in order to be able to do cross-border analysis. We have also this co-ordination in place between Chad and Nigeria. This helps us to assess the situation in the places of origin of Nigerian refugees living in Chad. What is the situation in the places of origin? We are doing this thanks to the data collection and data analysis, the tool that FCDO is supporting. We are co-ordinating also at that level.

It is the same thing with Central African Republic, where we have more than 140,000 refugees from Central African Republic living in Chad. Right now, we are discussing how to support their return. In the midst of this situation, we have also, let’s say, a pocket of hope in terms of return. This is what CAR and all the stakeholders in Central African Republic are doing in all this development pool to see what investment can be made in CAR so that return is sustainable. We have tools to analyse, to understand the priorities in the places of origin. There are some key priority projects but, once again, it comes to implementation of this project to make sure that when people return they do not come back, that the reintegration is effective and the return is sustainable.

Co-ordination with all these countries is essential. Most important is also the co-ordination within Chad. We are today at the crossroads where some of the mistakes made collectively in 2003 should not be made any more. I think this is the narrative that we all have. We all agree that for the first crisis of Sudan in 2003 there has not been proper development investment, in refugee hosting areas. How do we correct that? It should be, first of all, through co-ordination from the start, under the leadership of the Government. For the first time, the Government, the UN agencies, the NGOs, the diplomatic representation, all the donors, sit together and agree on a co-ordination model. All this is to make sure that the Government are taking leadership. We are there as UN agencies and NGOs to support the implementation of the new model of co-ordination. This co-ordination is being done through the line ministries, so that refugees, host communities and IDPs are integrated in the national development plan and in the provincial development plan. This is the way we are co-ordinating at all levels.

Q6 Monica Harding: That is very helpful. Thank you, Patrice. Abdouraouf, to come back to you, and you touched on this already, with 24 million people estimated to need humanitarian assistance and protection in the region this year but only 8.2 million prioritised by UN agencies for urgent support, what are the risks of leaving those needs unmet?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: We see it already happening with the cuts—the reduction of humanitarian funding that we have experienced in the region. There was a 50% reduction of humanitarian funding in 2025. When it comes to development support, ODA, a reduction close to approximately 70% across the region. The first point is that we can only see the aggravation of what I was mentioning—the access to education. We see it becoming difficult for children to continue to have access to the education system. Then, the other dimension I can also mention is when it comes to registration. We do not have the capacity, and Governments also do not have the capacity, to register individuals in the national database. This is one of the risks that I also see.

On the pathway towards solution agenda, if we are not investing—because this is not aid, this is investment—how do we make it happen? As one of my colleagues used to say, there is no humanitarian solution to humanitarian problems. For us, this is a call that we have reached a limit in terms of the current model, where we systematically repeat the same humanitarian aid. Something new that needs to come, in addition to what we used to do, is the investment, by opening space to international financial institutions. This is what we are doing, working closely with the World Bank, African Development Bank, Islamic Development Bank, and working also with some foundations like Mastercard Foundation. Mastercard Foundation is helping us a lot, working and structuring our engagement when it comes to localisation, working with the communities. We have received some support since last year to build the capacity of close to 300 local organisations led by women in that region. I think, definitely, if the cuts remain where they are, we will only see a very bad situation. The situation would be very bad, not only for the communities and the individuals, but also for the receiving countries.

Q7 Monica Harding: Thank you. When I was in the camps on the Chad-Sudan border, we were very interested in the women and girls and the violence that they had experienced, but it was felt there was nobody there to ask the questions because the resources were so stretched. It was impossible to go after atrocity prevention or accountability because there was no one to ask the questions of the women. I think that probably puts into what you are saying.

Can I come to you, please, Marc? Could you outline the operational difficulties of only receiving short-term reactive funding from international partners?

Marc Regnault de la Mothe: Yes. Good afternoon and thank you again for the opportunity to intervene today. First, foremost, maybe let me focus on the food insecurity in the Sahel. We have 9.7 million people who are facing food insecurity in 2026 in the Sahel. The drivers of food insecurity have already been mentioned by my colleagues, which I salute. We have a combination of conflict, of displacement, of climate change, and of economic pressures. It is both severe and structural at the same time. The needs are great. Again, in Sahel, 9.7 million people are in food insecurity, and the resources are reducing.

We also have longer-term trends. We have population growth. We have climate change, which is going to impact the Sahel in particular because there is a 1.5° difference in average temperatures that are forecasted for the Sahel. We also have El Niño. El Niño is forecasted to be quite severe in the next season, the first half of 2027. In the whole of the west and central African region, 6 million more people are going to experience food insecurity because of El Niño. These are additional challenges.

We have also the impact of the Hormuz strait crisis of the Middle East. Why? There is an increase in food prices, an increase in fuel prices and an increase in all the inputs that are needed to sustain food production. The other factor that you mentioned, and thank you for the question, is the decreased investment from donors. How do we face this? We have to prioritise. The prioritisation means assisting those who are most vulnerable and trying to assist them as well with doable solutions.

I wanted to come to that, to say that it is not all doom and gloom because there are solutions. There are possibilities to invest for the UK, for donors, in longer-term solutions. For example, in the resilience programme that we have across the Sahel, called the integrated resilience programme, we managed in the last years to restore more than 300,000 hectares of degraded land, assisting a cumulative number of 8 million people across the Sahel. This has a lot of impact in the long term. It helps to avoid and to reduce humanitarian needs. It helps to reduce migration. It helps to reduce coping mechanisms. It also helps to support human capital because there is not only land restoration and the green coverage, which is coming back to the degraded lands of the Sahel, but also children going back to school. There are also social protection systems that are being put in place again. There is a place for investment, and there is a place for making sure that families and communities want to stay where they are. If they have livelihood opportunities, they will not need to migrate. They will not need to be displaced.

Q8 Chair: Marc, can I ask you to outline the prioritisation challenges for the World Food Programme as a result of reduced funding?

Marc Regnault de la Mothe: Yes. It is a great challenge because we have needs that are very substantial, and the reduced funding that we are receiving obliges us to cut down on many programmes that are vital for people. I am not speaking only about humanitarian assistance or about nutrition programmes. Another figure that we did not mention is that there are more than 10 million children who are malnourished in the region. That is another programme that we have to reduce. We have to reduce the number of people we are assisting, assisting 4 million fewer people than we were planning to. These are big, substantial cuts that have an impact and that basically make it so that the negative coping strategies that have been mentioned by my colleagues, unfortunately, also come back in these communities where we had to cut and we are not able to assist any more.

As I was saying, 4 million fewer people whom we can assist across the Sahel is quite an impact. Of course, it is very difficult to face it. We face it by prioritising the resources and making sure that we have more impact on the most acute needs. We do so also by working together as agencies. With the UNHCR we conduct joint assessments. We target the resources to the same refugees, the same IDPs, not only with open-ended and endless assistance, but with durable solutions based on vulnerability. This is extremely important because it also helps us to reduce costs, to have cost efficiencies and co-ordination, and work together to make sure that every dollar, every pound, every euro is invested in the best possible way, because we know the resources are less. We have to make sure that they are directed to long-term solutions to national systems and to co-ordinated actions that reassure voters and those that provide the funding that their funding and their assistance is used in the best possible way. We do more with less resources, and we try to do better in a more concentrated and a more efficient way.

Chair: That still means you cannot cover everyone.

Marc Regnault de la Mothe: No, we cannot. We can do cost efficiency as much as we want, but if the funding is too reduced, there is only so much that cost efficiency can do. There are also needs that have to be met, and the needs are effectively very important. The reduction in funding has an impact. We cannot hide that.

Q9 Chair: Are there other organisations that have any capacity to pick up the shortfall from your reduction in funding?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: This is difficult because we all live in the same situation. NGOs, local structures and UN entities—we are all confronted with the same challenge. Even host countries as well, country of origin—as Patrice was explaining in the context of the CAR—countries of return, countries of origin, countries of destination. It is a difficult moment for us all.

As I was saying, this is not the time and there is not the luxury for any one of us to work in isolation. We need to understand exactly what the opportunities are, what the resources are and what the goals are. Based on trust and co-ordination, and in a well co-ordinated manner, how can we yield the impact of the reduced resources that we have? As Marc was saying, there is the example of Burkina Faso where, together with WFP and UNICEF, we have set up what we call the emergency response team, where we join up together with OCHA. In case there is a displacement quickly, we can mutualise and then respond to the needs. We cannot expect other entities to come up and take the shock. We are all living through the same realities. This is a difficult moment for the humanitarian community.

Q10 Chair: Marc, how do you predict that local populations will cope in the coming months and years without access to the food they require?

Marc Regnault de la Mothe: The coping mechanisms can be quite severe, and one of them is migration. Nobody wants to migrate from their communities if their livelihood is assured and if they have land to till and food to eat. When people do not have food, they do not have solutions to solve their problems. They need to pull the children out of school. They need to look for livelihoods elsewhere. They need to migrate. That is one impact that, of course, we are all concerned with. That is one of the main coping strategies that, unfortunately, people have to take.

The other ones I mentioned are cutting on their children’s education, cutting on health expenses, cutting on investments, sometimes decapitalising—meaning selling your goats, selling your seeds, consuming your seeds, not being able to invest in your future anymore. When you cannot invest in your future, life looks bleak and you need to look for a better life elsewhere. That is one of the drivers of displacement. That is one of the drivers of food insecurity.

With the medium and longer-term trends of climate change, of El Niño, of conflicts, and of higher food and fuel prices, the situation is not looking good. There is also a hopeful solution, which is to make sure that the engagement of the UK and other donors remains, that the resources are pooled in the sense that the successful programmes and successful resilience solutions are that the Governments together convene and decide where the best investments are for their funding and where they make the most impact.

One important thing is the long-term commitment and to make sure that funding for these programmes that are long term is there and is sustained. This is essential if we want to have some of these gains that I was mentioning, because the intermittent funding that changes year to year and that only gives temporary solutions to problems is not going to solve the issue, especially if there is less. We need long-term commitment and joining forces with the other members of the international community is essential. Thank you.

Q11 Chair: You have mentioned El Niño, and there are predictions of severe dry conditions as a result between November this year and January next year. Do you have contingency plans in place for that?

Marc Regnault de la Mothe: Yes, there are contingency plans for El Niño, and maybe one thing that we should mention is that it is not only dry spells and dry weather; it is also too much rain. When the land is not ready and is degraded, instead of being a positive factor for agricultural production, the rain becomes a danger. It creates floods; it washes away whatever remains of the fertile land that is there. That is why these resilience and land restoration interventions are so essential. In terms of El Niño contingency, yes, the contingency plans are there to assist more people, but the contingency plans also need to be funded. There will be an augmented intervention, but the augmented intervention can only take place if the funding is there to support it.

Q12 Chair: Patrice, does the UNHCR have contingency plans for these El Niño events?

Patrice Ahouansou: Our contingency planning is based on, for instance in Chad, the displacement from Sudan and the displacement within Chad, especially in the lake region where we have more than 250,000 internally displaced people. For us, once again, what is important is what role the Government are playing in all this in putting together the contingency plan. This comes to the shift that we are operating as well.

In the past, we would see all UN actors and NGOs sitting and working on the contingency planning without much Government leadership. This has completely changed. The Government is taking the lead. We are supporting them to finalise the contingency. This is exactly what we did for the Sudan situation, and the same thing for the IDPs, internally displaced people, in the lake region, to make sure that the solution from the start is not only a language that we are speaking as a UN agency or NGOs, but that the Government also are speaking the same language. How the displacement areas are targeted for government projects, for instance, is a conversation that we are having in Chad, and WFP and UNHCR are playing a critical role in terms of resilience programmes in the eastern part through the initiative that we call Haguina: WFP, UNHCR and IOM joining together to make sure that the lands that are degraded are regenerated and accessible for agriculture.

Once again, doing this means that we cannot only rely on humanitarian funds. That is why we are doing our best to make sure that all the key donors—the UK being one of the key donors—can also see the ability or the capacity to use the development fund as well as the climate fund in those areas, in order that, first of all, we can scale up all these initiatives, and also have a tangible impact for more people. At the end of the day, what we want is to break the dependency on humanitarian aid. To be able to do it, we need to value all the capacities that refugees are coming with, all the capacities that IDPs have, and invest in those capacities so that they can take care of themselves in the future. We are also changing the model, the way we are operating. This can only be sustainable if humanitarian funds, development funds and climate funds are accessible.

Q13 Dr Sam Rushworth: Could we carry on talking about displacement for a minute? Abdouraouf, could you share with us how displacement across the multiple countries in the region is going to complicate the work of UNHCR?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: I can give you a concrete example. I remember when I was assuming this function of regional bureau director for west and central Africa in Dakar. That was in July 2023. In the first days of assuming this function, we were hearing news about refoulement at the border between Ghana, Côte d’Ivoire and Burkina Faso. The refoulement was also taking place in a very tense relationship between those countries. You will recall the shift between the three Sahelian countries and the regional economic body, ECOWAS. Then later, of course, they have created the Alliance des États du Sahel.

As I was saying, the displacement pattern in the region is that you see people being displaced first as IDP, then after as refugees, then they come back as a returnee. If they do not find opportunity to settle, what happens? They go again as IDP, then they cross again as refugees. If they are tired, then they decide maybe it is not worth staying here; I have to go. How do we address solution opportunities and improvement of the protection environment? We can only do it through humanitarian diplomacy and regional co-operation.

This is what we have done between the Sahelian countries and the Gulf of Guinea countries by establishing what we call the Lomé dialogue. The Lomé dialogue has enabled us to invite all these countries in Lomé—countries like Burkina Faso, sitting in the same room just like here with Côte d’Ivoire, Togo and Ghana and, despite the political division, agree to discuss how best they can improve the protection of the civilians and collaborate to maintain the minimum principle in terms of engagement with the civilian population. I am proud to say, after two or three years of sustained engagement with that humanitarian diplomacy, a month ago we met again in Lomé, and we were able to sign a tripartite agreement between Burkina Faso-Togo, Burkina Faso-Benin and Burkina Faso-Ghana. Burkina Faso-Côte d’Ivoire we were not able to sign because there are still some elements that we are dealing with. It is a process, wanting to be patient, and then hopefully that signature will take place before the end of the year. That gives you the complexity that comes with the border engagement within a very complex political environment.

Q14 Dr Sam Rushworth: To follow up on that, to test my understanding, what I am hearing is obviously that there is conflict in the region, but that possibly one of the biggest drivers of displacement is actually people returning to discover that their home, their land, is inhabited by somebody else, that they have no livelihood to go back to, and therefore they are becoming displaced for a second or third time. Is that correct?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: Absolutely correct. That is why we say that return is not just a cross-border movement. Return requires investment. Return requires people to have opportunity to have access to services. If children were in a displacement, but at least they were going to school, you ask them to go back and there is no school. They will not stay. They will go. They will leave again. How do we make all this return much more sustainable? It is through investment.

In the region, this is what we are trying to do with our partners like WFP, UNICEF and others, of course understanding that with the reduced humanitarian resources that we are all confronted with, we need to create space and opportunities for much more development opportunities, much more development financing and much more investment coming from the private sector. We cannot do it alone. This is where I think that the UK is uniquely placed. When I see the way that you engage in Chad with the Government, with the UN system, with the NGOs, with the other donors, I think we need that kind of capacity. I also talk about the data. Data will underpin the way that we understand all this pattern complexity, so that we can all come together and understand how we can best push the pathway towards solution.

Q15 Dr Sam Rushworth: To some extent, I guess that the challenges are around having robust enough legal systems, land registration, mediation between people who are in conflict at a local level. To depart from that question a little, how many forcibly displaced people do you estimate will attempt to then just leave the Sahel and travel to Europe?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: We have some data. When I look at what happened at the border between Sudan and Chad last year, we have an estimate of 12,000 to 13,000 who have left Chad and then went to Libya. Then we have also some indication in terms of estimates of how many Sudanese have also left to Tunisia. I think that number has increased maybe six or seven times. Then we know that there are some Sudanese in Chad who are also living and trying to transit through Lake Chad, reaching Agadez as well. Then last week during the executive committee meeting with the Cameroonian authorities, they told us that they are now seeing Sudanese leaving Chad and coming to Cameroon as well.

Q16 Dr Sam Rushworth: It is becoming a wider and wider area. They are getting further away.

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: Absolutely. This is why stabilisation is key. How do we stabilise those people close to their country of origin?

Q17 Dr Sam Rushworth: Are you expecting the numbers of refugees fleeing further north into Europe to increase, or do you feel that you are seeing the reverse, that some of the programmes are starting to work?

Abdouraouf Gnon-Konde: If you look at some of the data that have been collected, you will have the impression that it is reducing. I would say their routes are just changing. Their routes are dynamic. If there is more control on one route, then people would just look at other alternatives. By the time we catch up and then understand the data there, maybe it is too late, you see? I am saying that the only way we can address it, as Marc was saying, is investment within local communities, giving opportunities in terms of jobs and access to social services. We can only do it if we work closely by aligning our intervention with the national priorities. Government leadership is key. We cannot do it without Governments. How do we make it happen in those regions?

Dr Sam Rushworth: Sudanese.

Patrice Ahouansou: Sudanese. Only Sudanese refugees.
