Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Duncan Capps and Rob Jones.

Chair: I welcome our witnesses to our Home Affairs Committee one-off sitting on border security and irregular migration. We are grateful to have our two witnesses. Will you introduce yourselves?

Duncan Capps: I am Duncan Capps. I am the border security commander in the Home Office.

Rob Jones: I am Rob Jones, director-general of operations in the National Crime Agency.

Chair: Thank you. We are really pleased you could come. This is a topic of great interest to the public. There have been some very high-profile stories over the summer, which we are keen to explore. We are also keen to find out what has been done and to understand the general situation. We will kick off questions with Ben Maguire.

Q1 Ben Maguire: We all welcome the fall in the small boat numbers in recent months. As a Committee, we have been looking at that closely. What evidence is there that it has been driven by enforcement activity or policy changes, rather than by external factors beyond our control?

Duncan Capps: It is worth stating where we are in terms of those numbers. Year on year, compared with this point last year: last year we had just under 37,000 arrivals last year, and this year to date we have had under 21,000, so 16,000 fewer individual migrants have arrived. In terms of boats that have arrived, we have had 305, which compares with 600 boats that had arrived at this point last year. In terms of migrants, that is a 43% reduction, while in terms of boats, it is a 49.8% reduction, or almost 50%. That means that with migrant arrivals, we are at the lowest point that we have had since 2020. I anticipate that by the end of the year—with a little touching of wood—we will come in below 2019, so we will have the lowest numbers really since the big phenomenon of small boats began.

To your question, the Border Security Command has now been in existence for two years, and the Government’s intent was to bring together all the various systems and to look across the whole of Government, particularly with international partners, to address what I know you all understand is a very complicated and complex problem. The idea was to try to put in priorities for where the Government were, to bring in the resources and to bring law enforcement, Departments and other Government agencies into that fight and really cohere the work with international partners.

We break down what we do broadly into three areas. First, those things that attract migrants to the UK—you will be aware of some of the initiatives that have been placed, that the Home Secretary has done, to try to make things less attractive to people who would seek to come here illegally. That includes our organised immigration crime taskforce, Operation Lockstream, under Deputy Chief Constable Wendy Gunney. That has had some great effect on illegal working and some of the pull factors.

The upstream piece is where we work with international partners. That is very much about how we deal with stability in those countries from where people seek to come here, working with those Governments and those along the routes to try to stop migrants making the journeys in the first place. We are very much trying to interdict the supply chains, where the boats, the equipment and the facilitation take place.

We then get into the work that happens in the near borders, principally with France, but also with Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany. In that regard, what we are trying to do is obviously to stop the migrants coming across, really digging into the facilitators, the illegal criminal gangs and the small boats equipment.

I will let Rob talk about some of the detail, but I offer that we have had huge success now in terms of working with partners to reduce the flow of migrants into Europe in the first place. We have had great success in terms of reducing the supply of equipment and boats, and particularly with our work with France and near-border partners, of being able to do that last line of defence on the beaches. The French have had a 60% success rate in stopping boat events this year. Put those combinations of factors together and that is why we have seen, month on month this year, a pretty steady and progressive erosion of migrants’ ability to come across.

Q2 Ben Maguire: Is that something new that we have been doing though? As a Committee, we have been hearing about lots of those measures for the past two and a half years. Why has that suddenly led to a change in numbers now?

Duncan Capps: I have been involved in this since December 2022, when I started setting up the small boats operational command, and I think that this is now the coming together and bedding-in of all those measures, with some of those upstream relationships now really starting to bear fruit. The resourcing, training and expertise of the people who have been involved in that take time. It is one thing setting out a policy and putting resource into it, but it takes time for some of those things to take place. Rob, you probably ought to comment.

Rob Jones: Thank you, Duncan. To focus on that point about why we are convinced that it is because of something that we have done and not just other environmental factors, we have done everything that Duncan has said, and we have made this the main effort for the NCA. We have built a response that focuses on five key areas: social media, high-value targets, equipment, criminal finance, and the movers and shakers in northern France. Those people all now face unbearable pressure day in, day out. That makes them desperate; it makes them change their business model. We have seen an adaptation because of that pressure, and what you have seen this summer—we will get on to some of this later, I am sure—is that pressure being exhibited in them changing their behaviour.

There are key indicators of our progress. At any one time, we have around 100 operations running. Those operations use the full suite of tactics available for national security threats. It was not always like that with organised immigration crime. There has been a clear demand signal to the system in the UK that this is an effort right across HMG, and the policy and influencing around that system by the BSC is really helpful. We have a laser-like focus on the operations. We have targeted known suppliers of equipment, and then, after that, that equipment has no longer appeared in crossings. That is point one.

Point two is that we have seen people finding it much harder to bring together the package of engine, boat, lifejacket and migrant at the same time and place in northern France. We have intervened with European partners on multiple occasions, as a direct result of UK intelligence, to prevent what we call a “go kit”—a car with all that equipment—from getting to migrants. We know that crossings would have happened if we had not dealt with that. All those things build up the pressure. We also had a record year last year in terms of seizures of equipment.

We do not just do that; obviously, we are seeking to bring people to justice as well. But, as we know, an awful lot of that criminality is based overseas. That is why, in the upstream effort, we have come away from France and gone further and further upstream, with covert tactics to confront this, to empower the goal-line defence in France and Belgium. By the time people have got that far, it is very hard to stop them. We are working way away from that now, at scale, to disrupt them and prevent them from getting to France. Those factors coming together have contributed to that drop.

Q3 Ben Maguire: What success looks like is obviously how many crossings there are, but do you have specific KPIs in other areas, particularly on reducing gang activity—you also mentioned the seizure of equipment—other than literally just the number of crossings being made across the channel?

Rob Jones: Absolutely. We also track seizures of equipment, disruptions overseas and arrests; we track a whole range of different indicators. There is always a lag from the activity that we undertake, because this is a movement that is dynamic across all the routes. A very strong year last year set us up for what we have seen this summer, and, as the weather deteriorates, we are working really hard to make sure that we are in the strongest position we can be in for next year. It is therefore not just about one factor; it is about monitoring all of those and making sure that there is pressure on all of them.

Sometimes, arrests are not the most effective way of stopping crossings; sometimes, taking disruptive action overseas is much more effective. Arresting somebody and getting them imprisoned overseas is good—we need to hold them to account—but driving down the numbers and getting people off the water is the ultimate bottom line for us. We don’t want to contest organised immigration crime in the English channel; we want to do it at a time and place of our choice, which means ports and airports, and we need to get migrants off that route. So, sometimes, it is better to disrupt them way away from the UK, even though that might not result in an arrest.

Q4 Robbie Moore: To build on that, I want to discuss the role of the Border Security Command a little more. As has rightly been touched on, one of the reasons for setting it up was to improve inter-agency co-operation. How do you feel co-operation has improved since the command was set up? Do you feel that it is of a status that needs more improvement since your involvement commenced?

Duncan Capps: Without being patronising, that is a very good question. We are two years into this journey, post election and the setting up of this organisation, which you will be aware pulled together some of the existing organisations, including mine. If you recall, my predecessor appeared before you this time last year, when the Maritime Command, the Small Boats Operation Command and Home Office Intelligence were pulled into Border Security Command, making it just under 3,000 people. That brought a really key operational intelligence focus to that.

I would offer that, two years into this, with the relationships, systems and convening authority of this appointment—you will be aware that it is enshrined in the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act, but does not require that—everyone understands how they can play their role, whether that is the organisations for which Rob has responsibility, the Crown Prosecution Service or the Foreign and Commonwealth Office. I think it has taken time for all that to pivot in, so I think the system is working really well.

However, we should be really clear that if we get to the end of this year below 25,000 arrivals, that is still 25,000 arrivals too many. I am not quoting the Home Secretary, but we are relentless. Everyone gets up every morning wanting to drive that down to a level where it is no longer significant, so there is always more to be done in that regard.

I think it was helpful that we managed to sign the deal for the next three years with France, which has given certainty in that space, and it is good to be able to coalesce around. I think that the direction from the previous Prime Minister, as well as this Prime Minister’s laser focus—you will be aware that he visited us in the summer—is helping. There is still more to do, but it is in a good place now.

As Rob said, what we have to do is certainly not claim victory now, and none of us are. We have to make sure that we set the conditions now to stop those people who would seek to travel to the north of France next year and make those crossings.

Q5 Robbie Moore: Just to press you, you said that there is still more to do, so are there any areas of the inter-agency co-operation that is lacking at the moment on which you would like to see more emphasis and focus?

Duncan Capps: I certainly would not single anyone out; I think everyone is really behind this. In a lot of cases, some of the initiatives that we have put in—let’s take our work upstream with the Foreign and Commonwealth Office and our taskforces that deal with the main routes up through the west of Europe, central Europe and the Balkans. Some of that work and those relationships, as well as the rationalisation of the footprint that Rob, the Home Office and the Foreign and Commonwealth Office have to make sure that we are all pulling in the right direction, is going to continue to yield real benefit. As Rob said, we need to do as much of that upstream as we can.

There is still more to be done in some of the work that we are doing within the UK to address the pull factors, but I am confident we are getting there. Based on a year’s-worth of experience dealing with immigration crime in the UK and illegal working, we can now be more focused on the things that pull migrants to want to come here illegally, and making it really difficult for them to do that. I would say those are some of the areas we can really work into.

Rob Jones: There are areas where we have taken very direct action in relation to illegal migration, and we are quite advanced in those areas, but there are other important areas that are catching up in priority.

Criminal finance, cash-based economy, the grey economy and the high street—all those things are relevant to illegal migration, because you do not want someone to think that they are going to come work for cash in a vape shop in the UK. That has spun up our Operation Machinize and our work with the NPCC on really pressurising what could be seen as a pull factor, and it is really important to make sure that the lies told overseas about coming to the UK—that you can melt away and work with impunity—are confronted and that people are arrested.

That is coming up to the level of activity that we have in the more direct areas of chasing facilitators and people who are involved in this directly. The informal value transfer system—the hawaladars—is really important to us, but it is really difficult to deal with. It is hidden from regulation and the reporting regime that we get with suspicious activity reports from banks.

All of these areas are catching up with the higher priority areas, and we need all of those to be flat out to squeeze this. With the help of Duncan and the convening power that we have across Whitehall, that is exactly what we are doing. Everybody is at the table, and that is the important thing.

The other benefit that I have seen is the legislation that came through. I have talked before about acts preparatory and going early to disrupt organised crime at its earliest possible stage, and we are now starting to use that. The business model that I described was largely legal. Ubiquitous social media is not illegal to run. The equipment is not illegal to possess. Using hawaladars is not illegal, unless we can prove a link to criminality.

We now have section 13 of the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 that allows us to seize equipment, which is brilliant. You have seen some of that recently, with 25 vessels seized in the UK under that legislation. That would have been 25 vessels with at least 79 people in. Criminalising that business model with the new legislation has been important, as well. That is another benefit of what the Border Security Command has helped with.

Q6 Robbie Moore: You touched on what I want to get on to next. Obviously, the statutory responsibilities of Border Security Command are much broader than just dealing with illegal migration and small boats issues, although that has rightly been given huge attention. That has predominantly been the key focus. Is that justified and what are the implications for other areas of work that you are also mandated to focus on, where less attention may be afforded?

Duncan Capps: You are right. The intention for Border Security Command was that it would be a system leader across all illegal immigration into the United Kingdom but, as you say, to start with, the clear and present focus for us was illegal migration by small boat and the facilitation around it. You will recall in the summer, when we talked about the common travel area and some other threats, that we are interested in that. I am sure we will come later to displacement.

If we are successful in the small boats area, is there a demand that has to be satisfied by other ways to get into the United Kingdom? We are very interested in those things. I also have a remit for wider national border security. You will be aware that I have the only law enforcement maritime capability in the UK. That was involved in the boarding of the MV Smyrtos. We facilitated that using our cutters. We still maintain a persistent presence in that.

I oversee the joint maritime security centre. We work very closely on national resilience and critical national infrastructure. That work is still going. We are concerned about the common travel area. We are concerned about clandestine entry into the UK, and we keep that as a plate that is spinning, working with Border Force, NCA and other regional police forces.

The focus at the moment is definitely on small boats, but it cannot be at the expense of not keeping an eye on those things. Because you and Parliament would not thank me if we fixed the small boat problem and all that happens is we get people displaced into other dangerous modes of entry. We all remember the awful situation at Purfleet where people died in a container. I don’t know if you have any thoughts on that, Rob.

Rob Jones: I have just a couple of points. The most egregious breach of the border remains small boats. That creates community tension and problems across society. We have got to deal with it, and we will be relentless in pursuing that. At the same time, there are other breaches of the border in relation to drugs, firearms and other issues. Be assured, we are looking at where displacement might go for organised immigration crime.

At the same time, we must concurrently be alive to the way those threats are developing, as well. We have to maintain our response against other smuggling methods. We have prioritised small boats. Specialists who understand smuggling and global networks and are experts in drugs are now tackling small boats. We are backfilling and developing other people to ensure we retain the level of expertise we need to tackle drugs. You cannot pivot towards one threat without there being an opportunity cost or consequence.

That is what we seek to mitigate with the ongoing work around resourcing where we have had an uplift in resources. We are holding the line, but this is challenging. The national security threat, the National Crime Agency’s remit around a range of threats, from child sexual abuse through to OIC and drugs and firearms—none of that has gone away. We are pursuing that concurrently while we get after this.

Duncan Capps: To offer one other thought, this organisation is a little like an athlete: it is now match-fit and getting better. I came in from the military, and when I first got here, I was surprised by the communication and slight stovepiping in some cases. I am not saying that we are completely out of that, but it is so much better, in terms of co-operation, understanding, clear prioritisation, and the link between Parliament making policy or Ministers making directions for resources to be allocated and actually being able to pull levers to make things happen. Those techniques and relationships will endure and are good for all these threats, and you can now pivot into those in a more dynamic way than you could before.

Q7 Robbie Moore: That takes me nicely on to my question on funding and its prioritisation. How do you as an organisation decide whether the interventions funded by the Border Security Command are value for money? On prioritisation, what areas of spending are you most confident represent good value for money?

Duncan Capps: Part of the compact to set up the Border Security Command was that it would act as the system leader, so it would run the strategy and plan by allocating funding to the various endeavours. Obviously, there is the core funding that sets up my organisation, but this year, there is £290 million being focused through organisations that have a role, including the NCA, agencies and the CPS.

We covered a little of this in our first statutory report, which was published in the summer, and we will deal with a lot more of it in our report next year. We work through my system leadership board, which all the director-general level representatives are on, and we monitor the progress in all those areas. Of course, when we come to the end of what is essentially our first proper year of doing that, that ongoing assessment process and bids for what will go on in future will hopefully allow us to identify the things that are having the greatest effect, which we will want to double down on and continue to fund, and the areas where we perhaps have not had as much success, so that we can pivot that funding. That process is pretty dynamic.

Obviously, one of the major parts is the funding that we channel through the National Crime Agency to establish the pivot that Rob talked about and the upstream network for us to work together on this. Rob, do you want to talk about measures of effectiveness in your area?

Rob Jones: To make sure that we are providing real value for the money invested in us, as we go from year to year and as we plan for future interventions, we look at what works and try to double down on it. That is connected to the funding, as the easiest answer to your question is that the Home Office pay on results.

Q8 Robbie Moore: What has not worked? In your time in this role, have there been any interventions that you have deemed not value for money and stopped?

Duncan Capps: We have not been through a full cycle yet, so it is probably too early in the gestation of the BSC to identify anything that has been genuinely unsuccessful. If I had to give an example, the work we did to pull together some of the recommendations on work in the UK under Lockstream has perhaps been focused against criminality more generally. Coming into the next year, we will focus that on organised immigration crime much more specifically, rather than on a more general part. I would not say that it has not been effective, as it has been very successful in addressing wider criminality, but given my clear main effort, we will pivot it much more towards what we see as the pull factors for small boat migrants coming to the UK. As you would guess, that is a lower-cost part of the area around people looking to come and work illegally here.

Q9 Robbie Moore: On the opposite side of this—maybe this will build on the answer you just gave—if more funding was to be awarded for you to make additional interventions, on whatever you deemed fit, to meet the objectives, where would that funding be allocated to give value for money? Would it be specifically to address the pull factors you mentioned?

Duncan Capps: You might want to touch on this later, but as you will be aware, post the one in, one out deal, the Home Secretary has pulled together all the work that was already happening under a small boats taskforce. That looks at deterrence, potential returns and that element of the issue, so that we can start to build in consequence when people jump the queue and come here illegally. That is an area that I expect the Home Secretary will want to resource using funding from BSC.

As Rob said, it is about addressing these things upstream, with partners in the countries that they come from, and capacity building. Critically, it is about doing that as the whole of Government, so that when we negotiate with those countries, we bring in all the equities cross-Government, not just organised immigration crime. There is a compact here: do they want things from us? Yes, they do. We need to wrap in those in a way that perhaps did not happen before.

If we can stop people wanting to leave their countries, we can stop the facilitators exploiting them to cross borders, and, critically, we can stop the supply chain. That is far more effective because when people get to the north of France—many of you have been there—broadly, they will keep trying until they are either successful or die, and that causes all sorts of problems. I would offer that the work we have done with France in the new deal, and particularly with the flexibility to look at what we have learned over the last three years and before to focus on the things that are successful—that is a core part of that, but it is only holding the ground.

Rob Jones: On your point around where we are at and what works, it is quite early because we have gone from the build and the uplift to then assessing where we can best place resources. Within the envelope we have, we are constantly looking at whether we can redeploy and get behind something that has had a tangible impact. Across the areas that I talked about—the five key areas that we operate against—the work on criminal finance for OIC is interesting, because the narrative around following the money and organised crime holds good, so there is impact. But on the money and the way the money flows work in relation to organised immigration crime, it is a much smaller pot of money and it is much more organic. The system, because it is based on trust, is harder to penetrate and disrupt. That does not mean that you should not do it; it is about finding the right place to target it.

Those are the areas that are catching up with other elements that we do, and when we get through next year, we will be in a much better place to get behind what we think is most effective. All of those areas are productive—I see benefit in all of them. If there was a wider effort, it would be upstream. On the big issues that draw people to the UK—that push people to the UK—some of those are beyond the NCA in terms of dealing with them. We are tackling the organised crime, but in dealing with the causes of this and making sure that the UK is not an attractive target for smuggling gangs, the price is an efficient and effective system that we support by undermining the organised crime. You need to keep looking further and further away from the UK for the causes of this and try to tackle it as far away from France as you can, as Duncan said.

Duncan Capps: One further point, which goes back to your previous point, is that communications and perception is an area that we are doing quite a lot in but can do more. As Rob said, this is unlike most other crimes, which try and stay under the radar. This is overt: it is on TikTok and all of these other apps. It is about working with those companies to try to put deterrence into this—not just in northern France but in the countries where people are coming from. That is an area we have developed this year and I think we can do more.

We are working with the French now in the camps to try to have communications with them. We do dialogue under a thing called Op Asper, where we try and explain the realities of what it means. We are trying to drive down people risking their lives. I think that there is a big piece we can do there to try and bring some truth to the perception of it. You saw some of the work that we did with the French for the first time, using some imagery to show people that might come that the reality is not what they think—particularly if you come from the Horn of Africa, you are going to go through countries and be exploited and so on. I think that is one area that we can do a lot more in: working with our ambassadors and high commissioners in those countries. It is one of the areas I want to develop next year.

Q10 Chair: Have you assessed how many attempts, on average, it takes for someone to get across in a small boat?

Duncan Capps: Yes. On average, I think it is not quite double figures, but seven or eight. I cannot verify that, but you will be aware that since last year, we have a very strong debriefing presence now in Manston. Most of the anecdotal evidence is that people try a number of times to get over.

Q11 Chair: There must be a point where it becomes not economically viable for people to do it. When we were in France last year, we were told that if it got to more than three attempts, people would just stop trying. Are you saying that it is still more than that?

Duncan Capps: I think it is more than that. There are a lot of people in the camps at the moment, which is bad in one respect because a lot of people are there, but it also represents the fact that the French are making it very difficult, particularly in the Dunkirk/Calais area, to get across. To take your logic, you are right that there is a moment—from a criminal perspective, if we look at the business model—where it stops being particularly viable. Part of our strategy is to drive it to a point where it is not worth the criminal gangs doing this, because the cost of getting the boat and the engine, as well as the risk of them being interdicted and jailed, is so much that they stop being in this business.

Q12 Dr Prinsley: You said that there are a lot of people in the camps at the moment. Can you give us an estimate?

Duncan Capps: Under 4,000, but close to 4,000, in the two camps between Calais and Dunkirk. I think the French estimate at the moment is that there are almost 3,800 people between those two camps.

Q13 Chair: What was it previously?

Duncan Capps: In my experience from when I previously did the job in SBOC, it was broadly about 2,000 between them. Our understanding from our French partners, who we work with closely, is that it is almost double what we would normally expect.

Q14 Dr Prinsley: What is your estimate of the average amount of time that somebody would be in a camp before they either get across or give up?

Duncan Capps: I cannot give you an accurate answer to that, but it can be quite a long time. Broadly, the poorer the nationality, the longer they tend to stay in the camps. It can be a period of months, if not longer, that they are living in what, as you have seen, are exceptionally unsanitary and difficult conditions.

Chair: Dreadful conditions, yes.

Duncan Capps: I am sorry that I cannot answer accurately.

Q15 Chair: Do you have any intelligence on the make-up of those 3,800? Are they predominantly single men or is it mixed with families?

Duncan Capps: In terms of gender, it is predominantly single men. There are some females and some families, but it is 90% single men coming over on small boats. In terms of nationalities, as you would expect, it is those that we most frequently see: those from Eritrea, Sudan, Somalia, Ethiopia and Afghanistan. The number from Afghanistan is slightly higher. Those who are slightly more wealthy will tend to come in more quickly, will take less time on the routes from their original countries and will broadly try to cross more quickly.

Q16 Margaret Mullane: We have had many presentations at this Select Committee, and the message—from, I think, Rob and your predecessor—was always that organised crime is very good at adapting, and that that is not going to change any time soon. I agree with the Chair that this is a massive issue in our constituencies. As well as the cost, it is the faith and trust, isn’t it? It is rolling on. As organised crime keeps changing, which it is good at doing, what is the biggest challenge now for you?

Rob Jones: My initial response is they do adapt, but we are thinking about their next move, and we are thinking about that now. I do not want to telegraph where we go next, because I want to be ahead and waiting when they change. The symptom we see is a higher number of attempts. That is grit in the business model—that is really good. The fact that there are more migrants in northern France is not a good thing—it shows the intent is high—but it shows that they are struggling to make the crossing. The fact that events have plummeted by something in the region of 50% and the fact about the number of times people are successful are important facts for communities that understandably feel threatened by this.

The assurance I can give is that we have seen the business model starting to shake, and we are actively working to understand where it goes next, if it does displace, to get ahead of it and be waiting. There are not an infinite number of possibilities for the small boats model. You have seen attempts to use larger vessels. You have seen attempts to evade the French patrols by going out of the areas where the French have very tight control. You have seen them try Belgium. The thing about small boats is that they depend on specialist equipment, on a just-in-time logistics model, and on people being able to bring all that together at the right time and in the right place. That is its vulnerability.

The strength of the model is that it was low cost, but we are increasing the cost because we are increasing the risk. We are increasing the cost to OCGs not just financially. Up till now, they have tried to absorb a lot of that, but they cannot do that forever, because they are driven by profit. We have also increased the cost to them in terms of risk. It is more likely than ever that you are going to get arrested. It is more likely than ever that your equipment is going to be seized. Every time that happens, they need to go again.

People will continue to attempt organised immigration crime and people smuggling. Attempting them in the English channel is getting riskier and riskier for everybody involved. We just need to continue and stay flat out until that viability is no longer there.

Duncan Capps: This is a very good question. The things that have worried me most this year have been the increasing size of the boats and the increasing occupancy. You will have seen that this year we had a boat with 230 people in it—extraordinary. It ought to be put on the record that it is only a matter of time until we have a mass casualty event in the channel. It is extraordinary that that has not happened so far. You will be aware that my officers were involved in a rescue with the French in French waters, on one of the western launches, where we rescued 168 people. None of them drowned—quite an extraordinary thing. That worries me.

You will probably want to do in a bit of detail the displacement into Belgium and down to the western beaches, particularly Normandy. That represents the fact that they are finding it more difficult to launch around Dunkirk and Calais; it is not impossible, but more difficult. The displacement into Belgium was a real fear of mine at the beginning of the year. We set up a taskforce with the Belgians to address that. Not only have we pretty much shut down that route, but we have increased co-operation between France and Belgium and Belgium and the Netherlands, noting that the Belgians have far more permissive use of number-plate and facial recognition. That has had the bigger effect of making it much more difficult for the boats and the engines to be moved from broadly where they were being stored—in the Venlo area around the border with Germany and the Netherlands—in time, as Rob said, with a ready pack. That has made it much more difficult and is one of the contributing factors, so that co-operation has been effective.

Again, on the western launch, it is much more difficult for the criminal gangs to operate in that part of France. It is a very long way. They have to get the migrants to move down there. They have to move the boats and the engines. As you can imagine, we are working very closely with the French to deal with that.

One thing that has made a big difference this year is the French changing their on-water maritime posture. They have physically interdicted 15 boats on the water. Q49 Mr Kohler: Of course, we used to have tools under the EU and the Dublin agreement, and now we have the successor: the EU pact on migration and asylum. Would being within that have a role to play and would that be useful?

Duncan Capps: Rob talked about Eurodac and the sharing of information. We would very much like to be part of that, however we can make that work. If we were part of a European returns agreement, that would not be unhelpful to us. However, there might be other ways that we can do it. You will be aware of the work that we are trying to do with the Calais group and partners to try and facilitate some of that.

Mr Kohler: That was a hugely diplomatic answer. Well done, that was good.

Q50 Chair: You see a role for safe and legal routes?

Duncan Capps: That is a personal view. I have slightly crossed the Rubicon. I was not sure that they were useful. However, if we accept that migration is a worldwide problem and that there will always be people who would seek to come to this country and that they are not all bad people, part of that could be safe and legal routes. We have safe and legal routes but the focus on the one that we did for the small boats deal was effective not only because of the returns but particularly because of the deterrent. My sense from that was that a lot of people did not want to cross to here by small boats. They would like to come here, but that allowed us to process their applications in France, to do their biometrics and to do all the checks that Rob was talking about in advance so that we were not having to do that when they arrived here. Speaking from a personal point of view, I think that is potentially something we would look at in the future.

Q51 Chair: Is the small boats returns task force part of your organisation, BSC?

Duncan Capps: It sits at the moment under Dan Hobbs in migration, borders and international, but I am intimately involved in it and my teams are in it.

Q52 Chair: What is it doing in addition that was not being done before?

Duncan Capps: It is looking at all the potential options that we could have above and beyond those things that we are doing, such as returns hubs, returns to more difficult countries. It will bring in immigration enforcement and all that work because, as you might imagine, the Home Secretary is very keen, after Op Hillmore, to be able to put something in place that will achieve some of the effects that we wanted from that, as obviously we cannot continue it.

Q53 Chair: So if you were designing policy, there would be upstream work, first, to communicate that the streets are not paved with gold and to give them a reason not to leave their home country, there would be communications that we need to improve, and in an ideal world there would be some form of safe and legal route so that someone who is a genuine asylum seeker could make an application without having to get on a small boat.

Duncan Capps: Working with partners.

Chair: Would you have zero tolerance on people arriving by irregular means?

Duncan Capps: That is a difficult question for me, isn’t it? I personally think, having listened to the Home Secretary and the Prime Minister, that it is not British to jump the queue. Some of the people who are jumping the queue are the youngest and the fittest, not necessarily the most deserving. My personal view is that we adhere to the rule of law in this country and one reason, ma’am, that people are not happy in this country is that people are seen to be getting away with it and there is not consequence. Part of that is about consequence. In a perfect world, yes, you would like a situation where you have policies in place that would mean that if you came in illegally there were consequences and you could deal with that.

Q54 Chair: We have heard, in international examples, that some countries have sanctions on migrants who break the conditions of their being an asylum seeker. For example, we heard that in Sweden if an asylum seeker is found working illegally, that is considered when their decision is finally made. Would that help you?

Duncan Capps: Again, I am moving into a policy formulation area, but I think it comes under the area of consequence. If you break the rules, there has to be a consequence for that, otherwise it does not form a deterrent. Part of what you laid out, which is our strategy, is the deterrent effect that, if you do break those rules, there is consequence in that: either return, not being able to work or whatever it might be.

Q55 Chair: It strikes me that working illegally is one of the big problems because people come here and they may be in debt bondage. We have heard takeaway companies telling us that prices will go up if they cannot employ people who have arrived by irregular means, but they are breaking the law. It is against the law to employ those people.

Duncan Capps: Just to reassure you, part of the really significant work that we do with immigration enforcement criminal finance and investigation is to try to get into those companies and stop them employing people illegally.

Q56 Chair: Are you involved in that?

Duncan Capps: Yes, I am.

Chair: So that comes under your brief as well. We will finish with some questions on public disorder and small boat arrivals. I know that you touched on it earlier, but we are keen to explore it before we finish.

Q57 Ben Maguire: I will go back to some of the issues that Peter Prinsley raised earlier about the protests and public disorder on the south coast prompted by the small boat arrivals. What challenges does that create for ensuring that those travelling by small boat are intercepted and processed safely and securely?

Duncan Capps: I laid some of that out earlier, sir. First, there is the danger of their crossing, which is such an enormously long journey. Through our work with the French coastguard we actually get pretty good early warning that they are arriving. You can imagine that although we saw it as a possible scenario, we saw it as unlikely just because of the sheer distance they were crossing.

The first boat that arrived on 6th into 7th September was unexpected and the response was the best we could put together at the time. My team went down there to do the landside response, supported by Border Force. You will be aware that that came under the coastguard’s responsibility because the RNLI worked with them—they were tasked to do it. The marina they were taken into was the one that they were allocated, which, as it turned out, was less secure or unsecure and not where you would want to do it. You will be aware that there was a protest that turned violent. I commend the Hampshire and Isle of Wight police for being incredibly professional in terms of what they did there. It allowed us to remove those migrants.

We subsequently had two more. The second arrival was intercepted by the RNLI. Because the sea was incredibly smooth and there were only 49 young men on the boat, all of whom were fit enough, we cross-decked them from the RNLI vessels into one of my cutters, a law enforcement vessel—it is a 42-metre, grey funnel boat. We moved that up to Dover, where we disembarked them at Western Jet Foil in the way that we normally would.

That is not particularly easy. If you have people who are obviously suffering medical problems or children, that would be difficult. We have to do it in circumstances. The final arrival, because it was night and it was a significant number, we brought into the site in Gosport, which was secured by the police. We then moved them by road to Western Jet Foil, and then on to Manston for onward processing.

Q58 Ben Maguire: You touched earlier on the fact that only about 2% of cases are dealt with by the RNLI. You mentioned that one of the arrivals was transferred to one of the Border Force vessels. Why did that not happen in the first place? Why did RNLI come in and get involved at that point?

Duncan Capps: To be clear, we have the boats that operate in the short straits that are principally there to intercept migrant boats. The cutters, of which there are four, are coastal patrol vessels whose job is law enforcement, interdicting drugs and dealing with criminality. They are supported by some smaller coastal patrol vessels. Their function is not to do rescues, and they are not suitable for it because they have high freeboard and they are not designed to be able to do that.

But because we had it down there—it berths at Portsmouth—we were able to transfer the migrants to it, because of the difficulty that we had seen the first time with public disorder. We worked with the local chief constable down there, Alexis Boon. We thought that was the least-risk option, to move them by sea, but that is not a standard response for us to be able to do that, and we cannot always guarantee that we will be able to do it.

Q59 Ben Maguire: So why was it that the RNLI picked them up in that? Who should have been picking them up? You said your cutters should not, so who should have been picking them up?

Duncan Capps: We patrol the short straits principally, where the majority of the risk is.

Q60 Ben Maguire: Your cutters would not, so who should have been there? You mentioned the coastguard.

Duncan Capps: Yes; but bear in mind, the thing that people do not actually realise that the coastguard in this country does not have its own boats. The coastguard works under the Department for Transport, but it does not have its own boats, which is why we have set the operation up in the short straits. The coastguard, if there is a mayday or an emergency will task any vessel that happens to be there or the Royal National Lifeboat Institution, which in this case was what responded to the vessels coming across.

They were in the right place. To put it into perspective, it is a seven-hour steaming for a boat to come from Dover to that area, so they could not be on task—and that day I think we had 500 arrivals in the short straits. They were occupied with that. The RNLI responded to that as it would to any other.

Q61 Ben Maguire: If that happens again, presumably the RNLI will be called into action again.

Duncan Capps: No, because we have an operational contingency in place where we will look to do those rescues.

Q62 Ben Maguire: Is that a new contingency that you did not have in place at the time?

Duncan Capps: That is correct.

Q63 Chair: How long did the crossing take?

Duncan Capps: The best part of two days. I think by the time they got to Dover they had been on the water for about 40 hours. By the time they were intercepted it was 30 hours. It is extraordinary. I can see what you are thinking: if that happened at this time of year, as the temperatures go down. Bear in mind that a lot of those people will have fuel burns from when the petrol mixes with the salt water in the bottom. I really do worry about this.

Q64 Ben Maguire: May I ask one more question? Sorry, I know we are running out of time. We touched again earlier on the protest, and also on people interfering with the rescue of small vessels. Could you tell us a bit more? It is obvious that that is hampering operations. You mentioned the contingency to stop the RNLI having to step in, but what contingencies have you put in place for this?

Duncan Capps: There are two questions. The first is about where the RNLI brought the migrant boat ashore at Eastney marina. You saw protesters in the water and at the tents that were rapidly set up to try to process the migrants, interfering with all of that. There were a number of arrests. That was really difficult.

Q65 Chair: Can we be clear that that is a potential impact on public safety? You cannot identify who the migrants are, and they could potentially just disappear. You would not know who they were, and the public are in danger because of that.

Duncan Capps: Yes, 100%. I do not need to say to you that there are safeguarding issues and vulnerable people. That is where we do the immediate lifesaving: getting people into dry clothing so that no one gets hypothermia, and making sure that no one is suffering from secondary drowning. All of that was really difficult because of the violence. You might remember that some of the people who were in the water were subsequently rescued by the RNLI, towards whom they were being violent. It was extraordinary.

For the second landing, we moved them by sea, so there was not an issue. The third one was at night. We could not do that and cross-deck, so the police put in a very significant operation to protect the site where we brought the migrants ashore, to allow us to do the initial triage and medical support and then move them out by boat. However, I will seek to avoid having to do that in the future by making sure that I have a Border Security Command vessel in that area to do those interceptions if we need to, while all the time trying to stop them in the first place.

Ben Maguire: That was my next question.

Duncan Capps: Obviously, it is about cutting it off at source. To be clear, the French want to shut this down as much as we do, for various obvious reasons. That is my main effort over this winter period.

Q66 Chair: To conclude, because I am conscious that we will suddenly hear some bells and Parliament will start again: from the public’s point of view, is it better for you to intercept these boats and process the individuals. They are going to arrive anyway, and we do not want them arriving without you having the chance to deal with them and potentially identify the criminals and others who are there.

Duncan Capps: It is just that—and, of course, to make sure, because we are a humane country, that people do not die. We broadly have an incredible record for saving life when you look at the number of people who come across.

Rob Jones: I agree with all of that. It is vital that you fix identity and get to them, and you can only do that if you intercept them and bring them back. It is an interception, and a consequence of that is that you save life. All of that is positive.

Q67 Chair: To conclude on the gangs, you must know who the gangs are, where they are from and what work is being done to tackle them. Is there work being done diplomatically with their source countries?

Rob Jones: Yes, there is. We have talked before about the over-representation of Iraqi Kurds and Afghans in this. There are some now who have come in to this from sub-Saharan Africa, and we are working on all of those to break the links that they have to Europe and to prevent them from moving people to Europe. They are getting arrested and disrupted at a scale that we have never done before.

Duncan Capps: I just want to say how much I appreciate the work of Rob and his team; I pay tribute to that.

Chair: We do appreciate that you are working in both the public and national interest, and we are very grateful for it.
