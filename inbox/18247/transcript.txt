Eddie Dempsey: I understand there have been some technical delays that relate to land ownership around one of the stations and the fact that the contract was not let until later than it should have been due to the general election. Jodie Lofthouse: I share the frustration that residents along the route have and share their ambition to get these services open as soon as possible. Tony Baxter: I agree with a lot of what the panel have said. A lot of progress has been made in terms of the three areas of operational readiness, which Chiltern is involved with. As Dave has said, our position has been quite consistent that from a sequencing point of view we need to have a resolution with the RMT and between the RMT and Chiltern to allow us to finalise those conversations with the ASLEF. Chair: You indicated that the discussions with the RMT are not the only issue. What are the other issues that are a factor towards the 744 days?

Tony Baxter: In terms of the other issues, which are now largely complete, the station is ready. From a trains point of view, we have completed modifications that drove some of the delay, which is regrettable. That is now complete.

The key sequence of events is to reach agreement with the trade unions, which is a live discussion.

Chair: For the unions, particularly the RMT, is the dispute primarily around passenger safety, job security, or just the fact that you feel they did not talk to you early enough?

Eddie Dempsey: It is about passenger safety. We have maintained a position around maintaining the safety of the second safety-critical person on trains.

Chair: We are going to come on to the detail of that. It is primarily safety as far as you are concerned.

Eddie Dempsey: Yes. We initially reached out to talk to people in 2024 and have had a series of conversations with people in industry and Government since but we did not start formal consultation around staffing matters until July of this year. That is one of the reasons why we are disappointed. We think this could have been dealt with sooner.

Chair: What would a satisfactory settlement look like as far as you are concerned?

Eddie Dempsey: It is very simple. We are talking about very few staff—less than 40—and a salary difference of about five grand for a second safety-critical person with a role in the dispatch process.

Chair: Is that five grand between having the dispatch role and not having a dispatch role?

Eddie Dempsey: What is being proposed presently is that 31 customer service staff would work on the trains in a customer service role, and we are saying to give those staff the full suite of safety responsibilities for train evacuation, protection of the line, and all the other duties we have seen guards undertake in derailments recently. The difference in salary is about five grand, so this could be resolved.

Chair: This is not necessarily about door opening and closing?

Eddie Dempsey: We are talking about a role in the dispatch process. It is not an argument about who presses a button to open a door; it is about who has a role in managing the platform-train interface process. We are talking about less than 40 staff and somewhere between 150 grand to 200 grand annually. It is probably 0.3% of the payroll bill to resolve this. We are in negotiations and we are hoping can get a resolution.

Chair: We will move on and may pick up the details of that in subsequent questions.

Alex Mayer: I would like to start with Tony: why did Chiltern Railways take the decision to propose driver-only operation on the line, and did you anticipate any opposition from the unions?

Tony Baxter: I can answer that in terms of a little of the history. The original specification for the East West Rail was as driver-only operated, which means there is one person with full safety accountability including operating of the doors and dispatch. That formed part of the specification for East West Rail when Chiltern became the operator, and it acted under that specification in March 2025. Chiltern supported that though; it was not imposed on Chiltern. Chiltern operates around 73% of its services at the moment within driver-only operations. We were selected following a selection criteria and process that the Department did as well. Does that fully answer the question?

Alex Mayer: Yes, basically. Eddie, at the moment how many members of staff do the trains between Oxford and Bicester Village have on them?

Eddie Dempsey: They run a driver-only operation presently.

Chair: Before we go any further on the details of this, can we just be quite clear that driver-only operation, DOO, refers to just one person on the train who is a driver and DCO is the driver-controlled operation but the assumption when we talk about DCO is that there is a second person on the train, and the discussion is about what they are doing, not whether there is a second person on the train because that has been resolved.

Eddie Dempsey: Yes.

Alex Mayer: We understand the RMT’s position that a second safety-critical member of staff is essential. Is there anything different between the part of the line between Oxford and Bicester Village and the new bit of the line that makes a second safety-critical member of staff more important?

Eddie Dempsey: Not particularly but if you are going to staff a train it makes better sense to have that person fully trained with the ability to operate the full suite of safety responsibilities. It does not seem to make sense to staff the trains but then not have that person able to carry out the full suite of safety responsibilities that a fully trained guard would have.

Alex Mayer: You were saying before that that second safety-critical member of staff would have responsibilities for evacuation, for example, which we could all understand. Could you particularly explain the issue of dispatch? Across quite a lot of the railway—we have just had some stats—drivers are managing to operate the doors. Why is that safety critical?

Eddie Dempsey: The platform-train interface is very important because it is where we find an overrepresentation of incidents where people are hurt on parts of the network that are driver-only operation. Out of the train operating companies run by the DFT there is only one that is fully driver-only operation. The majority of services are still run with a safety-critical guard on board. There are some companies where there is a mix of driver-only and guarded operation but we see about 70% of incidents related to the platform-train interface happen on the DOO services: traps and drags, people falling down between the train and the platform, and so on.

Where you have somebody responsible for the dispatch process it allows the driver to be responsible for the movement of the train and it allows that person to deal with issues around accessibility—people with additional needs getting on and off—and incidents that happen in that area. We have seen plenty of occasions where it has in fact been indispensable to have someone with those safety responsibilities on board. The most recent event is the derailment near Bedford and of course the tragic events that happened on East Coast last year. For the sake of maybe £150,000 to £200,000-plus on the wage bill a year it is important to have those safety responsibilities and competencies. It is probably best value for the public and a better settlement overall rather than staffing the trains but staffing them with people unable to carry out those safety roles and responsibilities.

Alex Mayer: In terms of a person hopping on and off the train to dispatch it, sometimes as a passenger I feel they spend a lot of time getting on and off and perhaps are not walking up and down the carriages as much, particularly on busier services, so they might not spot other issues on board the train, such as antisocial behaviour and so on. Is that an issue that you recognise that if somebody is literally having to get off the train at each station, press a button and so on, that they might not be manning the full length of the train as much as somebody whose job was literally to walk up and down it rather than have a role at each individual station?

Eddie Dempsey: All those things are important, but please bear in mind that on a lot of our network accessibility is important. A lot of incidents—antisocial behaviour or otherwise—do not just happen in the carriages of a train. Having a person with a role in the dispatch process is important as well as the other duties carried out. I do not see a conflict between the two, if I am honest.

Alex Mayer: Tony, did you undertake any route-specific risk assessment in terms of driver-only operation being safe on that bit of East West Rail?

Tony Baxter: Yes. In terms of the safety validation, we have done a lot of work around that. We cannot finalise safety validation until we have a method of operation agreed but we are very confident there is nothing unique or particular to that line of route that is different to the rest of our route that will present any risks. To remind the Committee in terms of the safety element discussion, the original proposal was for driver-only operation. On reflection after taking feedback around the role, the second person on board can play in providing customer service, visibility assurance and so on, we compromised and set a new position in July. We made the proposal to the RMT to place the customer service inspectors, which is a current role we already have on train. They have some elements of safety roles and responsibilities, which I know the public are very interested in in terms of assurance for people who may be travelling at night—extra visibility, antisocial behaviour, and so on—but obviously not for the dispatch of the train. We will finalise the safety validation once we have agreed the method of operation. Previous evidence submitted to this Committee from the Office of Rail and Road has set out the safety points around this and providing the right infrastructure and training in place is deemed a safe method of operation by a safety regulator.

Steff Aquarone: Eddie, to take a step back from the issue of the low six-figure difference between roles, which as a just-in-case thing sounds like a very small amount of money, it is a much bigger amount of money to put these customer service inspectors on when previously it was a driver-only operated proposal. Our local officials and reps and the management side at Chiltern Railways get through industrial relations very well and have done for some time. This is one area where that has not landed sadly but we are in negotiations as we speak and are hopeful we can make progress.

Eddie Dempsey: I have been a driver of driver-only operated services. I worked on the railway in a number of different roles and for me this goes right to the heart of what sort of railway do we want to have. Do we want to have a de-staffed dystopian network where passengers—

Tony Baxter: You are absolutely right; we share that concern around the whole aspect of antisocial behaviour, crime on the railway, and violence against women and girls, which is a key priority for the department.
