Dr Arthur: That is interesting. Thank you. The project has moved from a connection model to being delivered in phases. What does this mean? What is the difference there? As somebody who might want to use the network, what difference will that make to me?

David Hughes: It is partly a change in terminology, but it is more than that. It is probably one that is best for Ben to answer.

Ben Rule: As David said, to some extent it is just terminology. In moving from the connection stages to key outputs, we have done two things: first, we had to react to the fact that the Universal attraction that is to be built at Stewartby was proposed. We needed a plan that dealt with the arrival of that theme park. We had to adjust what we were doing to provide services in line as far as possible with the arrival of the theme park. That was the first thing.

Dr Arthur: What drove you to do that then? Were you told you had to do that, or did you just think this would be a good thing to do? Did you see it as an opportunity to raise revenue?

David Hughes: It was all of the above.

Dr Arthur: Why would you not have done that anyway right from the start?

Ben Rule: That is a good question. We are making sure that the schedule we are now working to delivers as many benefits as early as possible. David Hughes: The intent of the original approach was to open this in three phases. It is not revolutionary what we have done; we have just broken it down into slightly more phases. It is good practice to break it down into bite-sized chunks. There was also a very clear steer from Government in early 2025, when it was clear that Universal was going to go ahead. They asked us, “How can you adapt that opening schedule to get as much service into the park as quickly as possible? Alongside that, what else can you do as part of the overall economic growth agenda? How can you bring forward benefits in Cambridge, in Bedford? How can you sequence delivery of the railway to ensure that we get as much of the benefit as soon as possible?”

All those elements came together to drive what is not a massive change. It was always intended to open in phases; we just broke it down into slightly more phases with the intent of bringing forward benefits, in particular, as Ben says, trying to get as much of a service to the park as soon as we can. The park is huge. It will be the UK’s biggest tourist attraction, and it is literally 100 metres from one of our stations. We were going to have to adapt the design of the railway to cater for that.

Dr Arthur: I do not want to reopen the debate, but it is interesting that there is a focus on delivering economic benefit as early as possible. It would be interesting to see the benefit of changing your plans compared to the impact of the dispute. Let us not reopen that.

David Hughes: If I may, Dr Arthur, just close out, we have talked already about learning lessons. No one is happy with the position we are in on phase 1. The key thing for Rosemary, Jodie, myself and Ben is how we make sure, when we get to these future phases, that we are not in the same position. The first key lesson that we have taken away is about clarity of accountability. You heard witnesses in the previous session refer to the fact that accountability was not as clear as it should be.

In the first phase of the railway, Oxford to Bletchley, where we do not have services running, accountabilities were split—there is a lot of history around that—between what the Department was responsible for and what East West Rail was responsible for. For the first phase, East West Rail had a responsibility for the infrastructure but not for the introduction of services. Going forward, it is clear now that East West Rail Ltd holds accountability for the whole system. For future phases, Ben and I do not have direct control of everything, but we are the conductor of the orchestra. Our job is to make sure that, when we get to these future phases, we have not just delivered the infrastructure, important though that is, but all those other elements are in place that are necessary to bring passenger services into operation. That is the first lesson: clarity of accountability.

The second—again there was reference to this in the previous session—is getting ahead of the issues early. When I came in as chief executive just over two years ago, one of the first things I did was to bring in Ben, someone with a huge amount of rail operations experience, in order to tackle the sorts of issues that delayed us on the first phase now, several years in advance. You heard the union reps saying, “We didn’t get into consultation on these issues until relatively late in the day.” One of the big lessons from large rail projects is there is a risk of focusing on the infrastructure in the early days. You need to focus on the whole system from the get-go, and that is what we are trying to do to ensure that there is no repeat of where we are now when it comes to opening up future phases of the railway.

Dr Arthur: That is good. In this place, I have to keep reminding myself that the world does not end at Oxford and Cambridge. Is there scope for the line to go beyond these places at some point? Is that a possibility? Is there a business case there?

Ben Rule: It is part of the network and connected in many places to its wider parts. For example, freight will use East West Rail to connect ports and depots across the network. Ben Rule: There absolutely is.

Dr Arthur: Do these numbers make sense?

David Hughes: They absolutely do. Ben will explain why.

Ben Rule: There is a strategy. The wording as you have described is, “The railway that we will upgrade and build has capacity for an hourly service along the line of route.” Whether that is taken up or not depends on other investments. David Hughes: To build on that, the strategy is driven partly by constraints on existing network, but largely by what the demand flows are. If you look at the numbers cold, it does not make a lot of sense, but it reflects both the demand flows and the underlying capacity in the network.

David Hughes: No. If those constraints were unlocked in the future, it would be relatively easy to make sure that the East West Rail infrastructure could accommodate that increase in freight.

Olly Glover: East West Rail has been a bit of a journey in terms of the delivery timescales it has been working to. David Hughes: I question whether those original timescales were ever plausible. At the moment, if everything goes to plan, the earliest we would get our development consent order is late 2028, early 2029. For the bulk of the work that we have to deliver, we cannot get started until late 2029. Over the course of the next 12 months, we have to inform Government’s decisions about the project in terms of whether they want to commit to funding it. Over the next year, my team need to develop a very clear understanding of the likely cost envelope and delivery schedule. Our best estimate is that we will complete the project in mid to late 2030s. David Hughes: We present our OBC to the Department—I will use a season—in the summer; we are thinking around June/July.

David Hughes: We will go for a month. Around June or July of next year we will present to Government our outline business case and our draft DCO submission. David Hughes: That would happen after the DCO award, and so we plan for that to be late 2028.

Olly Glover: I have a final question for you, Mr Hughes. Over the summer, I read a book, “The Delivery Gap” by Jonathan Simcock. I need to say that up front I am on the list of the guilty.” You have said a little about what you think can be learned from previous projects. What else do you take from that time, and maybe HS2, as to how East West Rail will be different and how we can learn positively from those projects?

David Hughes: I could talk for hours on that. In terms of where we are now with the project, the key lesson that my team hear me banging on about all the time is making the right judgment about cost envelope and schedule. I talk about it a lot with Mark Wild, chief executive of HS2; he and I worked on Crossrail together. We want to set an ambitious cost envelope and an ambitious schedule. We want to drive the project hard so we get the most efficient delivery and can realise benefit as quickly as possible; but at the same time, we have to guard against the over-optimism that so many projects have fallen prey to. I do not want to be guilty of coming to Government next year with too rosy a picture on cost and schedule in order to secure the funding. What we need to do—and this is a lesson from HS2—is flush out now our best possible view of that cost and schedule to inform the Government’s decision about whether they want to commit to this scheme. Flush that out now, not after the event, not after you have let your main works contracts. Give your best possible judgment of that cost and schedule range.

Alex Mayer: We had consultations in 2019, 2021, 2024 and 2026. Is that it?

David Hughes: We are not planning on any further route-wide consultations. That is it; but that does not mean it is the end of engagement with communities and stakeholders along the line of route.

Alex Mayer: For anyone who wants to answer—Christian was nodding away—will the next time that the public participate be in examining that application for the DCO?

David Hughes: In a route-wide sense, yes. We are in Buckinghamshire this morning; we were in Bedfordshire last night; we are in Lidlington tomorrow. It is quite an important point for us. The Chancellor made the point that there had been four consultations. The Planning and Infrastructure Act changed that process. If it had been on the statute book several years ago, our whole approach would have been different. By the time it came on to the statute book last December—it makes significant changes to the pre-application process for the DCO—we had already done three route-wide consultations and we were just gearing up to go out with our fourth. The new Act will change how this is done in the future.

I would make two points. First, the consultations have been hugely valuable in refining our design. The feedback we get from the public has resulted in some quite significant changes to our design. Secondly, there is a danger that people think the Planning and Infrastructure Act changes mean we are not going to consult any more. When I answer your question saying we do not have any more planned consultations, that does not mean we are not talking to the public. The new legislation enables us to have much better and much more iterative engagement, such as we are having this week, with communities on the issues that matter to them.

The thing about the route-wide consultations is they are big, set-piece events. Before you even get out of the door, doing a three-month route-wide consultation puts six to nine months into your programme. The big win going forward is the Act enables you to engage in a much more iterative way with communities on the things that matter to them. For example, in terms of Bicester, the main thing they want to talk about is what we are doing with their level crossing. It is not the only thing they want to talk about, but they are not really interested in what we are doing in Cambridge East. Moving away from these route-wide consultations opens up the opportunity for us to engage in a much better way and at the same time speed up the process.

Alex Mayer: There are still decisions to be made. You mentioned Cambridge East. Is there any possibility that you will feel the need to consult there again ahead of next year?

David Hughes: We do not have plans to do so at the moment. I think I am right in saying it is not unusual in a DCO process to have targeted consultations if changes emerge. We have another year to go before we submit the DCO application. We do not anticipate significant changes to the scope of the scheme at this stage; we certainly do not anticipate running any route-wide consultations. It is possible that we need to do some targeted engagement if there were to be any significant changes at a local level in the route design. That might happen, but it is not part of our plan.

Alex Mayer: You mentioned that, had the legislation been in place earlier, it potentially would have changed the way you did those first four consultations. Are you making any changes going forward because the legislation is now in place?

David Hughes: The biggest impact of the Planning and Infrastructure Act is on the pre-application process. Believe it or not, it will enable us to get to the point of DCO submission this time next year more quickly than would otherwise have been the case. It has limited impact on what happens when you get into the examination stage, and the Government are now looking at this more broadly. It comes back to Mr Glover’s point. If you step back, East West Rail was initiated in 2017, and the earliest we will have spades in the ground is 2029/2030, i.e. 12, 13 years. There are all sorts of reasons for that, but it is not on a lower Thames crossing sort of timescale. It is part of a much bigger debate about how long it takes to get major infrastructure spades in the ground. We are part of that debate. The new legislation has definitely helped. We have mainly captured the benefit of it in the pre-application phase. It does not change the bar very much in terms of what we have to demonstrate in the examination itself.

Alex Mayer: Does anyone else want to say anything?

Jodie Lofthouse: The only thing I would add to that is, on this project, we have been engaged on the Planning and Infrastructure Act and the opportunities that it afforded very early on. We worked really closely and set up governance across Whitehall to make sure that we take on board and utilise opportunities wherever possible. David talked about a number of those. There is always more to do and more to consider. Utilising opportunities for East West Rail also allows us to identify and demonstrate other opportunities we can draw out of that so that we can leave more of a legacy from this project to support other major projects that are coming up behind us.

Samantha Dixon: Mr Irwin, how has the East West Rail project affected the delivery of Network Rail projects across the region?

Christian Irwin: The East West Rail project was set up by us in 2015 under an alliance with some brilliant delivery partners. There has been a standalone alliance team in existence for nearly 11 years that has worked in full collaboration with East West Rail Company, our operators and the Department for Transport, but also on discharging the Transport and Works Act order and engaging with communities through the delivery of the programme. Given the standalone nature of the alliance, we had a very lean team to manage delivery, the equivalent of 10 full-time staff, so it is a very cost-effective way for us to deliver our infrastructure projects.

In terms of the project itself, while we and all our colleagues are very disappointed not to have passenger services running right now, if you look at how this was delivered using a modern form of NEC contract, using the Government construction playbook in how we collaborate and build target costs; during the life of the alliance, through the design stage we were able to reduce the cost by nearly £300 million. Through the delivery stage, we saved £60 million and handed that back to the Department for reinvestment into future stages of East West Rail. During the construction period, which was very challenging through Covid-19—construction phases started at the back end of 2019 into 2020—we managed to offset nearly £300 million-worth of headwinds that impacted the project. From a delivery front, we are really proud about what we have been able to learn through the life of this project.

If you look at our future investment projects, certainly big programmes such as the midlands rail hub, the west coast main line modernisation, TransPennine route upgrade, there are a phenomenal amount of good lessons that will also affect future delivery. You made the point earlier: we need to act and operate collectively as an industry. It cannot be that one part delivers its bit and the other parts say. We are a railway and we need to deliver outcomes. Over the last 10 years, rail investment has been on a journey of shifting from being infrastructure-focused to being very much outcome-focused. The reality is, the East West Rail line will deliver phenomenal transportation outcomes. Let us not forget that the outcomes go far beyond transportation in terms of connectivity, access to jobs, employment, education and gross domestic product growth. That is what we need to focus on and why it is so important to have a fully integrated team under David’s stewardship, with Network Rail, operators and East West Rail Company, DFT, all aligned on the outcome. That is the lesson we take going forward.

Dr Arthur: Christian, of course, the 10 FTE lean team would sound a lot better if the project was running better, would it not? Yes? You said you have learned valuable lessons from the project. Were the problems the project has been beset with inevitable? If Network Rail had been delivering it, would some things not have happened?

Christian Irwin: I will just replay the point I just made. We as an industry are accountable. We work with our partners over in East West Rail Company. We are an arm’s-length body of the Department for Transport. While we were brilliant at specifying the infrastructure—"Great, we can crack on and build that, we know how to do that"—what we got wrong at the start of this programme was we did not specify the operation, the end outcome, how the rail system was going to come together. The railway system is very complicated. It is not just about the hard and soft infrastructure, operations and maintenance. You heard this morning; it is about how we operate the trains, dispatch and make sure passengers get where they need to be.

Dr Arthur: Are you saying these problems would have happened if Network Rail had delivered the project?

Christian Irwin: As I said, the journey that the industry has been on over the last five to 10 years has been flipping our investment approach from infrastructure very much into outcomes. We learned those lessons on Network Rail’s self-delivered projects as well as those delivered by other people. The reality is that it is a learning approach.
