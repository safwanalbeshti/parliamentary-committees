Lord Hannay of Chiswick: There were countries at the WTO that first prevented there being a multilateral agreement—principally the United States but certainly not only the United States—and then those who have also prevented the agreement. We were looking at being anchored in the WTO in a formal way but have been driven to proceed in the margins, as it were, of the WTO.

Can you characterise the main reasons that countries have given for not allowing this to be a multilateral instrument and to be anchored in the WTO? To what extent are those reasons simply classical uses of the unanimity requirement at the WTO, which people use to create leverage for trade concessions—possibly in a completely different field—that they are seeking? To what extent are they real, and ones which both can and should be addressed as this agreement—which is presumably in effect a living agreement, not simply a static one—moves along in the years ahead?

Chris Basiurski: I would say that there are three main reasons why some other members have been blocking incorporation of the ECA and other plurilaterals into the framework. The first is that we often hear a disagreement on the role of plurilateralism itself. The very nature of the WTO is to move away from plurilaterals, although we would argue that it is still a valid and necessary part of the global trading system to have plurilateralism. There are other members who would disagree and say that incorporation of the WTO means that we should move forward only at a multilateral level. There is a theoretical objection that we often hear from multiple members; they are a small minority but they are still there.

A second one is concern over the impacts of any plurilateral agreement, including this particular one. Those who are not members of it and not part of the negotiations on it will have to assess whether they are harmed by it or whether they have legitimate interests or concerns that would be impacted by it. That they have, perhaps, not a lack of understanding through reading it, but a lack of understanding of its impact in the future without it having been witnessed and seen has made some members reluctant to want to go forward. They do not really know what it means for them.

On that particular point, one angle is that we should be looking to talk as a community about it and how we can address legitimate concerns. There may well be a time when another subset of members will want to set up a plurilateral agreement to which we are not a party, and we would want to assess its impact on us and maybe address any negative impact and any harms that we might perceive. That is as important for us as it is for them.

The third reason I would bracket among the diplomatic and geopolitical considerations as well as perhaps being a negotiating tactic. In the current climate, it is very difficult to come up with any multilateral agreements. We talk about managing the negative externalities of the ways that different economies and different political systems work, but that makes it really difficult on the ground to negotiate agreements. Of course, there is probably an element of negotiating tactics among that. People who have concerns and want to make progress in other areas will want to see progress in those other areas before perhaps making some concessions in the things that we want. A really important message that we are trying to portray among ourselves but also with others is that we have to listen to other people’s concerns rather than just try to tell them what we want. That is where we are trying to strike the balance in this.

Lord Hannay of Chiswick: So what you are saying, if I have got it right, is that we as a country are going to be fairly active in trying to persuade non-signatories of this plurilateral agreement to join it, and thus to increase the chances of that by addressing the problems that they say they have up to now over joining it. Is that the sort of policy that we will pursue over the next few years, or will we just sit there, fold our hands and pray that somehow or other they will see the light and understand what a huge advantage it will be for them to join?

Chris Basiurski: There are two angles to this. There is trying to persuade them of the merits of joining the agreement, and there is trying to persuade them not to try to block the incorporation of the agreement. Those are two different things. There are some countries that we just know will not join the ECA, but we have been actively trying—we will continue to do this, and we are trying to find new avenues to do it—to recognise that they may have legitimate concerns and try to mitigate those, providing opportunities for dialogue for people to raise their concerns, and to demonstrate to them that they are not disadvantaged as a result of it and provide opportunities for them to come back to us in future if they feel that in practice it has gone against them. That is something that we are doing actively in the process of reform.

We are actively engaged in Geneva discussions. In fact, the committee may have seen that we released a paper a few weeks ago on the topic of plurilaterals. At this stage the conversations in Geneva are not about coming up with the answers but with the right scope for negotiations. We put in a paper that is trying to move the conversations in the way that we would like them to move in order to try to give assurance to those countries that are not going to be members of a particular plurilateral that they have opportunities for legitimate concerns to be heard and addressed and then for any negative impacts to be mitigated. However, those fears of potential impacts do not stop the rest of us being able to move forward.

It is a balancing act. As I say, one day we might be on the other side of this. We would then want our interests to be protected as well, but hopefully in a way that allowed those who wanted to go forward to do so in a way that would do no harm. We would certainly be open to that prospect, and hopefully we will be able to persuade other countries through the reform process to almost trust in having a process that will mean that they are not disadvantaged by it.

Lord Hannay of Chiswick: Is the paper that you spoke about something that you could share with the committee?

Chris Basiurski: Absolutely. It is a public document; it is one of those WTO communications that are released by the WTO but was written by us. We are happy to share the link.

Chris Basiurski: I will turn to Ayesha in a moment on the second half of that and what more might be done.

On the risks in taking this approach, from a practical point of view, the biggest legal reason for trying to incorporate in Annex 4 is to be included within the legal framework. From a practical point of view, going ahead with this interim measure has opened up some debates in Geneva about legitimacy and—as in the previous question from Baroness Lawlor—about the use of the secretariat, the use of the director-general and so on. That was always factored into our decision to go ahead anyway. However, we would not say it is outside the framework: we would say that it is within the framework, but has not yet been incorporated. As Ayesha said a few minutes ago, the negotiations came from the JSI processes. The whole process has been a WTO one but we simply do not have the annex incorporation at the moment.

For us, the question then was what would happen if we did not go ahead? What were the risks if we left it unimplemented and left, waiting for a day when an event would perhaps allow it to be unblocked? We did not see that as imminent or realistic in the short timeframe. It did not happen at MC14, which I think is what we all hoped for but did not expect. The e-commerce moratorium was not continued after MC14, which would have been a potential way around it. From my side of this, it also would not allow the small number of countries that are not part of the coalition of the willing to block it, whatever their reasons were.

We talked earlier about the balance of interests. We still needed to be able to go ahead, and to think that we have legitimate polylateralism. We stand behind this agreement and think it was right to go ahead. We want it to be interim, and to find our way into annex 4 in future, but the previous witnesses and Ayesha talked about the benefits that this gives. It can give tremendous benefits. There is also the risk of not utilising those benefits, and then not continuing negotiations on other things. I know you want us to reflect on various protections such as on source code, but opening up future negotiations to broaden this out can happen only once we demonstrate that this thing can operate, it works and can, we hope, get incorporated in the future.
