My overall priority, which is an easy one to set and much harder to deliver, is to make sure that we are ready to deter and, if necessary, defeat those who would do us harm. That means strengthening our capability, our defence industrial base and our alliances.

The industrial piece sits across all those things, providing the capabilities we need and working with allies to develop those capabilities where we can. In an environment where NATO asks us to be ready to potentially fight and win a war, we need to make sure that we have not just the defence industrial base that we need now to meet our requirements, but also a wider industrial base that we would need in warfighting to sustain our warfighting machine and see us to victory. There is a big opportunity, as we do those things, to support the key planks of the Prime Minister’s overarching political agenda, which is to re-industrialise our country, backing Britain by buying British and providing opportunities, particularly for the next generation and those million or so young people who find themselves not in education, employment or training. We need to spend more, but I also have responsibility to my colleagues across government and to the taxpayer to make sure that that money is well spent.

Q100 Baroness Carberry of Muswell Hill: Good morning. Perhaps I could pick up on your answer there, Mr Pearce, about the delivery plan and focus in on some of the procurement processes that we have heard a lot about during this inquiry. In particular, we have heard over and over again anxieties about pace and speed of contract processes. In some cases we have heard about it taking six or seven years even to get to contract. Is the delivery plan addressing this and how is that particular issue going to be dealt with, so that we can speed up processes that, at the moment, do not appear to be commensurate with the threats of modern warfare?

Rupert Pearce: I completely agree with your concern and accept that something has to change within defence, and fast. It is very much part of what we are looking to support and achieve through the rolling out of the DIP, but it is being dealt with partly through other means. Inherent in the defence industrial strategy is working with industry to speed up and simplify our procurement processes. Indeed, it is called out in the SDR as something fundamental that has to shift.

The requirement for that to change has only got greater. The SDR, remember, assumed that we had 10 years to deliver this transformation. By the autumn of last year, the prognosis was that we had only five years. We have to accomplish a lot more change at the front end of this programme to result in capabilities arriving in time.

We are doing a lot here. If I look forward, we need to procure in more ambitious and well-calibrated ways. We have a tendency to over‑procure and to design for the exquisite, as opposed to the good enough. In some areas we want to be exquisite still, because it is a key capability to overcome our adversary. In other areas, good enough is, by definition, good enough and that is what we need to procure for.

We also need to think about ways in which we develop our capabilities in increments. We need to be more modular in the way we design capabilities so that we can have a baseline capability and then spiral develop from that over time, managing the risk inherent in long-term procurements in cleverer ways. That has become especially true given the amount that we are now procuring in software, not hardware. So many more things we are buying now are systems, or systems of systems, and we need to think about the implications for that as well.

That is why the two most profound things changing inside defence are that, as the Secretary of State just said, we are moving to an integrated force model, so we start to look across all of our domains and front-line commands to procure for them, and, at the same time, instead of being given requirements by the front-line commands, we are now given problem sets. That gives us the design authority in my group to think about how we solve those problems. At one stroke, we get much more room for manoeuvre in terms of how we procure and we are able to work with industry, innovators and our allies to think about how we can design the solution. The pace of that solution has become one of the key value criteria in the way we procure. The whole mindset and philosophy of procurement is changing.

I will then pivot very quickly to talk about some of the very practical things we are doing. We are trying to simplify our procurement and take layers out of it. We are trying to engage much earlier with industry in inviting it in to figure out how we can solve these problems. Above all, we are trying to stratify our procurement. Yes, those big platforms require a lot more planning and design, but why take the same approach for everything? If we stratify our procurement and develop proper commercial pathways for different types of procurement, we can really shorten things dramatically.

We have done that in terms of procurement for Ukraine and the story of Commercial X. Commercial X has made over 1,000 procurements in the last three years and its average pace from genesis to contract is 50 days. That is the kind of thing we need to be looking at. We need to take that model, amplify it, deepen it, resource it properly and do more like that. There are a lot of good green shoots going on. We are looking to take our big platforms down from an average of the six years you called out to two. We are looking to take our systems down from three years average to one and everything else to three months. We have metrics to track that and the team are working very hard to get there.

Wes Streeting: I can say, because I have not been around long enough to take personal credit for it, that, on major platforms, that has already come down from six years to just under five years since the publication of the SDR. There is definitely more to go at, but it shows that words are being matched by action, which I found reassuring in my early weeks in office.

Baroness Carberry of Muswell Hill: All that is very reassuring. Thank you. Can we look forward to a report in due course on how successful you have been in speeding up procurement in all the different segments?

Rupert Pearce: Absolutely, yes. I am a devotee of, “You can’t manage what you can’t measure”. We need the measurements and ambitious targets, and then we track ourselves against them. This is very much the way we report on the objectives of the SDR. A nice thing about the SDR and the defence industrial strategy for me is that they come with hard metrics of performance. We can build a lot more into our reporting, which we would be very happy to bring back to you.

Wes Streeting: A love of targets is another big difference between this department and my last one.

The Chair: It is a big culture change you are ahead of.

Rupert Pearce: The cultural change is almost the biggest shift. Naturally, in the environment in which we have been living for the last 20 or 30 years, there is very strong risk aversion. There is a focus on inputs, not outcomes, and a love of process, sometimes for the sake of process. It is a comfort blanket. It is a place of psychological safety. It is entirely understandable and normal.

If we are going to accomplish change in my group—national armaments—we have to find new places of psychological safety that are commensurate with what we face, which is the need for speed, the need to deliver transformation in our armed forces, the need to dramatically strengthen and deepen the capabilities of our supply base so it becomes a national strategic asset, and the need to innovate at pace. We could build great capabilities but if they are out of date in six months we have achieved nothing. Those things require a change of mindset.

The key for us as leaders is to create a working environment where people feel inspired and empowered to behave differently. We know we can achieve that because, when we lifted the system from them to support Ukraine, because Ukraine was willing to take more risk, we operated at an incredible pace. That is something we are phenomenally proud about. The people have the capabilities to do this. We have to provide a system in which they can flourish.

Q101 Lord Camoys: I was very keen to ask about the lessons from things such as Commercial X. Witnesses to our inquiry have identified both Commercial X and Taskforce Kindred as examples where contracts have been placed quickly by the MoD and innovation has been encouraged. Wes Streeting: As the Chair just alluded to, part of it is about providing not just political direction but political cover for a willingness to hold risk. Part of it is accepting this environment, where the nature of warfare is changing at a pace we have simply not seen before, if you think about where the war in Ukraine is today versus where it was at the start and the way in which the Ukrainians are innovating at a rate of knots. You have to be prepared to fail and to fail faster, to learn and then move on and adapt.

I am afraid that, in our world, “failure” is not a word we are comfortable with. Failure is often used to drive political embarrassment, Urgent Questions and parliamentary scrutiny, and sometimes with good reason. We do not aspire to fail, but, in a world in which our success relies on us being able to not just out-produce but out-innovate our enemies, part of a culture of innovation is accepting that, for every great idea you have, there are plenty of failures and setbacks you have to go through in order to arrive at that ultimate success.

Being comfortable with that as Ministers is important. We have a particular asset in the Minister for Defence Readiness and Industry, Luke Pollard, who absolutely gets this and has been a driving force and a champion for the defence reform that was put through by John Healey. Part of the challenge is making sure that we take everyone with us. The other opportunity we have is a very forward-leaning, modernising set of defence chiefs. In a world in which you are handing over problem sets rather than issuing requirements and trying to do that across the armed forces, that is a big shift and not one that everyone will be comfortable with. Sometimes, in this phase, it will involve saying to people, “We didn’t ask you for the specific requirement. We asked you for the problem”. That is a shift in culture behaviour. Initially people might encounter that as bureaucracy. Actually, it is the path to bulldozing the bureaucracy, waste and efficiency duplication that is holding us back.

With each session, I feel like I am going back six years to covid procurement. It feels to me that there are an awful lot of parallels.

Wes Streeting: With this Prime Minister, I am struck by his willingness to run into really big, hard things. We have seen that domestically with social care. The other big asset we have is the Chancellor. The Treasury as an institution is one I have come to know and love in my previous experience in government. For reasons I understand on the fiscal side of the Treasury’s responsibility, with an abundance of caution and learning from examples of waste, inefficiency and white elephants that government has seen over the years, it can ironically sometimes lend itself to thinking that more regulation, process, scrutiny and sign-off is the answer. It can slow us down and work against growth, value for money and productivity certainly.

Building a high-trust environment with the Treasury is really important. That helps with the Chancellor because he has done my job before. That can be a double-edged sword when people know the department well and can see through flannel. That is a good thing.

Therefore that is a good ground for us to speed things up. That is not an event; that is a process. We are going to have to continue. Just as the Treasury challenges us on value for money, doing what we say we will and delivering to time and to budget, we will also have to do a bit of upward management to the Treasury to make sure that we are operating in a high-trust as well as a high-performance environment.

Baroness Harding of Winscombe: I do not doubt our Prime Minister’s communication skills at all, as you set out. This is not a party political point. It was quite striking that defence was not mentioned in his party conference speech. Do you need him to make a big strategic statement in order to help give your officials more air cover to change the way they work, take more risks and innovate, as you describe?

Wes Streeting: In defence of the conference speech, it was about the vision for the condition of Britain. It was a vision for the kind of Britain we want to be here at home. There is no doubt in my mind that he understands and is concerned about the threat environment that we are operating in, is concerned about the risks that we and our allies are carrying, and is engaged internationally. Rupert Pearce: Baroness Harding, if it gives you comfort, I feel that I very much do have air cover for the change programme that the national armaments group is engaged in and I am leading. I find the Ministers enormously supportive of the process of change and actually asking how they can do more to support. Q103 Lord Best: My questions are all about the SMEs—the small and medium-sized enterprises. We have heard a lot from them. It is not only that they bring innovation, new ideas, entrepreneurial spirit and all that, but the large defence contractors depend upon them to scale up and do a lot more, and they face incredible barriers. I know you set aside £2.5 billion between now and 2028 for the SMEs, but here are some examples of things that they face: a brand-new start-up being asked for three years of audited accounts, a procurement document that managed to pass the 1,000 pages mark for an SME to deal with, security clearances that take more than six months for the individuals concerned.

Can I put some specifics that you might like to react to and see whether you are able to comply with these ideas? Could we have a single front door, so that there is somewhere for SMEs to go where they know their queries and issues are going to be discussed directly? It may be that the Defence Office for Small Business Growth gets expanded and does that job. Could we have a single standard contract for smaller SMEs, instead of these incredibly lengthy and difficult contracts? Wes Streeting: That is a great challenge. There is no doubt in my mind about the fact that we need to do much more to support SMEs to engage in opportunities that will come through the defence investment plan. We do not start from a bad place, but we do not start from a place that I think any of us would be happy or comfortable with. There is a lot more to go at.

Instinctively, I think the Defence Office for Small Business Growth is and should be the front door. It was established back in January this year, so it is still nascent. There is an opportunity, and actually a responsibility on us, as well as looking at what we can do to remove barriers in defence processes and drive up SME spend, to continually engage with the SME sector to see how that is working, as we are still in the early stages. The website, the contact centre and the guidance services are specifically designed to improve access and reduce confusion. The proof of the pudding will be in the eating: how do SMEs feel that that is going? How easy do they find it to engage?

Similarly, I absolutely take your challenge on payment terms in terms of making sure that we pay readily and on time for work that is being undertaken, but also recognising that, if we are dealing with our primes and, for all sorts of reasons we are all familiar with, a well-laid timescale does not quite go to plan, there is a big difference between a BAE or a Leonardo adjusting and reprofiling, and an SME, which is standing up the workforce and getting ready to do the work, being told, “We’re not going to be with you for another three months”.

Making sure that we are agile, responsive and supportive, and recognising the support that will be required where those sorts of things happen, goes back to what I said earlier about being a good partner. That is not just about being a strategic partner in terms of setting our overall strategy and thinking about procurement. It is also about how we work effectively on contract design and delivery.

In terms of the single standard contract, it is of course instinctively appealing to me, but I am a politician and I like simplicity. I will turn to Rupert to talk through how in practice that is doable.

Rupert Pearce: I am going to bounce that on to Ben in a second, having said that, because he is in charge of it. You are looking for facts. We deal with over 12,000 SMEs today. We do £5 billion a year of business with SMEs today, albeit only 25% of that number is direct, so a lot of them come through the primes. I want to change that. The extra £2.5 billion target by the middle of 2028 is actually growing our business by 50% with SMEs, so it is a very big ambition. That, in itself, has to drive a change of behaviour.

You have obviously seen and read the SME action plan. That is a holistic plan looking across all the things you are talking about: making access to capital easier and addressing payment terms, both direct and indirect. Actually, we pay SMEs very quickly. Sometimes what happens is that it goes into the primes and then the primes pay them slowly. That is what we have to address by setting expectations of standards with primes, without which they will cease to be primes.

We are looking to standardise contracts wherever we can and get rid of longevity. We now have a single front door, but we are not set up for 12,000 companies to come through that door at once. Somehow we have to digitalise that engagement as well, probably working through ecosystems and communities that bring SMEs to us.

I will hand off to Ben to talk about some of the granularity, because there are a lot of facts here that are moving in the right direction, When I meet with SMEs—and I would call out particularly the recent SME round table at Farnborough, which I attended with Min DRI—I see a complete change of tone now. There is much more trust, transparency and engagement. SMEs are coming up with ideas. We are taking those ideas and running with them.

For example, one reason why SMEs struggle to engage on the larger programmes is that they are very large and often require a lot of skillsets they do not have. They might be able to deal with one piece of the procurement, so they are forced to sit behind the primes that put together the front piece. If we procure in more modular ways, we give SMEs a chance to bid direct with us for the module. Better still, why do we not have a dating agency for SMEs to come together and collaborate in coalitions that can bid against a prime? Those are things I warmly encourage and endorse and we are looking to get after, but you cannot get there without the dialogue changing and without creating forums for SMEs to meet you and talk about their specific issues. Then we can start to fix them.

Ben White: The Defence Office for Small Business Growth is the front door. It started only in January, as the Secretary of State said. At the moment it is concentrating on those cross-cutting issues that we have identified affect all SMEs. That is the most powerful and impactful thing it can do immediately. As we scale it up, it will be able to receive more of these requests and deal with companies.

Dealing with 12,000 companies is a challenge, so we are still working through how we provide a level of service that is proportionate and achievable. A standard contract is a really good suggestion. One of our accelerated commercial pathways is specifically for SMEs, trying to make it easy for SMEs to work with us, taking all the best practice that we have and applying it, removing any barriers that we find along the way and that becoming a standardised approach for how to contract with SMEs. It does slightly depend on what type of product or service they offer as to which contracting route we would choose, but that exists now. It has been launched.
