Examination of witnesses

Gerald Vernon-Jackson, Lucy Butler and Rachael Wardell.

Q38 The Chair: I formally welcome you and ask you each to introduce yourselves to the committee and give your perspective on where we are up to in legislative finance and supply and demand within the care system. We will then go into formal questions.

Rachael Wardell: Good morning, everyone. I am the executive director for children, families and lifelong learning at Surrey County Council. I am here today representing the Association of Directors of Children’s Services and speaking on behalf of the sector. The few words that I would say about the current position of reforms is that—as you know—there is a huge package of reforms going through children’s services at the moment including the Families First Partnership programme, which is a really important focus on children’s social care, Best Start in Life and the package of SEND reforms.

The Families First reforms need to be fully implemented by the end of March so that process is under way at the moment. We are anticipating that this will have an impact on the care sector, both in terms of the number of children who come into care and potentially the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 and the way that care is provided to those young people. At the moment there is a real challenge in the sector and I hope we can explore that in this committee today.

Lucy Butler: I am really pleased to be here today. I am the chief executive of Home and Future, which is the South East Regional Care Cooperative. There are 19 local authorities in the region of the south-east. We have 15 signed up to the regional care co-operative and are working with 18 to implement the fostering reforms. Regarding where we are in terms of the reforms I can speak particularly to sufficiency and the work around that; probably less so on the wider things. I was previously a director in the south-east in two authorities so I have that background from a director’s perspective.

I would like to give two caveats about regional care co-operatives. First, we—the south-east—were one of the pathfinders with Greater Manchester. We are very new and are dealing with an issue that has been around for 30 to 40 years so it is not a quick fix. Secondly, you have just heard me say that we have 15 of the 19 local authorities signed up with us. We are not mandatory; it is a choice whether or not local authorities join us but obviously we will be more powerful together.

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: I am deputy chair of the Children, Young People and Families Committee at the LGA. I was a social worker a long time ago and worked in both residential and fieldwork. I then ran Portsmouth City Council for 15 years so I have both those bits of experience.

I want to highlight the financial pressure, which inevitably always happens and people know about, but we are having to put in about £1 billion a year across local authorities into children’s social care and that is not funded centrally. I was with a council earlier this week. Based on its savings target that it has to hit this year, it needs to find over £100 million in cuts.

I also want to highlight that the complexity of kids that we are looking after is growing. More and more young people have complex needs in mental health, substance abuse and exploitation. The number of children we are looking after fell slightly last year but overall it is growing, which is a big problem. We have issues of unregistered homes, which I am sure we will come on to talk about. I am really pleased Lucy is here from the regional care co-operative because councils have withdrawn from providing direct services over the last 25 years. Now we are probably having to go back into re-providing them and we need to think about how that happens.

My last point is that there is a real need for better co-ordination particularly with the health service. The number of beds available for children and adolescents with severe mental health problems has fallen enormously. We are the end of the road; the buck stops with us. Increasingly young people are being sent from the criminal justice system, for us to look after and not be locked up in prison, which is probably right for them but it produces an enormous problem for us. In my council we have one kid who we have been looking after costing £25,000 a week. That is not unusual and is becoming more and more common. Those are some things that would be interesting to talk through today.

Q39 The Chair: Those were very interesting opening remarks, which we will dig down into through our questioning. I would like you all—Lucy particularly—to contribute around the new care co-operative model that you are developing. You have touched on some aspects of it that you think will be helpful. Could you elaborate a bit more on how it is being rolled out, what the funding arrangements are with local authorities, how it will tackle sufficiency of supply in the system and the issue around boundaries that you are incorporating?

Lucy Butler: There is a lot there so you might have to prompt me if I forget some things. As I said we were one of the two pathfinders nationally along with Greater Manchester. We are very different: Greater Manchester is a combined authority that already had a relationship and was working with those 10 local authorities; in the south-east there was no real history of local authorities working together.

Something that we were focused on was how we could best collaborate to get a better deal for children. We have been looking at joint commission arrangements in the region and particularly working with health partners. In the south-east we have five ICBs and two provider collaboratives so it is not a simple picture. We have established a memorandum of understanding with the ICBs and provider collaboratives to work together to get better homes for children so there is a really strong ethos. However, that has not been replicated nationally; we have not seen the jointness there so that is something we are particularly interested in working on.

We are working with health and local authority colleagues to look at joint, integrated pathways of support and joint provision for children. We have also been supporting the instruction of end-to-end fostering in the region. As I said, the south-east is a really big region so to do that effectively we have been working sub-regionally; we have been working with five sub-regions. It is really important that we stay close to local authorities and place. We are looking at how we can bring the advantages of working in a more regional way to have better conversations with providers, have a bit more control and say and to do the things that work at a regional level.

We would say that local authorities are really good at commissioning services for children but there are some things that you need local authorities to come together to tackle. Those are usually the more difficult issues such as working together with health and really supporting children with more complex needs. That is where the power of regional care co-operatives comes in.

We have also been doing a lot of work around workforce, in particular residential workforce. What we are hearing is that they might not have the skills that are needed to support children with multiple needs, so we are looking at how we enhance training and support for residential workers. We are also looking at how we bring the voice of children and young people into the work that we do. We really want to hear from children who have experience of Tier 4 provision and of unregistered provision. We really want to support local authorities to have the right homes for children at the right time.

It is really interesting and sufficiency is a complex issue. People often say there are not enough homes for children but it is much more complex than that. I can give you a really simple example: we have 2,220 children who are living outside the south-east region and 1,936 children from outside the south-east who live in the region. That is just one example of how complex sufficiency is. How do we unpick that?

One really good thing about regional care co-operatives is that it will be a national initiative. Going back to that statistic I just shared with you, most children from the south-east live in London, and likewise most of the children coming into the south-east are from London, so we are doing a piece of work with London to look at how we might work together. Some issues can be tackled regionally and some need to be tackled nationally. I hope that answers your question and all the things you asked me.

Rachael Wardell: I agree very broadly with everything that Lucy has said about the advantages of regional care co-operatives and the benefit of working at scale and collaborating across boundaries. When Lucy refers to children with the most complex needs, they are quite low in number but are usually the children for whom it is most difficult to find a placement or we might be trying to find a placement in an emergency, because quite often what happens to these children is a form of crisis. That is often what leads to them being located outside their home local authority. Some things that Lucy was describing arise out of the conditions at the point at which a placement decision is made.

Once you have successfully found a home for a child in which they are doing well, moving them purely for geographical reasons does not make sense. You want them to stay somewhere where they are looked after and thriving. There are lots of complex moving parts. The thing that we can do very well together as an RCC is commission specialist provision at greater scale than any one local authority would need but that is responsive to those particular needs.

We have all referred to mental health as being a big issue. There are no longer the inpatient places that there used to be. Those children are probably better off outside hospital but they need specialist provision that meets their needs and they are often the children that we are the most worried about and who most need stability. They also need to be close to home because their existing networks—maybe their existing education, which I am sure we will come back to later in this session—family members and friends are all important to them. Having a solution that is regional and close is really important.

I do not think Lucy would disagree that RCCs are not the answer to everything and we should not try to get them to solve all the challenges. There are lots of things that local authorities already do well and many local authorities now are growing their in-house capacity and capability, which we should encourage. The local connection is vital and, where we can deliver something locally without needing to reach for that scale solution, that seems to be the most sensible and pragmatic way forward. The RCCs offer that additionality for those complex placements.

Lucy Butler: Yes.

The Chair: Do you not have a lever to pull?

Lucy Butler: No. You might want to ask Rachael this as well but there is something about the confidence in RCCs. The Chair: Could you remind me what the timetable is for assessment of the pathfinders with the view to rolling it out nationally? You said unless it is done nationally as well then there are still impediments in the revised system you are trying to put in place.

Lucy Butler: There are a further five RCCs that have just been established in the last few months, so they are very new. They are doing the groundwork that we did initially, which is bringing together data, establishing relationships and having conversations with their local authorities about what their priorities are. That does not cover the whole of England; it is 100 out of 150, so two-thirds. There are plans to roll it out further.

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: I used to work in a secure unit in a regionally provided place and, to be blunt, it was horrible: you locked kids in, the only exercise they had was in a yard because of the place it was in, et cetera. Regional care co-operatives are going to be enormously helpful but not quick. I would not expect a huge amount of change within five years because they are going to have to get up and running, so I do not expect things to happen too fast. We need to be absolutely clear about the scale of the problem. If we look at the number of kids we were looking after between 2018 and 2022, when we were spending more than £10,000 a week on a child, it rose from 120 to 1,500 in a four-year period and it has gone up from there. We have had one recently where we spent £25,000 a week looking after the child.

The bit that is important is that regional care co-operatives can really help at the high, specialist end of the market. We have to be really careful about saying they are the answer to everything. When you are looking after a kid it is really important to try to make sure that you maintain links with family, school and friends and moving them even 20 miles means that lots of those break down. The longer a kid is away from home, the more chance they will stay in care for a long time.

Trying to work to keep those links of school and family going is best done at a local level because we know what the connections are and how we can try to make sure we keep those. Regional care co-operatives are enormously good for the high end of the market; I would not want them to be able to solve the local stuff about ordinary fostering, children’s home places, kinship care and things like that.

Rachael Wardell: It may be that your later questions will explore this further. When we are talking about placements that cost £25,000 a week I would not want to give the impression that we are necessarily getting £25,000 a week of value out of those placements. I would not suggest that those necessarily meet a child’s needs better than lower-cost placements. Obviously for children with complex needs, their placements are likely to be more expensive and intensive and the staff will have specialist skills. We do not see value for money in those placements.

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: If you are a social worker and you have to try to make sure that the kid is safe, that is what you will do. That is the prime motivator, not what it costs. Your choice is maybe zero and that is a real problem.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: There are two things that have come to me. First, for a tiny proportion of children you will deliberately send them a long way away because of county lines or whatever. I just wondered what percentage that might be. Secondly, on the £25,000 a week, about 35 years ago I worked in an inpatient child and adolescent unit as a nurse. At today’s value I do not think it would be any more expensive. Do you think health is taking its responsibility seriously in terms of treatment? For some children—given the right treatment at the right time—it might be more costly then but if you look over a five-year period it is cost-effective.

Rachael Wardell: I will take the health question first. Health colleagues take their responsibilities very seriously but they are chronically under-resourced. Children’s health funding takes second seat to adult health funding and within that, mental health funding takes less. They are not able to respond in the way that they would want to.

There are areas of conflict. One obvious one is about children’s behavioural issues, which may or may not be driven by a mental health condition. Where something is labelled as behavioural and falls outside the purview of the health service to respond to it, we might have a very strong view that it is actually a mental health condition. We see that kind of conflict for sure.

Q40 Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Thank you for the very interesting answers so far; I am sure there will be lots more to come. I want to ask you about the market management measures contained in the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026. Do you think it is likely to make any difference to the situation that the DfE reported: that the children’s social care market is fundamentally broken? Will it manage to reshape the market in the way that the Government want?

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: The answer is probably no. I would hope that it would do better but the reality is no, partly because of the huge pressure on placements. Secondly, these are increasingly run by large organisations that are funded by private equity whose demand is for profit, profit, profit and profit and then some more profit. They are big enough to be able to do their accounts in a way that big corporations do. We have a rule in councils that any developer wanting to build that has profits less than 10% does not have to contribute towards social housing. Very few developers declare that they have more than 10% profit and we do not have the resources that they have to go through all their accounts and look at them.

It is the right thing to be doing and I welcome the Government’s change of heart from the previous Government to say they are going to look at it, but it is not going to make a huge difference. We are going to need much more power than is being proposed to be able to regulate that market and make a big change. Rachael will know; she has to sign the cheques.

Rachael Wardell: You are right, the answer is probably not currently constructive. There are very welcome measures in the Act but they are proposed as last resorts—for example, the profit cap. Probably as a sector we would like to see profit-making—certainly profiteering—providers, where the profit margins are very high, ruled out of this very important area of work.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: How are you going to do that?

Rachael Wardell: The only way is going to be through legislation. If the profit cap is used as a first resort rather than last resort—in other words, if we are active in the market straightaway—that becomes really important. If it is left as a last resort we will see continuing movement from organisations seeking to use this market to make high unwarranted profits.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Is there not a risk that the clever people who run the accounts of the companies will outwit the government officials?

Rachael Wardell: That is always a risk and the only way that you could do that is by outlawing profit altogether, which is not an option.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Is there a future for voluntarily and publicly run services?

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: There has to be. If we get to the position where it is only the private sector there, then they have a monopoly and we can be ripped off even more.

Lucy Butler: We have to be careful with these measures. They are welcome but we have to be careful of adverse consequences and the impact if we suddenly see big swathes of the market disappearing. That is a real concern. What is preventing some providers—the voluntary sector and local authorities—from entering into this area more? We need to be really understanding of that and really support those providers.

There are all sorts of issues: it takes a long time to register and set up a home, et cetera; all sorts of things are getting in the way. We need to be as focused on the blockers as we are on what is preventing people from entering this, what is stopping them, and what we can do to support the providers that we want to support our children.

Lord Faulkner of Worcester: Is there not a real danger that the children with the most complex needs will be right at the end of the queue when it comes to future investment and so on?

Rachael Wardell: No because they are currently costing the system so much per individual placement. Really well-invested funds into specialist placements would bring the overall costs down. That is why what we say about the RCCs and other measures is that they should focus on those young people first. From our point of view, if the resources that are currently in the system were redirected into securing and developing specialist placements, that would be a very worthwhile investment and they would be first in the queue for most DCSs.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: Can I ask a supplementary? I am just thinking about these profit caps and the difficulty of doing that but I can absolutely see the importance of it. I wonder if there could be pressure on at least some providers to invest more in the skills and professional development of their staff because another issue is that they are making a lot of profits. Even if there is no profit cap they could at least be investing in the quality of the staff they recruit, the way they train and develop them and all that. We need to build a whole ecosystem of qualified, talented, effective people in this field and it is not quite happening at the moment.

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: It would seem that the best ones will do that; the ones who are not so good will not because they are interested in the profit. A great thing about having the regional care co-operatives here is that we are able to make sure that we are investing long-term in highly skilled staff who are well trained and well supported. and it is a really good thing to be doing. Rubbish providers will remain rubbish providers.

Lord Barber of Chittlehampton: If the market were working, they would be exited from the market.

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: The problem is that the market does not work because we are desperate for placements. If you are a social worker and are trying to find somewhere to keep that child safe the money is irrelevant. You will just try to take it and persuade Rachael that she has to write the cheques. We are in a system where there are not enough places and too many children so there is an enormous pressure just to take something to keep a child safe.

Rachael Wardell: One professional development route for practitioners in children’s residential is into social work. Although that is obviously important professional development and to be welcomed because we need social workers, there is a lot to be said for staying in the residential sector. These are often low-paid workers but they are not low-skilled and those skills are not sufficiently valued in the pay scales in their settings.

In local authorities that are trying to grow their in-house workforce and provision you can definitely see that investment in workforce. Lucy alluded—in relation to the RCC—to something that one can do at scale on a regional basis, which is professional development of children’s residential workers. You are right: it is an issue.

Lucy Butler: The Government are reviewing the training and development of residential workers and that is to be welcomed because we do not think we currently get it right. There is also something for us to learn from European countries. The social pedagogy model, how much they invest in their residential workers and the value that those workers have is much higher than in this country and you can really see the quality of that support.

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: I left working in residential care because I got too many broken bones and eventually I thought, “Enough is enough”.

Baroness Hollins: I was interested in what you said about learning from European countries. I visited Dresden and saw the public services apprenticeship model for a five-year programme for school leavers, where residential support or support in the community was developed alongside other public service roles with equal salaries at the end of the five years. It was very impressive and they did not seem to have the same shortages of social care workers.

Lucy Butler: That is because it is valued and the training is exceptional.

Baroness Hollins: They trained alongside other public service workers.

Lucy Butler: These are the workers that are working with the most vulnerable children in our society and deserve some investment.

Q41 Baroness Redfern: I have to declare that I am the vice-president of the Local Government Association. Can we talk about skill mix within agency staffing?

Lucy Butler: Do you mean social workers or care workers?

Baroness Redfern: I am talking about care workers.

Rachael Wardell: They will not always be as highly skilled as your permanent workforce.

Q42 Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: You have slightly touched on this but we are very interested in trying to identify the main factors that contribute to the continued use of unregistered homes and whether you feel the safeguards are really structured properly in such situations. We have all read that somebody from Plymouth is sent to an unregistered home in, let us say, Blackpool. Why send them and how do you supervise what is happening during that period?

Rachael Wardell: I will speak to that. It is usually a choice of last resort. We would not send our children a great distance or use unregistered if we could avoid it. I have been a DCS for nearly 14 years now and there was probably a time when unregistered provision was cheaper and lawful, and there may have been places that used it for preference on cost. That is absolutely no longer true.

Obviously it is now unlawful to place in an unregistered but also the cost is very high indeed. Some highest-cost placements across the sector are unregistered placements and will have usually been specifically created in premises and with a staff that are designed to wrap around a young person who cannot be accommodated anywhere else. The choices that are being made are very different now from how they might have been a decade ago.

I cannot obviously speak for the percentage of unregistered use right across all local authorities but I can speak briefly from my own. There are about 960 children in Surrey’s care, which is a low per 10,000 rate because of course Surrey is so huge, of which one child is in unregulated in extremis and actually doing very well where they currently are.

Another challenge is that the read-across between regulated setting and high-quality provision that meets a child’s needs is not exact. Sometimes a child is doing better in an unregulated setting, even if we would not have chosen to place them there originally. Once they are settled and the right support is around them, moving them to a registered provision just because it is registered might not be the right choice.

Almost every DCS will have a story they can tell you of a time when they have had to move a child into registered because that is obviously the right thing to do but that ended up breaking down really quickly because the child had become stabilised in the other setting. What we tend to be motivated to do is support those providers to become registered, which is why the speed of registration is really important. You have to work with providers, get your setting registered and then we can maintain a registered provision around these children.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: If I just take the one child that you have talked about, as a purchaser can you put pressure on CQC to go and visit that place quickly? You are actually telling me that good quality care is being provided to that child. Something that we are challenged by is how long registration takes and we have had people who one believes are in it for the right reason but cannot get the registration.

Rachael Wardell: It will be Ofsted that visits, which is the registering body. What Ofsted will say—we see evidence of this—is that when it knows that a local authority is seeking the registration of a provider, it will try to prioritise it. It certainly tries to prioritise local authorities’ in-house settings to be registered for the same reasons. Again we will all have experienced occasions where we thought it was not as fast as it could be, so it is still an area of concern for us.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: That is very helpful to us because we do not know the answer but maybe there is something that could be done around that.

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: I have heard of people having to wait a year for Ofsted to come and register them, which is ludicrous. Maybe something we could or should be looking at is maximum times that Ofsted has to be able to do this piece of work and get a place registered.

The Chair: Is it a capacity issue within Ofsted?

Gerald Vernon-Jackson: Yes.

The Chair: Is it that they do not have enough people registering or is it—as we have heard—sometimes a problem, say, with the local authority getting planning permission for any changes to the property that is required in advance of registration? Where is the balance in this? Where is the real blockage in the queue?

Rachael Wardell: It is not just in one place, which is what makes it difficult. One point of influence recently has been the change in the regulations that meant that supported accommodation now needs to be registered as well. That led to a flood of applications into Ofsted in relation to supported accommodation. Some things that you have described are a problem. Sometimes it is not easy to get planning consents for settings. Sometimes it is a delay in getting a registered manager; for a setting to be registered the manager also has to be registered, so those things slow us down. Sometimes it just seems like things get stuck and we cannot find a good reason. It is a combination.

Rachael Wardell: That is correct. There has been a change to the rules in that, if there are two homes closely located to each other, one registered manager can cover two. If a registered manager moves completely to another setting, they would have to be re-registered in the new setting.

Baroness Watkins of Tavistock: Is that partly because there is no central register for registered managers? If you are a registered social worker you can go and work in another place easily, can you not?

Rachael Wardell: Their registration is recorded at Ofsted but the issue is it is not portable.

Baroness Redfern: Would portability help with this?

Rachael Wardell: Yes.

Q43 Lord Mohammed of Tinsley: I would like to just stay on the subject of the issues of delays of registration and the impact. Can I probe a bit further on how these delays in registration have impacted your sufficiency planning, whatever the reasons are?

If there is a delay in registration then they are not meeting their forecast sufficiency plans. To a certain extent, though, all local authorities rely on provision by other providers. Again, when we are looking at our sufficiency plans, we look at the extent to which providers are trying to develop their provision in our local area.

Lucy Butler: Some local authorities, particularly in the south-east, have developed fully staffed homes but they are not registered. That is a really big issue. That will be off-putting to people without big equity behind them, for instance. That is something for us to consider.

We are looking at both what we can do in terms of upskilling residential staff, putting influence on Ofsted and working with Ofsted to address this issue, but also looking at the sufficiency nationally and what we can do to make that more proportionate to where the children’s needs are. At the moment, it does not match children’s needs at all.
