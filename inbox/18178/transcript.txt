I want to ask about three things this morning: first, the disruption, secondly, the budgets, and thirdly, the political vision and delivery leadership. I am going to focus on Minister McDonald and Director General Jones. Minister, with the vision, the aim and the political side of this, there is no perfect structure, but can you update us on what the rationale was for getting rid of the Department that, in its creation, had sent a signal that we were putting science, innovation and technology at the heart of Government? In particular, how is the delivery governance run? You have two Ministers in Cabinet, a Secretary of State for DBIST and a focused AI Minister. Chris McDonald: Thank you very much, George. You are right: you have been through a few machinery of government changes yourself as well, and you would probably agree with me that the civil service is incredibly adept at following those instructions. Of course it comes with some cost, as any restructure does. It is very important to set out the rationale for it, as you say.

To my mind, in any sense—you will forgive me, I am an engineer and I come to it from an engineering perspective—form always follows function. You have to adapt the form of government to the function that you want it to carry out. We are fortunate in that the Prime Minister has given a very clear objective; in fact, he gave that objective very directly to me when he appointed me to the job. What he said to me was that he wanted to put science back in the service of industry. That is a really strong focus on the application and commercialisation of science. I know that this is something that you wrestled with when you were Science Minister too; we recognise that the UK punches well above its weight in terms of curiosity-driven research, basic science, but that much of that science is commercialised overseas.

Despite the fact that the Prime Minister gave me that objective, I was very clear in a speech that I gave early on to the Universities UK conference that that does not in any way diminish the work that we are doing on curiosity-driven research. That is quite clear, and the funding remains for that; it is protected. What we need to ensure is that those results bear fruit in the UK economy for the advantage of people in this country. Really, it is about ensuring that that end-to-end pipeline of commercialisation works. I have a bit of experience of this; this is the job that I used to do in industry. It is hard, it is very difficult to do, but we have the best advantage in the UK because we have such a strong fundamental science base.

That is the overriding objective. There are many things we need to do, but there are two things that it strikes me that we need to do to be successful at that. The first, as I have said, is to really strengthen the pipeline of scientific discovery, but we also need to expand the industrialisation capability. That downstream science activity happens in collaboration between Government and industry, and a lot of the activity happens in industry as well. We have seen a decline in industrial research floor space in the UK over a long period of time. Much of that is associated with the fact that we have had a decline in large industrial UK headquartered firms, so there is a structural aspect there. Government needs to help to commercialise the science, but also to intervene in such a way as to encourage firms to invest in industrialisation capacity. The support we have had for many Governments over many years around the Catapult network is an excellent example of that.
