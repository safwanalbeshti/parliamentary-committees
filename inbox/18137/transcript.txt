Q23 The Chair: Thank you. You are very warmly welcome today. The purpose of today’s session is to understand low-carbon molecular storage technologies, namely hydrogen, biomethane and gas storage with carbon capture and storage. I will start by asking each of you to give your perspective of molecular storage technologies and their potential to help mitigate the risk of intermittency during a Dunkelflaute event as the grid decarbonises. Who would like to start?

Kisha Couchman: I am happy to start. First, it is important to recognise that there is not a single solution to a Dunkelflaute. They will range in duration, so it is important for us to have a portfolio of flexible technologies to be able to respond to the system requirements. The UK is already developing a range of technologies. We have batteries, pump storage and interconnectors. We are developing consumer-led flexibility, which is how we shift consumption on the demand side. Ofgem has also just announced its cap and floor regime, which will bring forward investment for long-duration energy storage projects. That will be quite fundamental to how we deal with periods of low wind going forward.

However, in a prolonged period of low output, it is not just the number of gigawatts that you have installed on your system that will be important, but the number of terawatt hours or gigawatt hours that can sustain the system for a multi-day or multi-week period. That is where molecular storage is particularly useful. Today we have natural gas, which provides a significant source of stored chemical energy and it is also dispatchable generation. The challenge for us is to find the low-carbon alternatives to that source that we use today.

The Chair: Can you say what dispatchable generation is?

Kisha Couchman: Of course. Dispatchable generation is where you burn a fuel to generate electricity. Today that is predominantly natural gas and there are gas-fired generation power stations.

The Chair: It is immediate generation?

Kisha Couchman: Yes. Hydrogen, as you mentioned, is one of the options that we are looking at as an alternative to burning gas, because hydrogen can be produced during periods where there is excess or surplus renewable electricity. We have the benefit of a very large offshore wind fleet, so we have the opportunity to harness some of that renewable electricity to produce hydrogen. That can be stored in very large quantities and transported to a hydrogen-fired power station, which can then generate electricity. That is one option.

We have natural gas with CCS and that is where you attach carbon capture technology to capture carbon emissions. That is particularly useful because it has the benefit of lowering carbon emissions and it also has useful applications in the industrial sector.

The third option is biomethane, which offers a complementary role and potentially a more near-term option, because it is chemically very similar to natural gas. You can use the existing gas infrastructure without major modifications. The industry is quite confident that that can be scaled up in relatively short order, subject to gas quality checks and sustainable food stocks.

The thing to underscore is that it requires a range of different solutions. There will not be a silver bullet. The core objective is trying to build a resilient portfolio and ensuring that we have the market frameworks and the policy frameworks to bring forward the investment that is required to value each of the technologies that will have a different role in the system.

The Chair: Each technology has a role and it depends on incentives to bring those technologies to markets.

Kisha Couchman: Exactly.

The Chair: I will ask you a little bit about the sources of biomethane. You said that it has great potential. Of course it is methane like natural gas, fossil gas, so modification to the existing infrastructure will be minimal, but where does the biomethane come from?

Kisha Couchman: It tends to come from agricultural sources predominantly. I would be happy to follow up in writing with some more specific details on the sources of sustainable food stocks and biomethane.

The Chair: Is it land that we currently grow food on?

Kisha Couchman: Yes.

The Chair: I just wanted to be clear about biomethane, the scaled-up production of biomethane. Martin, you wanted to come in.

Martin Lambert: Yes. I can complement what Kisha has said. I fully agree on the portfolio approach, with a range of different options available, because there is no one single answer. That is definitely a key takeaway.

Molecular storage is essential for covering Dunkelflaute because the energy density of molecular storage is so much more. Batteries can do so much, but if you tried to cover a 10-day Dunkelflaute with batteries, you would need to cover a large chunk of the country with batteries to be able to do that. That is clearly not practical or sensible. Kisha has outlined the three types of molecular storage pretty well, but I will add a couple of points. We store a fair bit of natural gas today, but a key point for natural gas is that it flows as well as being stored. What I mean is that we get natural gas from Norway. We can import it as LNG, then we store some in the Rough gas field offshore of north-east England and we store gas in LNG terminals. There is a buffer, but the storage is just a buffer to smooth out the flows that we are bringing in from elsewhere.

Biomethane and hydrogen both need to be manufactured, so you have a different approach. You manufacture the product and then you can use it.

The Chair: You manufacture it and store it?

Martin Lambert: Indeed. To answer your question on biomethane, just to be clear for everyone, you start with stuff called biogas. On a farm, you effectively anaerobically digest biowaste and you produce a mix of methane and CO2 called biogas. To make biomethane, you separate the methane and the CO2 and that gives you grid-quality biomethane that you can then inject, with certain constraints, into the grid. You are right that there is a limitation on it, but various studies that we and others have done show that probably you could—currently we produce pretty small amounts of biomethane—increase it to about 10% of current natural gas consumption. That would still be sustainable because when you produce food you produce waste as well, but if you produce too much, you are right, you start to affect agricultural land and so there are constraints. We could do more with biomethane but there are limits on how far it can go.

To finish off, hydrogen has to be manufactured. There are two types of hydrogen, loosely called blue and green. You can make blue hydrogen by taking natural gas, using carbon capture and storage, and then you make hydrogen that can be stored. Green hydrogen is when you have renewable power, you can put it into a thing called an electrolyser and you can make hydrogen that way by breaking water down into hydrogen and oxygen. The problem with making hydrogen, storing it and making it back to electricity again is the round-trip efficiency. If you put in electricity, you make it into hydrogen, you store it, then make it back into electricity again, that is quite low round-trip efficiency. You can make it when you have very cheap electricity, surplus wind you do not need, but if you make too much of it, it becomes quite inefficient, hence the need for a portfolio approach. I will stop there and let you follow up.

The Chair: Can you explain when you make too much of it and it becomes inefficient?

Martin Lambert: Yes. On a very windy, sunny day in the future energy system, it is quite likely that the instantaneous wind and solar production will be more than the normal demand for electricity because we have built lots of wind farms, lots of solar farms and there will be more potential production than we need.

The Chair: There is free energy available for electrolysis.

Martin Lambert: At that point you can use the effectively free electricity for making electrolysis. A complex modelling question that is quite hard to answer is how many hours a year you will be in that position, and that depends on many factors. When you have free electricity to make use of the available wind power, making green hydrogen and storing it is a good thing to do, but then you have to add the cost of the electrolyser, which may not run for that many hours, and that affects the economics as well.

The Chair: The economics of electrolysers could be of interest. I would be very grateful if you could send us further information on that. Many thanks.

Brett Ryan: I will pick up on some of the points on hydrogen, but first I want to echo Kisha and Martin’s points. We need a portfolio approach to ensure resilience in the future energy system. When we talk about hydrogen’s role in the ability to mitigate the impacts of a Dunkelflaute, it needs several components linked together to make that happen. Of course the first is the storage element itself. When we are talking about the scale that we need to be delivering sustained power generation over the days, weeks and months that we are talking about for a Dunkelflaute scenario, you are really looking at—

The Chair: Months? I think we have heard that two weeks is the longest possible, or 11 days. It is the first time I have heard of Dunkelflaute events of months.

Brett Ryan: I was talking about the ability of the hydrogen storage system to be able to provide that power. Technically you can do it over weeks and months, whether or not the Dunkelflaute weather event itself persists.

The Chair: Sorry, I misunderstood you there.

Brett Ryan: We are looking at large-scale geological formations, depleted oil and gas fields, salt caverns and limed rock caverns. They are all technically feasible and some are already being used for hydrogen storage. Martin has pointed out that you need hydrogen production upstream of that to create those molecules in the first place. The UK has a twin-frac approach, as it is called at the moment, following electrolytic production and CCS-enabled production.

The Chair: Could you go through those again?

Brett Ryan: Yes, sorry. Electrolytic production is commonly called green hydrogen, which uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. There are a number of different pathways for CCS-enabled production, but effectively they are using a fossil fuel as a feedstock to split out the hydrogen and carbon. Once you have produced it, you then need to transport it to the geological formations, because they are in fixed locations. You cannot just put them where it is handy, so you will pipe the hydrogen to the storage facility.

When the Dunkelflaute conditions arrive and you have low renewable output, but still have high electricity demand in the system, you withdraw the hydrogen molecules and run them through a hydrogen-to-power unit—in the industry we shorten it to HTP—that can range in size and technology. You can have fuel cells, gas engines or combined heat and power units, all the way up to grid-scale combined-scale gas turbines. They can be from tens to hundreds of megawatts in generation capacity. Once you have all of those elements combined, that allows hydrogen in molecular storage and low-carbon dispatchable technology to help mitigate the Dunkelflaute.

Hydrogen has the unique advantage of solving a problem at both ends. You are taking the excess renewable generation from the clean power buildout and absorbing some of the excess generation capacity, converting it into hydrogen, storing it for when you need it later and bringing it back online when the renewable output is low but your electricity demand is still high.

The Chair: Excellent, thank you. Lord Ashcombe, your question.

Q24 Lord Ashcombe: Good morning. I think this leads on from what you were saying, Brett. Does the UK have sufficient hydrogen storage to meet the energy demand in a multi-day, multi-week Dunkelflaute, bearing in mind that we have information here that people seem to think the amounts are fairly different? Secondly, I believe that in a Dunkelflaute, very often we are not going to have the interconnectors producing what they will because it is highly likely it will happen in northern Europe at the same time.

Brett Ryan: There are various numbers in the public domain on the technical potential for hydrogen storage capacity within geological formations. There are all sorts of characteristics of the individual geological formations that will decide whether it is technically or economically feasible to develop a site into storage. The specific references will be in our written submission. A number of reports put the technical potential as 1,000 terawatt hours of storage capacity within those geological formations, but once you start looking at the site characteristics—access; whether or not you would get a planning consent on those sites; whether the formation has the technical integrity to store hydrogen in great volumes over long durations—of course that number comes down.

What the system needs is still up in the air at the moment. There are various models out there. The CCC’s seventh carbon budget says that the system requires in the order of nine terawatt hours of hydrogen storage by 2050. NESO’s future energy scenarios have a similar figure by 2050. There is a strong expectation that of the 1,000 kilowatt hours of potential storage we can feasibly deliver around 10 terawatt hours technically and commercially.

Lord Ashcombe: Thank you. I should have declared that I work for an insurance broker that does a lot of work with hydrogen.

So “What do we have today?” is my first question. The next is: how easy is it and is it realistically possible to repurpose natural gas storage for hydrogen? I know you can do it for biomethane.

Brett Ryan: We have very little today. Some small salt caverns have been used on Teesside since the 1970s. They have about 25 gigawatt hours of storage capacity, but one thing to note is that because they have been used to support the chemical industry, they have not been subject to the vast injection and withdrawal rates that we would expect for supporting the power sector. The cavern itself is proving its technical integrity and ability to store hydrogen over long durations, but it has not gone through the same injection and withdrawal rates that we would expect for future use.

That being said, the technology is being proven elsewhere at the moment. One of our members is supporting the ACES Delta project in America. They have two salt caverns storing hydrogen, each one with about 150 gigawatt hours of capacity, and they are using it to feed grid-scale gas turbines. They are blending the hydrogen with natural gas at about 30%. They are proving the technical ability to store hydrogen in salt caverns and withdraw it for the power sector. Sorry, forgive me, what was the second half of the question?

Lord Ashcombe: The repurposing.

Brett Ryan: Absolutely, it is technically feasible, and there are two aspects to it. One is converting a cavern, the subsurface itself. That is absolutely technically feasible. Similarly, you have to address the topside equipment. You will have compressors and gas-conditioning equipment, things like seals on your pipework. They are all very well-known and all technically possible. It comes down to the commercial frameworks to be able to do that economically.

Lord Ashcombe: Would we be able to use the Rough field? The biggest one we have is the Rough.

Brett Ryan: You are right. Rough, the offshore gas field, is bigger than most of the caverns that we are looking to use onshore. Centrica absolutely has a plan to be able to repurpose that for storing hydrogen, as long as it has the right investment signals and certainty that will be required. It is doing a lot of technical feasibility work to ensure that that is possible.

Martin Lambert: I can add a couple of points on that. It is important to distinguish between hydrogen storage in salt caverns and hydrogen storage in depleted hydrocarbon fields. Storage in salt caverns has been proven for years. ICI did it, when ICI still existed, in Teesside. Hydrogen storage in salt caverns is an absolutely proven technology and there is no issue with doing that. The other comment on salt caverns is that in the UK we are very blessed with lots of structures. We have several existing and we have a lot of potential for more salt caverns in the UK.

Lord Ashcombe: They would need developing?

Martin Lambert: Some of the current salt caverns have been used for hydrogen in the past. Many of them are being used for storing methane today, but they could be relatively easily converted back to storing hydrogen. I do not have a figure on how much they could store, but they could be converted.

Storing hydrogen in depleted hydrocarbon fields is a quite different proposition altogether. There is some testing being done in Austria. RAG Austria is doing some tests for that at the moment, but if you think about it, the issue is that you put the expensive hydrogen you have produced in a hydrocarbon field and you are not quite sure what will come back out again. From conversations with the people in Austria, they get back some hydrogen but it is mixed, so it needs to be treated when it comes out again. To be honest, given we are so blessed with salt structures in the UK, I think we should explore that fully first and give converting hydrocarbon fields a second order priority.

Lord Ashcombe: Bearing in mind we have heard about moving stored carbon from A to B, how easy is it to move the hydrogen from A to B? Do we have a network that does that to get it to the right places? We know that we have a problem with getting power around the grid at the moment.

Martin Lambert: You can move hydrogen in pipelines. That has been done for ages. There are existing hydrogen pipelines, fairly small scale, but hydrogen pipelines exist in the US and north-west Europe—Belgium, the Netherlands, northern Germany. The harder bit is you cannot automatically convert a natural gas pipeline to hydrogen. You can eventually convert it normally, but it depends on the metal used in making the pipeline, the seals and the valves, so it requires a fair bit of expenditure to upgrade a former gas pipeline to make it suitable for hydrogen. National Gas has Project Union, which is doing quite a lot of work on looking at what would be required to convert the pipelines for hydrogen. It could be done but it is not an overnight switch. You need to put in some investment to make the transition.

Brett Ryan: The Government have set up the developing hydrogen transport and storage business models with the intention that they will be rolled out together as a network. There was a commitment with the first regional hydrogen transport and storage network that the allocation would launch later this year and ideally would target commercial operations by 31 December 2033. That is due to the long lead times for such major infrastructure. There are plans to do that, but unfortunately the industry has seen a bit of a slowdown in the development of those business models and the launch of that first allocation. That is a key thing for our members, because we have members developing plans in various parts of the country with different options for what the first regional network could be, but it brings together the storage and the transportation by pipeline, which deserves to get a mention.

Kisha Couchman: I agree with what the others have said. One thing to add is that repurposing gas salt caverns for hydrogen, while technically possible, would be economically challenging and would need to be done case by case. Our members have suggested that it would probably make more sense to develop the salt caverns specifically for hydrogen rather than trying to repurpose. That is also because you need to plan whether you need the gas in the cavern for system resilience. If it is still useful in the system, that needs to be co-ordinated before we start to repurpose it for something else.

The other thing is just to reiterate the point about the entire hydrogen value chain. It is very difficult to develop storage without a clear view of having sufficient volumes of hydrogen production, and producers also need the confidence that there will be demand. That needs to be done in co-ordination with developing the hydrogen-fired power stations that will ultimately produce or use the hydrogen to generate electricity. There are lots of different things and it ends up in a sort of chicken-and-egg situation. The business models that Brett mentioned are key to this and are what is required for the development in order to allow that investment to come forward.

Lord Ashcombe: If I may, I have one final question. If we need to make new salt caverns, and the washing process is not a short process to open them up, how long is that going to take to get to a sufficient volume, whatever I mean by that?

Kisha Couchman: It takes roughly six to 10 years to develop a hydrogen salt cavern, so the lead times are very long and those investment decisions need to be made as soon as possible. We would suggest that we make those decisions ahead of need. There are obviously various useful indications about the range of hydrogen that we could have on the system. NESO’s future energy scenario pathway, which was published last year, suggests three terawatt hours by 2035.

Kisha Couchman: Three terawatt hours by 2035. The Climate Change Committee’s balanced pathway is three terawatt hours by 2040, so there is a slight difference in those two scenarios. If it takes 10 years to develop, we need to be making those decisions now.

Q25 Lord Jay of Ewelme: Thank you very much. I want to turn the attention back to gas, if I may. Kisha Couchman: I am happy to go first. DESNZ, the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, did a piece of analysis that was published earlier this year, which recognised that much of the fleet could potentially continue to remain in operation throughout the 2030s.
