Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Dr David Lloyd Owen, Hugh Goldsmith and John Abraham.

Chair: Good morning and welcome to this meeting of the Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee. This morning, we return to our ongoing inquiry into the reform of the water sector, and we shall be dealing with questions around ownership of companies within the sector. We have three panels, so we are going to try to keep a good pace. For the benefit of those who are following our proceedings and for our own official record, I invite our witnesses to introduce themselves to the Committee.

Hugh Goldsmith: Good morning, everybody. I am Hugh Goldsmith. I am currently visiting professor at the College of Europe in Bruges, where I teach infrastructure, economics and finance. Before that, I was head of the water and waste management division at the European Investment Bank.

John Abraham: Good morning. My name is John Abraham. I am the chief operating officer for industrial water and energy at Veolia, covering the UK and Nordics. I am a civil engineer and a fellow of the Institute of Civil Engineers and the Chartered Institute of Environmental Managers. I have spent nearly 40 years in water, waste water and energy, across design and build operations and maintenance. My responsibilities include several major PFI contracts for Scottish Water, Northern Ireland Water and the Ministry of Defence. I will say more about Veolia later on.

Dr Lloyd Owen: My name is David Lloyd Owen. I have been following the water sector since the late 1980s, first as an equity analyst, and for the last three decades as an independent consultant.

Q119 Chair: Thank you all for your attendance. David, you said your experience goes back to the 1980s. That is a timely prompt because that is where—back in the mists of time—I want to start: believe it or not, I was still an undergraduate in 1989. I want to ask a question of David and Hugh; John, feel free to chip in if you feel moved. What were the problems with the logic of privatisation as it was carried out in 1989? What questions were being posed at that point, if privatisation was the answer?

Dr Lloyd Owen: When you look at privatisation per se, it was well expressed quite recently by Roch Cheroux, the new head of Dŵr Cymru, or Welsh Water: it is not necessarily the management model that matters, it is the regulatory model. There has definitely been a clear and present failure in regulation for the last 15 years, arguably for the last 20 years.

Hugh Goldsmith: Your question was about what problem privatisation was going to solve. The White Paper in the mid-’80s was clear that the objective was to provide benefits to the consumer. The benefits that were going to flow from privatisation included more efficiency, more innovation, and access to private finance. It was also couched in terms not only of customers becoming shareholders but of employees becoming shareholders and therefore interested in benefiting from the profitability of the company. That clearly took place in the context of the wider privatisation process.

Q120 Chair: Some of this would be driven by outcomes for customers—efficiency savings and the rest of it—and some was a political project around share ownership, which was part of the political zeitgeist at the time. Let us park the politics; we can do that. From the perspective of outcomes for customers, the drive for efficiency from the private sector suggests that there were inefficiencies. Were these problems somehow an inherent part of being in the public sector?

Hugh Goldsmith: I am not aware of what the specific inefficiencies were. I worked with the water authorities prior to privatisation; I worked with the water companies and the regulator after privatisation. The first thing that happened with the new water companies was that they started to look at ways of using all their assets more efficiently. For instance, they looked at voluntary redundancies to reduce staff numbers; they were clearly driven by an efficiency agenda in those early years. I was not aware of specific issues around efficiency.

The other thing that is important to state is that they were borrowing from the Treasury or the Public Works Loans Board, and their finances counted on public sector borrowing. One of the big motivations was to take the investment off the public sector balance sheet.

Q121 Chair: John, you can bring in the private perspective.

John Abraham: In 1989 I started working in the UK water industry, and I remember fierce competition for funding at the time. Do we spend money on our railways, roads, public buildings, hospitals or water? Water got pushed to the end of the queue all the time and did not get funded. On the other hand, I remember going to what they used to call a treatment palace in the north-west. There was a lot of over-design, and no real focus on building efficient, fit-for-purpose solutions. So there was a big drive to get more efficiency in design and construction.

The other element was that the whole of Europe was thinking about urban wastewater treatment Acts and trying to clean up. Primary treatment was the main source of treatment at the time; there was no secondary treatment—that came in in the ’90s, and the UK were the first adopters. We were far ahead of the game in terms of secondary treatment; we built the next generation of treatment plants very early in the process. Privatisation of the industry allowed funding to be focused on three drivers: quality, growth and performance.

Q122 Chair: This is a familiar tension: everything is important until there is a crisis in the NHS, and that is where the money goes. David, you spoke earlier about regulatory failure. How much of what has happened subsequently is a consequence of regulatory failure as opposed to ownership?

Dr Lloyd Owen: It is an essential factor. The regulatory history can be broken down to events in two crucial phases. Under Ian Byatt from 1989 to 2000, and during his final AMP3 period in 2005, there was an emphasis on increasing investment in infrastructure and its maintenance first. Post that, the regulator moved to what some would tritely call a cheap and nasty approach, whereby cost to customer overruled everything.

Chair: Just keep the bills low.

Dr Lloyd Owen: That is right. In terms of operating spending and maintenance, it remained static for 25 years, even though £200 billion of new assets were brought in. Ultimately that meant a lot of new assets were simply not managed or operated: they were allowed to stagnate.

Q123 Chair: Some studies show that the growth in value among water companies has been mainly funded by debt, not equity. With the benefit of 40-year hindsight, to what extent does that tell us something about the logic behind the initial decision in the 1980s? This is an easy question for the academics.

Hugh Goldsmith: It is clear that all the investment in the water industry was being funded by debt because when they were privatised they were debt-free. All the debt was paid off.

Q124 Chair: The debt was taken on to the public books; in fact, they were given a bit of a sweetener, of £1.5 billion, were they not?

Hugh Goldsmith: They were given a £1.5 billion green dowry. The companies started life with working capital from the Government, and they were debt-free. No equity was paid into the companies; the purchase price from the people who bought shares went to the Government. It was a transaction about the future value of shares, not an investment in assets. The companies started life with a clean balance sheet; therefore it was always going to be more attractive to fund all their investment through debt. Debt funding is the standard way that infrastructure assets are funded all over Europe: you borrow to invest.

When the first regulatory review came out, it gave a regulated capital value that showed significant regulatory equity in the system; but that was created by the particular regulatory model—the indexed RCV—that was being used. There appeared to be equity in the companies even though equity had not been paid in for investment. Debt funding was always going to be the main game for financing actual physical investments.

Q125 Chair: This sounds like an accounting trick just to magic capital out of nowhere, or is that a misunderstanding?

Hugh Goldsmith: The RCV—regulatory capital value—model was developed in the Treasury by Ian Byatt; when he was working in the Treasury there was something called the Byatt report, which presented a way of making infrastructure assets attractive to private capital. One of the things it does is to take inflation risk away from investors—in effect, passing it on to consumers—because consumer bills go up by RPI every year, less any efficiency gains, and the assets inflate by RPI. If that is all debt funded, your equity value goes up over time, but cash is not being paid in to build real assets. If you were dealing with a greenfield project, you would do it on project finance: debt comes in, there is a slice of equity that comes in from the investors, and that is all capital that goes into building the project, taking on risk in delivering it, and the operations. In this case, that was not how the model worked.

Dr Lloyd Owen: At privatisation the credit rating agencies gave the sector two As, the second highest possible sovereign debt rating. Ofwat made clear in its next two periodic reviews that it was perfectly happy to have a triple B rating. One of the consequences was that the cost of capital—the coupon—rose, as did the debt; the more debt the water companies have taken on, the more expensive servicing that debt has been.

There is an interesting contrast between this and Scottish Water. Scottish Water has as much debt in terms of its regulated capital value as the sector in England and Wales: however, the interest it pays is materially lower and that has an impact on its costs.

Q126 Chair: It has an impact on its stability. Keeping the focus firmly on customer outcomes, how would you characterise the broad trends in England since 1989 in 30 seconds or less? I am thinking about pollution, leakages, consumption, investment and customer service in the round.

Hugh Goldsmith: I have been drinking water from Thames Water for around eight decades; the quality of drinking water has not changed dramatically. There has been an improvement from 99% to 99.3%. The big improvement has been in in waste water treatment, as John mentioned. The introduction of European environmental regulations has driven much of the investment in the sector: the long-term objective of the water framework directive is to improve the ecological quality of water bodies. The drivers are towards environmental quality. There are people who are suffering from sewer flooding in their basements, but those numbers have gone down. Ofwat keeps various performance measures, and there have been improvements. The big impact has been around the environmental improvement of waste water treatment, and that is where the big investment has gone.

Q127 Chair: You are saying that that was driven by the water framework directive.

Hugh Goldsmith: First, there was the urban waste water treatment directive, then the nitrates directive and others—there is a whole web of directives governing environmental activities. Since 2000—in Europe anyway—there is the water framework directive, and that got translated into the UK. The aim is to achieve the long-term benefit of making all rivers of good ecological quality for fish and for our grandchildren to swim in without the risk of something nasty happening to them.

Q128 Chair: We touched earlier on the grand vision of a share-owning democracy in the 1980s: there would be employee share owners and a wider public share ownership. Yet in 2026, very few water companies are even publicly listed. My final question on this for the moment is, what impact has that had, and is it where we should be looking for change?

Hugh Goldsmith: I would say that the water industry has evolved in many ways. There was the multi-utility phase, where United Utilities was created as a combined water and energy utility in the north-east. Welsh Water tried to do the same thing in its link with SWALEC; that got turned into Hyder, which got into financial difficulties. There were strategic investors such as Veolia, Suez, and FCC; RWE—the German multi-utility—was an owner for a while. The 1999 price review—where the share price dropped below the capital value—was a critical moment for the industry. Since then it has been financialised: in the latter period, the major investors have been financial investors seeking to extract cash from the business and increase leverage.

Q129 Chair: It was a watershed moment for the industry, in terms of the way that the focus shifted from customer service to financial management.

John Abraham: Ownership is only part of the story. During the first third of the privatisation story, a huge amount of capital was put in to build secondary treatment—real, fundamental, basic treatment—to clean up our rivers and improve our drinking water. We build things in the water industry really well; the CapEx delivery is very good value for money.

The problem now is how we are operating in maintaining the assets that we have spent the last 38 years building. The same people are operating the same assets the same way because there is an operational monopoly within a monopoly organisation. My company, Veolia, is the biggest water company in the world: it looks after water for 110 million people. If you get on the Eurostar and go to Paris, we are producing water for 4 million people there. Yet there is no market in the UK: I cannot deliver my world-class operational and maintenance services across England and Wales. I have some market in Scotland and Northern Ireland and the Ministry of Defence, but I have no market in England and Wales because it is operated and maintained by the same people the same way. That is where the problem is; not the ownership, which can be detached as a PFI is run.

Chair: We will call that a good-quality elevator pitch. We are going to move on to look at the experience elsewhere in the UK. Henry, you are going to lead the questioning.

Q130 Henry Tufnell: To crack on about Welsh Water, is it attractive to bondholders and can it attract debt at a competitive level?

Dr Lloyd Owen: It is a mixed story. Originally, we called it Gwaith Cymru, the Welsh way. The key element to Welsh Water—the Glas Cymru model—was that it was not for profit, therefore any surplus was either put into customer rebates or discretionary spending.

Unfortunately, the actual performance of Welsh Water—on all the publicly available metrics—has not been a great success. I would classify the 10 water PLCs as follows. Wessex and Northumbria are owned by international utilities; they generally outperform the rest of the sector. The following three—Severn Trent, United Utilities and Pennon—are still market listed and perform on average. Welsh Water is below average. Finally there are the four private equity companies; they are dramatically below average, especially in the case of Thames. That is key. One has to have no sentimentality about ownership models: private equity has no other purpose than asset stripping.

In the case of Welsh Water, there is no doubt that the finances have dramatically improved. When it was brought out of SWALEC, its debt was 93% of equity: it was a highly leveraged company. These days the debt is 53%, and it is the least leveraged of all the water companies, so that side has undoubtedly been a success.

We had an honest moment last week when the new chief executive of Welsh Water spoke to the Financial Times. He previously ran Sydney Water, so he has a lot of experience in a state-owned company run on private sector lines. He said that, unfortunately, he believes that the Glas Cymru model is not delivering any specific advantage and is entirely dependent on the regulatory environment.

Q131 Henry Tufnell: The gearing is below average, at 62.8%; the credit rating is going down, so it has gone from A minus to triple B, with debt servicing costs equating to 25% of bills. With that financial situation, how will it get the money to pay for infrastructure?

Dr Lloyd Owen: It is the best rated company in the sector. One of the things you need to bear in mind is that the headline figures for debt are the lowest ratings; its overall debt is A minus to A. Anglian is the only other company that has an A rating on its senior debt, but debt rating is clearly one of the biggest challenges for the sector overall.

There is a regulatory issue here: in the early 2000s, there was no ceiling on debt, which allowed Macquarie to gear Thames Water for up to 95% of its equity through various investment vehicles. That was a real issue for the private equity-owned companies. Now Ofwat says 65% should be the ceiling, and 55% gearing is desirable, so it is being brought down; but it is also evident that a lot of the gearing shot up after the 2008 financial crisis, when the cost of capital was historically low. We have now returned to normal levels, and the water sector is not equipped to finance those things without a profound impact on its profitability.

Hugh Goldsmith: Before the creation of Glas Cymru and the not-for-profit model, EIB was the biggest lender to Welsh Water, and subsequently it has been a major lender up until the UK left the EU and EIB lending became difficult to access. I checked online last night for information about the EIB’s lending—I want to make it clear that I retired from the EIB in 2021, so I am speaking on a personal basis. The figure I found online was £780 million; it has been lending amortising loans.

The difference in many countries is that people borrow and pay it back like a mortgage. They do not take on long-term bonds that rely on their ability to refinance. That is what has caused the big problem for Thames right now: it cannot pay its maturing loans. Welsh Water took on a significant debt from the EIB, and I noticed that Tideway tunnel also took on long-term amortising loans. Once you have lost access to that source of finance—it is still the biggest source of water sector finance in Europe and possibly the world—then your national infrastructure bank should potentially be stepping in.

In the Scandinavian countries, municipally owned companies largely run all the water services. They could not separately access the levels of finance they need, so they have set up a communal credit bank. Austria also has a credit bank. Public sector banks can channel the necessary funding for that investment. Welsh Water was doing that before and after it went into a not-for-profit model, but I do not know what the attitude of the National Wealth Fund is now towards financing the water sector.

Q132 Henry Tufnell: What about accountability within the governance structure? Glas Cymru as the holding company could have up to 200 unpaid members to hold the Welsh Water board to account; it has 47. Who are these people? It is not representative; there is no one from the Welsh Government, no elected individuals from consumer bodies, and no local authority involvement. We are owned by bondholders in Luxembourg and then run by these 47 people who answer to no one.

Dr Lloyd Owen: I was a member of Glas Cymru for 11 years. The selection is somewhat arbitrary: members are invited to apply, but as you say there is no actual representative mandate. The idea is to act as a proxy, a quasi-shareholder. They turn up twice a year to meetings and ask interesting questions, and that is as far as it goes. There is no other form of representation.

Q133 Henry Tufnell: In terms of interesting questions, leakage increased by 15% from 2019-20. The company has not met its target to reduce per capita consumption, and for three consecutive years it has been one of the worst performers in the sector for supply interruptions. Major missed targets remain. Presumably the interesting questions that are being asked do not pertain to performance because performance has been going south. Do you think that is a problem?

Dr Lloyd Owen: When I was first involved with Glas Cymru, I was an idealist: I thought it would be an exemplar for the rest of the sector. I am afraid that the reality has been a disappointment. There is a crucial issue here that does not apply to England: the 1999 devolution settlement meant that under Ofwat’s blessing, it reports directly to the Senedd, the Welsh Government. You cannot apply that model in England, because there is no regional devolution.

For example, Kelda—Yorkshire Water in those days—also wished to become a mutual; Ofwat overruled that because it did not have the devolutionary element. It eventually got swallowed up by private equity, which was not in anybody’s interest.

Hugh Goldsmith: After the 1999 price review, the regulator effectively cut bills, and that was what pushed the share value below the regulatory asset or capital value. Several companies went to Ofwat to effectively de-privatise themselves. There was the Kelda proposal; Anglian and Southern Water had proposals. Some wanted to become friendly societies. The idea was to move to a model where the assets were owned by the public sector; the water companies would then take on the staff and operate the assets, moving much closer to the French model. That was being proposed by the industry itself. Ofwat opposed that until the proposal from Welsh Water; the reason, of course, was that it was perceived as being part of the devolutionary settlement. That was what the Welsh people wanted, therefore there was a way that they could create a not-for-profit model in Wales.

Regarding the actual governance structure, I agree with you. I have worked for a company limited by guarantee: it is a strange governance structure. You wonder who it is accountable to.

Q134 Henry Tufnell: How do you think the Scottish model compares in terms of that accountability?

Hugh Goldsmith: I am not familiar with the exact governance model in Scotland. My understanding is that it is publicly owned—therefore there is public accountability—but I am not sure whether local as well as national Government are involved on the board.

Historically, the previous stage was in 1904, when the water sector was being taken out of private ownership and the Metropolitan Water Board was formed. There you had representatives from local government plus the Thames Conservators, people who were democratically accountable for the area where the services were provided.

In terms of that governance element, Scotland also has a different regulatory system, where the price settlement seems to be negotiated, rather than the confrontational version, where companies want this and Ofwat says that and ultimately the Competition and Markets Authority makes a judgment.

The big difference in Scotland is that it counts as public sector borrowing. If you are trying to ensure that, as the French say, l’eau doit payer l’eau—water should pay for itself—there is this principle of full cost recovery. If people are generally willing to pay for it, then you want a structure that it is not on the public sector balance sheet but can none the less be publicly owned. There are many examples of that.

Dr Lloyd Owen: Scottish Water started off as 11 entities in the 1990s, which merged into four. They became Scottish Water in 2002, whereas England and Wales were merged into their current state in 1974. So there has been quite a bit of catching up on the rationalisation of Scottish Water. It has adopted the Ofwat model, in that it has an external regulator, but with a different relationship: it is indeed state owned and state funded for debt, but the company is run as a quasi public-private entity—in other words, it is supposed to generate a profit and so on. When you look at the actual performance metrics, by its own admission it outperforms the English and Welsh companies on certain metrics but underperforms on others.

You have to bear in mind that Scotland has a completely different relationship with its water resources. It is a mixed picture: western Scotland has an embarrassment of riches, but eastern Scotland is as vulnerable as southern England when it comes to reservoirs and droughts. Scotland is not necessarily a paragon: it exhibits both the joys and the miseries of being in the public sector as compared with the private sector.

Q135 Henry Tufnell: Veolia deal with Welsh Water and Scottish Water. Our colleagues in the Scottish Liberal Democrats found overflow data for 12 sewer assets managed by PFI, nine of which are run by Veolia. There were 513 spills in 2023 across these assets. In terms of the workings of the public-private partnership model, how can that be? In Wales, Veolia has a contract with Welsh Water for the Five Fords waste water treatment works, and in Herefordshire—

John Abraham: I do not think we have any operations in Wales. We have Seafield, which is the catchment in the Almond Valley in Edinburgh, where there is a rigorous compliance regime. We face severe fines every time we breach our licence, but that has not happened because our standards are higher than those required by the regulators.

There are various spills arising from storms, which are fully licensed. Three or four weeks ago in Seafield, we had a once in 300 years storm: we dealt with it very well. We work well with Scottish Water all the time. We have a lot of data that proves that where you almost ringfence a catchment and you have a PFI like we have with Almond Valley, Seafield and Esk, AVSE, which I think you want me to talk about later on, the performance is strong, efficiency is high and there is value for money. We have invested a lot of money into that plant: it is the biggest waste water treatment plant in Scotland. We produce 108% of the energy that the plant requires, and it is the only one in the whole of the UK that is a net exporter of energy. We will hand it back to Scottish Water in less than three years’ time, so the concession is coming to an end. We are proud of the performance of Seafield, but I can take that data away and look at it.

Q136 Henry Tufnell: Our friends at the Liberal Democrats identified 165 spillage events in Seafield in 2023, and long-standing complaints from local residents about smells from the plant. Is that a success?

John Abraham: If you are familiar with the plant, it is near Portobello Beach, in a very built-up area; it is the largest treatment plant in Scotland, so there is not zero smell coming from the plant. At certain times when the wind is blowing in a certain direction, there can be a very mild odour, and there are local residents who are unhappy with that. It is a well-documented and understood issue around the Portobello and Leith area. But if you compare the performance of our PFIs to other major plants in England and Wales, such as Beckton and Crossness, there is a positive difference.

Q137 Henry Tufnell: Is this a blueprint that other parts of the UK should be looking at?

John Abraham: England and Wales are already working with Ofwat on what it calls direct procurement for customers, or DPC. They are looking at major investments similar to the Thames Tideway tunnel. For example, Anglian Water is looking at a PPP model for desalination plants to ringfence the supply of drinking water from the sea. It is looking for separate finances, and Ofwat is pushing forward the direct procurement model.

Q138 Chair: David, picking up on your point about the relationship between the company and the regulator in Scotland, you contrasted Scottish Water—which is regulated by the Government body SEPA—with more adversarial relationships in the rest of the country. That might be a way of saying that it is a bit cosy in Scotland. Relationships south of the border have produced a remarkable range of metrics against which performance is assessed. How does that compare to the situation in Scotland? Is it the same or is it different?

Dr Lloyd Owen: One of the things you need to appreciate about Scottish Water is that it has faced a catching-up exercise since 2002: you could argue that England and Wales had a 10 to 13-year headstart. In practical terms, the key element to date is that the regulator has been much more amenable to increased costs and spending than in the case of England and Wales. With the current price review period up to 2030, that has now levelled out. The cheap and nasty model in England and Wales has been superseded. I would argue that the relationship between the regulator and the company in Scotland has been an exemplar for England and Wales, in that it has been more consistent and less confrontational about spending; there has been an acknowledgment about the challenges it faced and the fact that it had to deal with them.

Hugh Goldsmith: The debate about regulation and the conclusions from the IWC review have raised an issue about what happened, and what went on, with regulation. One of the most interesting interventions was from Professor Stephen Littlechild—the author of the English model—who went on to be the electricity regulator. He made a submission to one of the regulatory hearings—I cannot remember exactly which one—where he said that the model had failed. I think he described it as purgatory, with the regulator trying to outguess the companies in a five-year cycle that then just starts again. Ultimately, when people do not agree, they go to the Competition and Markets Authority for a decision. He argued in favour of the Scottish model because it is closer to the negotiated outcome that you get in some American regulatory systems.

Chair: The third section of this tutorial will look at the ownership and management of water outside the UK. Terry, you are going to lead the questioning on that.

Q139 Terry Jermy: We have already touched on some examples of ownership models around the world, and the Committee has received a significant amount of evidence and examples of direct public ownership, delegated private management and something in between. What approaches do you feel might work best for England, and what are their advantages or disadvantages?

Dr Lloyd Owen: The Glas Cymru model can be used as a template. While acknowledging its shortcomings, there is a lot to be said in favour of bringing in a sense of stakeholder participation.

Terry Jermy: That is the governance point you were making with Henry.

Dr Lloyd Owen: That is right. The Prime Minister’s allusion the other day to increased state investment in the sector is a way forward. Looking at this retrospectively, things such as private equity simply should not be allowed. Companies should either be owned by a sympathetic utility, as is the case in Northumbria and in Wessex: their parent companies operate other utility companies and understand them. Welsh Water is flawed, but it is a way forward. And the listed model is still appropriate. The crucial thing is to eliminate private equity from the sector, alongside reinstating a sense of public participation.

I forget the exact year, but until the mid-1980s you had local council representatives on each of the water authorities. There used to be about 50 members on the board, and that was then cut down to 11; I think they were removed in 1984. That element of accountability is important: apart from anything else, it relates to the process of rebuilding trust in the companies. We have to appreciate that we have lost that trust.

My greatest analogy here is the drought of 1976 versus ’95. In the 1976 drought, people stoically accepted standpipes. In ’95, a friend of mine was working for Yorkshire Water when it mentioned that it might have to use standpipes. He went to a garage, took out his Yorkshire Water company credit card, and the person next to him spat in his face. I do not think people realise just how fundamental, how crucial, that element of public understanding—stoicism, fortitude, whatever you prefer to call it—is for a utility. When it was lost, it was damning.

Hugh Goldsmith: I absolutely second what David has said. What are the objectives of this change? Rebuilding public trust has to be a fundamental part. There are many ownership models out there. You still get listed companies that are publicly owned; Acea—the utility that supplies energy and water to Rome—is listed on the stock exchange, but it is 51% owned by the municipality of Rome. Having been through this learning experience in the UK, the challenge now is what we should do next. Public ownership, or something like it, is probably what the public is expecting. That seems to be the zeitgeist. The question is, how do we get there?

An example that no one has mentioned is Northern Ireland. I went there when it was looking for a large EIB loan to fund two projects, including Alpha, a project to build new drinking water plants to supply about half of the drinking water in the region. At the time, they were called PFI-type projects; they were both DBFOs. The company that delivered on Alpha—I think it was Kelda—did an amazing job. It found a way to redesign and rethink the way the project was going to be delivered, which cut the cost down from £156 million to £110 million. It was done as private finance: the EIB lent about 40% of the money, a Dutch bank lent another proportion and then there was some equity. The project was built, it worked, and the performance was outsourced to the private sector; at a certain point Kelda decided it wanted to withdraw from operating its non-core business, and it sold the equity to Northern Ireland Water. So it remains a private company—a project finance special purpose vehicle—but it is now owned by Northern Ireland Water, which has been able to achieve synergies through integrating it with other parts of its operations. So that is another way in which you can make that transition: the public sector can purchase equity.

We have to get away from the idea that there is a perfect model, or that, “This is what they do there, so it’s what we have to do here.” These things evolve over time. You can try to learn lessons and see what could be done better, but I absolutely mirror what both John and David have said: you cannot look at ownership in isolation. You have to look at ownership, governance, finance and affordability as a package; these things have outcomes, ultimately, in terms of consumer tariffs and consumer perception that their opinions have an influence on the performance of the companies.

Q140 Terry Jermy: As a Committee, our task is to tease out those different ownership models and their advantages or disadvantages; fortunately we do not have to make a decision. Within the models we have looked at, there are clear trends about how different models serve different purposes, whether that is being better for the environment or better for customers. Are there any particular trends that the Committee should be particularly aware of? For instance, which model is better if we wanted to get strong environmental improvements, or is it not as clear as that?

Hugh Goldsmith: There is no particular model that gives you particular outcomes. A lot of academic research has looked at public ownership versus private ownership; there are certainly cases where you have a badly performing public sector and you can get big improvements. Terry Jermy: Consistency is key.

Q142 Sarah Bool: Welcome, Marine. It is lovely to have you with us online. We want to look particularly at the French model. We know that France uses a leasing or concessionary model for many of its water resources. Can you explain how that works and set out some of the advantages and disadvantages of this approach?

Dr Colon: I will start by explaining the broad picture so that you can understand where the lease contracts fit in. First, the main difference and characterisation of the French model is that local governments are responsible for delivering the water and sewage services. They own the water systems and take all decisions regarding investment. They have to look for funds to invest according to their long-term planning, and they organise operation and maintenance, which means they decide whether to create their own public water operator or go for a lease contract. We call them concession contracts nowadays, according to the EU directive.

That contract is signed between the local government and a private operator. They agree on the duration of the contract and they agree on commitments that should be met to deal with the core issues and challenges that characterise this territory. This allows the private company to bill directly to the water users. With this money, it pays for its operation and maintenance costs. If there is a little investment that is included in the contract, it also uses this money to fund these investments. Most investments that are included in these contracts today are replacements of worn-out pumps, for instance—everything that is turning engines in treatment plants. There could also be bigger investments, which are less used today, but it is still a possibility; for instance, in Nîmes, close to Montpellier where we are, the city contracted with Veolia to add biogas to the generator in the waste water treatment plant. This is the kind of element that can be agreed on.

The French model has around 22,000 water and sewage utilities; it is a lot. More than half are operated by public authorities, and they deliver water to half the population. This means that, compared with English and Welsh standards, our utilities are very small. We have only a few utilities that look like a standard English utility, around Paris, obviously. I am talking about Eau de Paris, SEDIF, and SIAAP. The situation may look like the situation in England before the Water Act in 1973.

In France, 31% of water services are operated by private operators, but the regulation is very different from the one that you have talked about so far. We do not have a central regulator like Ofwat. The regulation is made when there is a private operator through the contracts. As mentioned earlier, if performance is not met, operators are fined. There are penalties included in the contract. There are some incentive mechanisms to make sure that the operators deliver what is expected during the duration of the contract. The state still has a role: to check that the drinking water quality and the effluents released in the environment meet the standards set by the state. An important thing to think about regarding the French system is the huge responsibility of the local governments.

Q143 Sarah Bool: In terms of advantages, there are so many smaller utilities, which means it is individualised and very personalised for the area. Do you see that as a benefit to the system, or do you think there should be more central regulation and larger organisations involved?

Dr Colon: There was an attempt at the end of the ’90s to bring in a central, big regulator. The project has now been abandoned. At that time, there was a crisis of mistrust due to a lack of transparency about the management of water utilities. This has been overcome by setting up a platform where water utilities have to share data about their systems and performance: main performance indicators.

The main drawback of our system is that, as you pointed out, all these small water systems lack capacity in terms of expertise and in terms of financial capacity to face the current challenges that we face with climate change. The state has been trying to merge these small services. There has been a drive and we have dramatically reduced the number of water and sewage utilities over the past 10 years. There is still a long way to go to reach a size where it becomes more comfortable to tackle the current issue we are facing.

Q144 Sarah Bool: Looking back at the contracts, you said that if you need to maintain a performance level, there will be fines. Do you think private sector involvement has generally improved service levels, efficiency and investment?

Dr Colon: Are you talking only about the private operators or the public operators as well?

Sarah Bool: I do not mind. You can address both if you want.

Dr Colon: In terms of performance, we are doing quite well. Among the thousands of water and sewage utilities, I am not saying we are meeting targets perfectly everywhere. There are some issues in some places. We have very good public operators and less good public operators. There is a great diversity of situations.

The main lessons that we can get from the French experience is, first, that we have to have a strong public sector where local authorities are able to really understand the main challenges that need to be addressed by the operators, set the right targets and so on. It is important to build this capacity within our local governments and authorities to improve and make sure that you can take advantage of a very good firm that is very experienced and can deliver. If you do not understand its work, or draft proper terms of reference, you will not get the best it can offer. The capacity of our local governments is key. Local authorities must be able to drive their operations.

In terms of investment, we have an issue of under-investment. We are quite worried at the moment in the sector because the pace of replacing old pipes is not good enough. We know we are going to have to handle a lot of investments to adapt to climate change. The way we fund investments only through water tariffs is seen today as leading to a situation where water tariffs are going to increase dramatically. The model really depends on the volume of water that is sold because, as was said earlier, only water can fund water in France, which means only water bills can fund investments in the water sector. Since we know that we are going to have to reduce the amount of water sold, we are in a tricky situation and we are looking for a new model to fund our water systems.

Q145 Sarah Bool: Excellent, that was about to be my next question, so you have already answered that. We are having a big debate and asking questions here in the UK about how to fund things, with that under-investment.

It has been mentioned that there are many cases where private contracts have been ended in France and the local authority has taken over control of the operations, such as Paris and Montpellier. What was the driver behind the taking back of those contracts?

Dr Colon: The root cause comes from the ’90s, when there was a big crisis of mistrust towards the model of concession contracts. Prices were going up very rapidly because there was a lot of investment to be made, as was said earlier, regarding sanitation to meet the standards set by the European Union; that was a great driver, and we could clean our rivers, but it is very costly. At the same time, utilities and local government relied on the private sector to bring money and expertise in the treatment and so on. At the same time, there has been a lack of transparency in the management and some cases of political corruption that you might have heard of. All this has created a lot of resentment regarding this model of involving the private operator. There has been a growing idea that we should move away from this model and trust more in the ability of the public sector to manage itself.

This is one of the roots of many decisions to go towards building a public ownership of the operator, but it is not the only reason. It is not only the moral idea that it is not right to make money from the water business; there is also something else. At a time when water issues are so sensitive, there is a will by many big cities to keep control of the whole water cycle and to not think about water services as a separate topic from urban planning and other local public services or issues, so that they could also use water and sewage services as a way to develop sound local development policy for water-resilient territories. That is also a very strong driver.

Q146 Sarah Bool: Just very practically, in terms of if a local authority such as Montpellier is taking back control, what steps need to be taken in reality? What has to be done?

Then it was about creating a small enterprise in Montpellier at that time. In Montpellier, a 25-year contract with Veolia was ending. The issue was to find the right people—the head of the Régie—to recruit the employees, build an organogram, fund offices, equipment and so on.

As I am sure you are aware, the issue of human resource management is key. In the case of France, it is interesting that we have a law that protects all employees of local public services. If there is a change, if a new operator takes over, whether public or private, the new operator must offer positions to the employees working in the utility so they can keep their jobs. This not only protects workers but also helps guarantee the continuity of service.

For instance, in the case of Montpellier, the head of the water production plant chose to follow the Régie and not work for Veolia any more. It was very nice for the Régie to rely on this high-level expertise to guarantee that once it takes over, it will have the expert from the water treatment production plant with it. That is something that is tricky.

Then at the end of the day, you get a Régie that looks like a small enterprise, with people coming from the ex-private operator and employees coming from other types of organisations. The challenge is to make all these people work together with common values.

In Montpellier and in other cases, it is a great opportunity to involve citizens. In Montpellier, citizens have been involved throughout the process leading to the decision to create a Régie and to choose the type of Régie that should be created. On the governance board of the Régie now, there are seats for the citizens.

This is what happened in Paris and Montpellier. In Montpellier, the billing system and the call centre were managed by Veolia for the first years of operations until the Régie felt confident enough and developed its own system.

Historically, because we came from a situation of having municipal water utilities, it means that for these private companies that provide water services, the shares are owned by either municipalities or provinces. The Water Supply Act also dictates that these companies have a security of supply that they must guarantee and have a duty of care for the infrastructure they own and use to provide services. They are private companies but owned by local government shareholders, so the idea is that they combine a degree of autonomy for the water utility to make decisions that are in the best interest of securing supply and maintaining the infrastructure, while having the shareholders be local government means that they are going to particularly focus on the level of services that are being provided to the consumers and on the tariffs not being too high.

On the one hand, the advantage of the model is that it creates a distance between the public shareholders and a water utility, which basically operates relatively autonomously. On the other hand, the disadvantage is that you have a model in which you have shareholders that are at arm’s length, and a utility that operates relatively autonomously. A lot depends on how the utility fills that role.

The challenges for the Netherlands will especially come in the near future. The challenges facing the sector with droughts, such as we had last year and for the previous four or five years, are highly problematic. Newly emerging contaminants are problematic. We are also at a stage where a lot of the infrastructure and investment from 50 years ago needs to be replaced. This is something that is just developing. The big question is how the model will fare under these pressures, because one of the things that is going to happen is that the tariffs are going to increase. In 2022, we paid €1.90 per cubic metre. This year it is up to €2.70 and it is likely to increase further in the future given the challenges we are facing.

Although we have 10 water utilities, we only have four water quality laboratories because most of these laboratories are co-owned by different utilities. In my view, it is that broader structure that makes a water utility provide an enabling environment and a situation in which it can perform well.

If I look at the cases that I have studied abroad, they often try to reform the utility, perhaps change its ownership, or make it into a private company owned by a public shareholder rather than a direct public utility. I am not so convinced that that is enough. You need to think more about the broader governance structure and the enabling environment in which that operates. Professor McGaughey: The empirical evidence is, as David Hall, has suggested. Let us also just take a step back and think about the economic logic. Water is a natural monopoly. It does not make sense to have competing pipes and sewers. It does not make sense to build two Victoria embankments, because it is going to cost too much. It is cheaper for one firm to provide everything. If something is a monopoly, then that means there cannot be competition to bring down prices and increase quality for the benefit of consumers. At privatisation, an economist called Stephen Littlechild said that what you could have is competition in the takeover market for water companies. Somehow, magically—he did not quite explain how—these benefits would be passed on to consumers. Of course, that just has not happened at all.

The numbers I quoted to you before were that we are the seventh worst in surface water quality—rivers and so on—fifth worst in bathing water quality, and we have the six highest bills. If you wanted to ignore the logic and just look at empirical stuff, we would have to look at lots and lots of countries, see how the law has changed over a significant amount of time, and then match that together with data or regression analysis. The answer is that you would probably find just what 90% of countries around the world have already found, which is that it is probably going to work better in public ownership because water is a natural monopoly.

David Henderson: It is interesting that we just heard the word, “probably.” Of course, it is impossible to prove a counterfactual given that we are talking about what might happen in the future. Surface water quality is not just affected by water companies. Unfortunately, we have a lot of contributors: farming, house building and run-off from highways actually—a lot of chemicals can come off tyres for example, which can cause a lot of pollution. It is not quite as easy to compare surface body water quality in one country with another. The population density matters hugely.

As I am sure this Committee knows all too well, the other thing about cost is that it sadly costs a lot more to build the equivalent piece of infrastructure in this country than in just about any other country in the world. We see that in aviation, transport, housing and energy, and the same is sadly true in water. With the cost of materials and the planning process, it takes years and years and years to build reservoirs. It is shameful that we have not built a single reservoir in more than 35 years.

That is not for want of trying. We have sought again and again to build reservoirs that have been rejected by the last Government and by regulators. Finally, once we get the approval to build these reservoirs so that we can cope with the pressures of climate change, then the games begin with the planning authorities or the environmental impact assessments. It takes so much longer and ends up costing a fortune more than it should.

There are complicated reasons why the average bill in England might be more than the average bill in France or Germany. It is interesting that, despite all those planning obstacles I just mentioned, we do not have the very highest bills actually. It is despite that actually. The companies are bending over backwards to try to be as efficient as possible. Why? Because it is in their owners’ interests. They have no interest in running inefficient operations. If anything, Ofwat has squeezed down the allowances provided to companies too much, which is why bills fell since 2010 for the following 14 years, down by a fifth according to real terms, which is why the maintenance of our assets is not anywhere near where it should be.

We are right to look at Scotland. Scotland’s regulator took a very different approach. It consistently enabled just-above inflation investments to maintain the quality of the assets. It actually looked at the assets and established what was needed, as opposed to Ofwat, which looked at these backward-looking models. That is not specific to having a privately or publicly owned company; that is about the quality of the regulation. Sir Jon Cunliffe’s report spends a lot of time looking at what good-quality regulation is. That is a crucial ingredient. As he found, it is true no matter what ownership model you have.

Professor Saal: Can I just do a hopefully quick follow-up on a related point? I agree on natural monopoly. These are natural monopolies.
