All the things that we are familiar with have gone, so people are most likely to be confused, to retract from the health system itself, and to date I can confidently say that we are still trying to find people who are lost from the healthcare system during that transition. We are yet to find out footing, especially because a lot of money had been invested in this holistic healthcare system that was supposed to offer distinct services to different populations and service all equally. Overnight, all that is gone and we are all expected to go through one service and system, which is not the easiest thing. I think one thing the Government did well was to react to that by trying to figure out how to ensure the continuation of services and to ensure that people are still accessing medicine, but the same breath, there are so many gaps and challenges, just to mention a few.

Just for context, about 84% of Zambia’s programme funding for HIV was coming from PEPFAR and almost 80% to 90% of the SRH funding was coming from other funders—UK and other European funders. With the reduction in funding, what we are seeing on the ground is that, while the facilities in the hospitals are continuing to work—the hospitals are still there—looking at the social and cultural barriers around reproductive health and HIV stigma and things like that, they need a lot of community interventions. Last year, the Government came up with an essential services package, and the community interventions have been removed. They have also removed standalone sexual health centres that used to exist. There were about 32 centres for HIV, and DREAMS centres in more than 21 districts that we were part of, and they no longer exist.

I did my master’s at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, and one of the things I remember is that I could go to a sexual health clinic there and access services, because in a clinic that is designed like that, there is no issue of stigma or discrimination. I don’t have to explain several times to a provider why I need something. We had a similar arrangement here with the different kinds of wellness centres, DREAMS centres, but we don’t have those any more. As we know from even insurance data, in any community, only about 15% of people are comfortable enough to go to a hospital, so we are losing a lot of people and a lot of prevention opportunities, and that has been a very big challenge.

Some of the other challenges we have seen is a loss of momentum in some of the work that we are doing. In the last few years before the funding cuts, Copper Rose was able to influence about seven policies in Government. We influenced the Family Planning 2030 commitments and we ensured that the Government had a specific commitment for adolescents’ access to reproductive health services. Last year, we were working on a gender strategy with the Ministry of Health, because they did have a strategy—there is a national gender strategy, but it has not been brought into direct ministries like the Ministry of Health. We were working on the USAID programme for gender, and from the time the stop work order happened, that gender strategy has just remained hanging. That is a missed opportunity, because there is no funder who is now willing to continue with that work. We have a draft, it is there, but it is just not going to go on because there is no support.

Lastly, we have seen also that there is a loss of staff and a lot of people who have lost their jobs following the funding cuts. UNAIDS estimated that about 16,000 people lost their jobs and only about 6,000 have managed to get their jobs back. That means that there are still many people who are not only unemployed but are underemployed because with a lot of these programmes, highly technical expertise is required—to interpret data, epidemiologists, statisticians—but a lot of those people are not working or they are going down to a job that just earns them an income. From where I am standing, that is one of the biggest challenges I see.

However, what works and what has been working so far is that those organisations whose models are embedded into the Government way of working have continued. However, in the immediate sense, in the first three months, what we saw, which was very scary and has raised a lot of trust issues, was to do with data management. A lot of data was sitting on USAID sites, and some of the health management information systems and many other systems were on US Government servers. As soon as the funding was cut, a lot of those servers have been lost, but also a lot of staff who sit even in the government facilities, like M and E, and data staff, have been lost and some of them have not come back.

This has also brought a lot of trust issues, because while we want and need to be self-sufficient as countries, we think that it has been very disturbing to be abruptly removed from a system. We think that good partnership lies in effective communication. A lot of trust has been lost, but I still think that there are a lot of opportunities going forward. But if I am being very honest, that is where we are. The biggest hit has been on the community. If I had to quantify the data—and research has been released. A study was published in 2025 in the Open Forum Infectious Diseases journal, and it was qualified by Ministry of Health staff. They estimated that in the first three months since the funding cuts, there were 32,000 new preventable deaths—for the first three months’ interruption. They said that if the disruption continued for a further four years, we should estimate 330,400 deaths.

These deaths are greater in children, because if you understand the reproductive health landscape, which is HIV most importantly, there is 1.3 million people living with HIV in Zambia right now. When you look at the UN targets, the UN has said 95-95-95, which means 95% of people should know their status, 95% of people should be on treatment, and 95% of those on treatment should be virally suppressed. As a country, we have achieved these targets, which goes to show that whatever these programmes were, they were working. However, these targets have not been met in children. From zero to 14, these targets have not been met. In children, 82% know their status, 88% are on treatment and 86% have suppression. What that means it that we have missed an opportunity either in preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV or in testing children who have already been delivered. When it comes to additional work that needs to be done, it will definitely have to be in children, and we should not overlook that.
