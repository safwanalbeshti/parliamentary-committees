Witnesses

I: Baroness Brown of Cambridge DBE FREng FRS, Chair of the Adaptation Committee, Climate Change Committee; Dr James Richardson, Director of Analysis and Chief Economist, Climate Change Committee; and Florence Bates, Head of Climate Change Risk Assessment and Adaptation Progress Reporting, Climate Change Committee.

Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Baroness Brown of Cambridge, Dr James Richardson and Florence Bates.

Chair: Welcome, everybody, to the Environmental Audit Committee for the first meeting of our new study of climate adaptation. We are very pleased to be joined by an excellent first panel to set the scene. Could each of you please introduce yourselves and the organisation you represent?

Dr Richardson: I am Dr James Richardson, chief analyst at the Climate Change Committee.

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I am Baroness Brown of Cambridge. I have chaired the adaptation committee of the Climate Change Committee for the past eight years.

Florence Bates: I am Florence Bates. I head up the climate change risk assessment and adaptation progress reporting team within the Climate Change Committee.

Chair: Thank you very much. We will probably direct most of the questions to you, Baroness Brown; if you think they are more appropriately answered elsewhere, we will leave you to make that decision. On general preparedness, how well prepared is the UK for the climate impacts that we are already experiencing and for those that we anticipate experiencing over the next 10 to 20 years?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I think we are all beginning to recognise that the UK was built for a climate that no longer exists. As a country, it has taken us a long time to come to that recognition. We have not been doing enough to change our environment. This summer has obviously brought that message home to us very clearly. We had five heatwaves, one of which lasted 37 days in England. We had 33 days in the summer where somewhere in the country was experiencing temperatures over 30°C.

The initial assessment of the number of heat-related deaths in May and June alone was that there were almost 3,000. That is normally the kind of high figure we would see for the whole of a very hot summer. Given that the heat went on for July and August, I would not be surprised if we see a number of excess heat-related deaths nearer to 5,000—now, that is my estimate, but we are going to see a very distressing number.

This is having a significant impact on our health. Indeed, July saw the highest number of attendances at A&E of any month in the history of the NHS. It is putting our health system under pressure, and at a time when the staff in some hospitals are struggling to work in very hot and uncomfortable conditions, and in very hot conditions at home where they may not be sleeping very well. It is putting everybody under pressure and our health is undoubtedly suffering.

Of course, it also delivered one of the most severe droughts we have seen. We had a huge proportion of England under a hosepipe ban, and many of us still are—not that I have been desperate to water the garden recently, but many of us still are.

That very hot and dry weather meant we had another record wildfire year with lots of very distressing incidents, again putting another of our emergency services under real pressure. Both last year and this year, we have seen some areas of the fire service saying that it was in critical and extreme conditions. We have seen disruptions to the rail system—we have seen problems with rail buckling and problems with subsidence. We have seen people advised not to travel and we have seen lots of delays to travel.

We have seen our education system disrupted, with more than 1,000 schools closed in June because the temperatures in schools were too hot for children to learn and for teachers to teach. We have seen insurance claims going up. The insurance industry reported that they have had the largest number of subsidence claims ever, and that the average cost of those claims has gone up from about £5,000 to something like £20,000. It is affecting our homes and our buildings in numerous ways.

This summer has made it absolutely clear to us that we are not ready for the climate that has already arrived. Our plans for the climate that is coming are not adequate. The climate we have now is the best we will get. Even if we get to net zero around mid-century, we are going to see temperatures rise around 2°C or so by then. We are absolutely not ready for that. It is going to be significantly worse. We have about 6 million properties at risk of flooding. The Environment Agency does great work, but we are going to see something like a 40% increase in the number of properties that flood by mid-century. The investment we are making today is not keeping up with the rate at which we are seeing the risk from climate increasing.

Chair: May I stop you for a moment? If you were to point to the biggest adaptation gaps that need to be filled, what would you cite as the key priorities for the Government?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: The one we have not spent anything like enough attention on in this country is heat: the huge effect that heat is having on people’s health and, indeed, how we function, recognising that 92% of homes in this country are predicted to overheat by mid-century. We had heartbreaking stories this summer of young mums in flats in high-rise buildings where the temperatures inside were getting up to 40°C and they had nowhere to go. They had to get down from a flat at the top of a big block and find somewhere to take their small children because it was too hot to stay at home. We are going to have a lot more of that unless we do something about it.

We have said that we need to see a significant increase in investment. There are estimates—not ours, because that is not something we do—in the literature that climate change is costing us up to about £60 billion per year. That may be an overestimate, but even if it is 50% of that, £30 billion a year is a very significant impact. We are saying we need to see investment of around £11 billion per annum between now and mid-century to start to address this—to tackle the biggest issues. That is probably split about 50% Government and 50% private sector. We have not been able to do a detailed analysis, but that is roughly what you might think of.

The biggest chunk is about dealing with heat, and a lot of that will involve dealing with our existing buildings, dealing with our existing hospitals, dealing with our schools, dealing with our prisons, and making sure there are public buildings, like public libraries, where that young mum can take her children when she cannot stay in her flat. Those sorts of things are big. We also need to make sure that our NHS can cope with the impact of heat on our health, the additional admissions and the additional impact on A&E.

Chair: I would like to focus on the issue of excess deaths. Your report estimated excess deaths from extreme heat numbering between 3,000 and 10,000 by 2050. As you say, we are not certain on the numbers this summer. I have seen a live estimate around 4,000, which I think came from the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine. You have suggested nearer to 5,000.

It feels like we talk about the numbers but do not pause for a second to reflect on what they mean. If we stop and look back over the last 30 or 40 years to a tragedy where 50 people died in a single incident, these are things that feel like seminal moments in our nation’s history. We are saying that upwards of 4,000 to 5,000 people are buried in the ground, and families have been through funerals that would not have happened but for climate change and our inability to keep them safe in these temperatures.

We have barely paused for a second to reflect on the deaths we have had as a result of climate change and as a result of heat. We think about the terrible incident in Nepal: we were all spellbound, but the numbers who died in that incident were probably equivalent to those who died in this very wealthy G7 nation because we are not adapted to the temperatures we now live in. I would ask you to reflect on whether we are, as a country, taking seriously the number of people who are dead today who did not need to be, because of the conditions we have been through. Have we taken that seriously enough?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I don’t think we are taking it seriously enough. I think one of the problems is cultural, in that we do not see ourselves as a hot country.

Chair: No.

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Certainly in England, we have to start seeing ourselves as a hot country in the summer. We have had a whole sequence of very hot summers in 2018, 2022, 2024, 2025, 2026. We have to start getting used to the fact that this is what it feels like now and it is going to get hotter.

It has been very useful to see the Met Office, for example, doing that weather forecast for 25 years’ time, and having a young weather presenter showing the weather map with temperatures in England reaching 40°C, to bring it home to us that we have to be prepared for these conditions. We have to know how to behave. We need better engagement with the public and better information, but we also need to make sure we have buildings, communities and planning that account for higher temperatures, and the right places for people to go.

Chair: Yes. I invite you to reflect on that cultural question, because we all saw a politician in this place saying, “Oh, it’s just nice weather. Why don’t people just get a t-shirt on and go out and enjoy it?” I am sure they spoke for many other people who said, “Well, in this country we are used to moaning when it rains. Now that we have some nice weather, we should just enjoy it.” When you talk about the impacts on people’s health and on our very lives, before we even start on the economic consequences or anything else, that kind of attitude feels pretty irresponsible, doesn’t it?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I find it quite worrying. I spent a lot of time this summer reminding people that you should not call this “good weather”. Indeed, I think on one weather forecast the presenters said, “It’s going to be another lovely day,” and I thought, “No, you shouldn’t say that. For some people, it isn’t going to be.”

Adrian Ramsay: Thank you, Baroness Brown, and our other panellists for being with us. You have set out very clearly the grave impacts we are now seeing in terms of the new climate reality. On the action that needs to be taken to protect our citizens, you set out your latest progress report and the recommendations that you want to see. Where have the Government acted on those recommendations and where has their response been insufficient?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Let me separate out the two relatively recent reports. Our last progress report was in 2025, and we are currently working on our next progress report—on where we are now—which will be published in spring next year. In our April 2025 progress report, we found there were no adaptation outcomes, across the 46 outcomes we looked at, where we could assess that the delivery was good, so we were making good progress. We did see improvements in policies and plans, and that has been a consistent trend for the last few years—planning is getting better—but we are not seeing the consequences of those plans. We are not seeing yet that things are making a difference and we are seeing an improvement. As I say, we are in the process of repeating that analysis, so I cannot comment on it. I am sure there will have been some progress.

In May this year we published our advice on the fourth climate change risk assessment, which was called “A Well-Adapted UK”. We have seen some progress in response to that. The most positive thing is that we have seen the Government commit that we will plan for 2° of warming by 2050 and that Government Departments have been told they must set measurable objectives—which I might call targets—for 2050 so that we can monitor progress towards them. I hope that will also mean they can look at how much it is going to cost to get there and get that planning into the spending review, so that they will start to be able to put the funding in place to make the changes we need to see.

We have also recently seen an increased focus on wildfires. We were very pleased to see the Prime Minister having a wildfire roundtable with all the key people, and one of our committee members was able to attend that. We have seen significantly more ministerial interest and recognition and comments, so some positive signs. We absolutely have to see concrete targets and plans in NAP4, and an indication that there is funding to support them, because the changes are not going to happen unless there is funding as well as policies to support them.

Adrian Ramsay: So there have been some signs of greater ministerial interest, but the question is what that delivers in practice in terms of funding and actions. Your latest progress report will be of interest to this Committee and others in setting that out. You mentioned NAP4, the fourth national adaptation programme. The Climate Change Committee’s assessment of NAP3 was that it fell short of what was needed to prepare the country for the impacts of climate change, and of course the needs have only got greater since then, as you have outlined. The Government have decided to wait until 2028 to publish NAP4. What is your view on that? Should it be brought forward? What needs to happen in the meantime?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Well, 2028 is when NAP4 would be published in the normal cycle, as laid out in the Climate Change Act. There has not been a decision to delay it. We are expecting the Government’s climate change risk assessment, which in the normal cycle will be published early next year, and then the NAP will follow after that. I would not say that was a decision to delay it in any way.

What I would say is that the critical thing, now that Government Departments have been told to set targets and measurable objectives in line with 2° by 2050, is that they get on and do that. They do not have to await the NAP to do that. They should be doing that now. Since the spending review comes before the NAP, it would be very good if they did them in time to get bids in for the spending review, to start making progress in some of these areas.

Adrian Ramsay: The Climate Change Committee has previously said that the status of NAP remains too low across Government. You have said that there are some signs of greater ministerial interest, but in terms of the extent of this challenge, which you have laid out very clearly, and the very real risks we are seeing now, let alone where they are going to get to, what do you think is needed to drive the level of focus on climate and nature security and climate resilience up the Government agenda in a very cross-Government way? For example, there has been increasing talk about whether there needs to be a Cabinet-level Minister, or whether there should be a resilience and adaptation commissioner, a bit like the Children’s Commissioner. What mechanism do you think would help to drive the cross-Government nature of work that is needed and drive it up the agenda in the way we need to see?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: First, seeing Government Departments having measurable objectives is a big step in the right direction. We have not seen those yet, but if they are good, that will be a big step in the right direction. But you are right that it needs strong cross-Government co-ordination. Making it a big part of the Government’s 10-year plan, with mechanisms operating across Government to oversee that, could be a way of achieving that. We did once have a Cabinet sub-committee for climate change—a ministerial-level committee for co-ordinating climate change activity in both mitigation and adaptation. You could use a mechanism like that. There could be a stronger role for the Cabinet Office, which already has a role in terms of looking at national risk.

There is a range of mechanisms that the Government could use. It would be good to see some focus on looking at choosing one of those to strengthen and co-ordinate, because it is very hard for DEFRA when so many of the requirements fall outside of its remit, particularly heat, for example. Heat falls under MHCLG, under the Department of Health and Social Care, under schools and education. Quite a small chunk of heat falls under what DEFRA needs to do. We need much stronger cross-Government co-ordination and a range of mechanisms could be used for that.

Julia Buckley: On cross-departmental working and accountability, you said in your report that a lack of ownership of adaptation across Government is limiting delivery. Who do you feel should be accountable for ensuring that we drive this forward?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I think every Secretary of State should be accountable. It has been positive to hear that the Secretary of State for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs has adaptation as one of DEFRA’s departmental priorities. It is quite shocking that it wasn’t one of DEFRA’s high-level departmental priorities, but it is encouraging that it is now. I absolutely think that Secretaries of State in every Department need to know what their Department’s measurable objectives and targets are and make sure they are being delivered.

Julia Buckley: Just to push back ever so gently, if it is the responsibility of everybody, who is going to lead the charge, as it were?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I think our Prime Minister has to do so, and he has shown that he is prepared to do that on wildfires. It would be good to see him recognising the impacts. This is absolutely one of the major challenges the country faces in so many ways, with potential impacts on security as well.

Julia Buckley: In your report you go into detail about whether DEFRA has significant clout among colleagues, and you compare it with the Treasury. We heard evidence on the three different climate change challenges. The Treasury seems to have picked up on the net zero challenge and seems to be quite powerful on that, but much less so when it comes to adaptation and climate change. Do you have a view about the role of the Treasury vis-à-vis the role of DEFRA?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: The Treasury clearly has an important role. I have to say that of all the Government Departments we have been trying to get to, the Treasury has been the slowest in responding to us, but I think we now have a meeting in the diary that has been arranged to talk to the Treasury. That has been disappointing.

Julia Buckley: That is very interesting. What about the roles of the devolved nations? How are they involved and how could we strengthen that further?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: It would be good to see greater co-ordination between England and the devolved nations, because so much of this of course goes across boundaries. Nature does not stop at boundaries; rivers don’t stop at boundaries; floods don’t stop at boundaries. As you will hear from the fire chief, fires don’t stop at boundaries. It would be good to see more consistent and shared adaptation, objectives and outcomes across the devolved nations.

It would be very helpful if we had consistent data and reporting, because they all tend to report different types of data in slightly different ways with different definitions. We definitely need that better co-ordination to manage the risks that span national boundaries. I think you will hear detail about fires from the horse’s mouth in the next session. That is key.

There is also the challenge for the devolved nations that the nature and environment part of this is devolved for them; of course, the national infrastructure bits like the electricity system and things are not. Quite often these things interact with each other. They are working slightly with one hand behind their back in terms of putting together their individual plans.

Julia Buckley: Thank you; that is very helpful, particularly the recommendation about consistent datasets from the different nations. Who should they submit that to? Should that be to the Climate Change Committee? Through what forum would you recommend to this Committee that that be organised?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: We were asked by each of the devolved nations to review their equivalents of the NAP. The NAP is for England, not for Britain. There is the SNAP for Scotland. They all have slightly different names. We report for each of the nations on that, because they all have slightly different ways of expressing their objectives and outcomes and a different focus. The different focus may be appropriate, because of course weather and climate impacts are different, depending on where you are. We have tried to frame our recommendations in “A Well-Adapted UK” to make them relevant to all of the four nations, but I have to say that they fit better for the England picture than they do for the others.

Julia Buckley: That is very helpful. Thank you.

Dr Roz Savage: Baroness Brown, you have already touched on some aspects, and you have described the challenge. Most Government Departments are aware that they need to get involved in this, and you mentioned that the Treasury may have been laggards in coming to the table. Are you able to name which Departments are engaging with you and which have perhaps been less forthcoming when you have offered to meet?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Yes. I will start by saying that it is a slightly interesting challenge, because shortly after we published “A Well-Adapted UK” we had a change of Prime Minister, and quite a significant change of Ministers, so there is a before-and-after kind of picture. When we published “A Well-Adapted UK” we met with Ministers from DEFRA, DESNZ, MOD and MHCLG, and our offer of meetings with Secretaries of State and Ministers was not taken up by the Department of Health and Social Care, the Department for Education, the Ministry of Justice and the Treasury.

However, subsequent to the change in Prime Minister, we have now met with senior civil servants in Health, Justice and Education. We have had some very strong statements from the Health Secretary, for example, and talk of the fact that they recognise that they now need a summer heat plan as well as a winter pressure plan, so we are seeing some positive statements. As I have said, we are only just about to catch up with the Treasury. We now have a meeting with the Treasury Exchequer Secretary in a couple of weeks’ time, I think.

Dr Roz Savage: Are you saying that there are no Departments still remaining that you want to engage with but have not yet agreed to meet?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: We have not yet met with the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade, the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport, the Department for Transport and the FCDO. Some of those I think are quite significant—for example, the Department for Transport.

Dr Roz Savage: Indeed. Thank you very much.

Chair: To stick on that for a moment, we know that, as you said at the start, there is particularly huge vulnerability in our hospitals and care homes. We have also heard about the impact on children’s learning in schools. Children doing exams in heat of more than 20° had something like a 20% greater chance of failing to succeed than if they had done them in cooler temperatures. It is welcome to hear that you have had senior civil servant meetings, but do you still think it would be wise to have ministerial interest from the Health Department and the Department for Education, given the need for leadership in those areas and the very serious considerations?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Absolutely. We would very much like to see ministerial engagement and recognition by Ministers in those Departments that their Departments are supposed to be setting measurable objectives. We recognise that that is quite a challenge. That is not an easy thing to do, and Departments do not necessarily have all the skills they will need to do that. We would like to see Ministers and Secretaries of State recognising the importance of their Departments managing to do that, and also recognising the importance of the implications for their spending review bids.

John Whitby: What are the priority indicators that the Government should be focusing on as they amend their approach to measuring climate adaptation progress?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Flo, would you like to pick up here?

Florence Bates: Yes, thank you. At the Climate Change Committee, we like to look at measures of adaptation outcomes and also adaptation outputs. In our regular progress reporting, we find there is a real and large number of critical data gaps—including instances of overheating in schools and also in key facilities such as in social care—where we do not have enough evidence or data on the uptake of key adaptation solutions, including cooling.

We are also lacking data on the impact of climate change and adverse weather on some of our key infrastructure systems, including in telecoms, for example, as well as the impact of climate change on domestic food production and food prices. There is also a real dearth of data when it comes to the impact of climate change on our natural environment, which is key. There are some key critical data gaps. We also find that the indicators are often not updated regularly enough on a national scale.

John Whitby: Is there anything we can learn from the approach of other countries?

Florence Bates: We think it is key for the fourth national adaptation programme to be accompanied by a monitoring evaluation framework that allows us to track progress against the measurable objectives that Baroness Brown was talking about, and report progress annually in a published report that the public can see. That is something the Scottish Government have been doing alongside the Scottish national adaptation plans. That is a key source to learn from.

John Whitby: Baroness Brown, you mentioned the 2° by 2050 assumption; I am wondering how comfortable we are with that. Given that global temperatures increased by 0.4° in the last decade, and by 0.7° in the last 20 years, we have probably reached 1.5° now. If we are making the assumption of 2050 for 2°, are we comfortable we will not get there before that?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: The best advice we have from our colleagues in the Met Office and other areas is that that is an appropriate target. It is better to start planning for that, particularly because we need to be planning for the sort of extremes we are going to be seeing. It is better to start planning for that and get moving now. We will keep that under review as time goes on and more data becomes available.

Yes, we need to recognise that we absolutely need to be planning for a minimum of 2°. We are slightly disappointed that the Government did not accept our recommendation, not that we needed to be fully planning for the temperatures at the end of the century, but that we should expect there to be 4° of warming on the risk register for the end of the century, to bring home what happens if we do not get on with mitigating and reducing emissions. We should be thinking of long-lasting infrastructure like the new electricity system we are putting in place, and the new reservoirs and water system we will need to build. We should be thinking, as we maintain that through its life, about how we would be able to cope with even significantly higher temperatures by the end of the century.

Chris Hinchliff: I am going to push back slightly, Baroness Brown, on your answer to my colleague about whether 2° of warming is a reasonable base for making our adaptation assumptions. Existing fossil fuel extraction projects around the world will potentially emit seven times as much carbon emissions as the planet can take to keep us to 1.5°. The UN’s latest estimate is that we are heading for 2.8°. Do you think our country has an understanding of what adaptation, particularly in relation to our critical infrastructure, looks like if we hit 2.8°?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Of course, 2.8° is an end-of-century figure. We suggested to the Government that we should look at a significantly higher figure for the end of the century, as you cannot yet rule that out. You are right that we need a wake-up call on standards. I understand that automotive design standards are typically for a maximum temperature of 50°C; well, a car out in the sun on a very hot day this summer could have temperatures close to that.

We have seen challenges with the temperature standards for the design of electronics. When the data centres supporting Guy’s and St Thomas’ failed in the summer of 2022 and we lost all those patient appointments, it was a huge challenge for the health service, but also for people who needed critical appointments for tests and things. I do think that a whole range of industries need to look at the standards they have worked to, in many cases, for the past 20 or 30 years and recognise that the climate has moved, but they have not moved. That is very important.

The other issue is that we need to learn to revise standards much more quickly than we do at the moment, because it is no good if it takes 10 years to agree to change the standard. We need to make sure that our industries are working to standards that align with the climate of the future.

Chris Hinchliff: Yes. On critical infrastructure, you mentioned that you have yet to meet with the DFT, which has yet to accept your offer of a meeting. Is that right?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: Yes.

Chris Hinchliff: We already have a repairs and maintenance backlog on our local roads that will cost more than £18 billion. I think there is a further roughly £6 billion-worth of repairs needed to bridges across the country. If I think about one of the critical railway lines, my constituency depends on the Fen line railway, which is sat on top of peat that is drying out and shrinking. That is a physical problem that we cannot get around. Do you think the Department for Transport understands the scale of the risk our infrastructure faces and what adapting to that looks like? Crucially, does the DFT have an adequate price tag in mind for what it needs to do?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I will speak up for the Department for Transport, in that it is one of the Departments with an adaptation plan. In general, in our assessments, the planning for climate change in the transport system is quite good. But you raised the challenge: that doesn’t mean that has been translated into what they are putting in for the spending review and whether the funding is going to be there to make the changes that are needed.

Chris Hinchliff: There is a separate funding point, but to be clear, you are reasonably confident that the DFT has an adequate plan for the Fen line railway if the peatland continues to dry out and the whole thing shrinks and collapses?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: I cannot comment on that level of detail. We have a very small team in the adaptation committee and we do not get into the detail of every bit of railway line, but we do look in detail at the quality of the plans and then at the quality of what we can tell about the implementation. We would say that the planning in the last assessment was good—I think that is true to say—but the implementation was not good. We have seen that with the challenges, yes. I am afraid I cannot comment on the individual bits of railway line, but I sympathise with your problem.

Chris Hinchliff: I meant it to be indicative, but I take your point. My next question is about the Department of Health and Social Care. The Government have told us that they are supporting the NHS with £6.7 billion over nine years to target the most critical estate risks, including cooling, to prepare for the extreme heat you have mentioned. Do you think that stacks up, relative to the magnitude of the problem our NHS is facing?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: The challenge we have is that the amount that has been announced, which might include air conditioning and cooling, is down at about £30 million or something. I have a figure somewhere—it is £32 million. There is a list of other things that that might cover as well, so that does not sound like an adequate sum of money. We have heard some very large numbers for the estates overall, but given the condition of many of our hospitals, that is not going to be just focused on cooling and ventilation. We do not have the detail of how that funding is going to be spent, so it is very hard to say. When you get down to the figure of £32 million that was identified for cooling, ventilation and some other things, that does not look like an adequate sum of money.

Manuela Perteghella: I want to talk a little bit about climate finance. The responsibility for identifying and managing climate risks is distributed across Departments, but the Treasury oversees public expenditure. The Climate Change Committee report estimates that a total additional investment of around £11 billion per year, shared between public and private sectors, is required for us to be well adapted. Does the UK currently invest enough in climate adaptation relative to the scale of the risks it faces?

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: We certainly do not think we are investing enough at the moment, but I am going to hand to James to pick up the detail on that for you.

Dr Richardson: It is worth saying that the £11 billion is the total of how much we think needs to be spent per year—it is not additional. Some money is being spent towards that. Unfortunately, we are not able to identify with any certainty how much, particularly because the Government does not set that out.

Part of the answer is that we need better data on what is being spent for climate adaptation already. A bit like the point we just had on the health service, there is a budget for maintaining the estate, but how much of that is going into climate adaptation in the health estate? We need better data on that. Undoubtedly, though, it will not be enough. There will be particular areas where, because we have not caught up with the need, we will be spending far too little, particularly in areas around cooling.

We already know that although there is a flood budget, activity is taking place and we have been doing it for a long time, that budget is not at the scale needed. Similarly, there are activities happening to build new reservoirs and so on, but again it is not happening at the scale needed. I think we can be confident that although there is some money being spent towards that £11 billion, it will not be enough.

Manuela Perteghella: You talk about flooding and cooling, and of course we are having very hot summers, which result in fires and so on. What kind of areas should the Government prioritise in their climate adaptation expenditure?

Dr Richardson: We think cooling is the largest area of that £11 billion. It is about 35%, so that is just shy of £4 billion that needs to be spent on that. That is the single largest part of the spend, and I think it is also going to be the area where the least activity is currently taking place. The other big areas are floods, water—which is mostly in the private sector in England, although not in the other nations—and transport.

It is worth saying that some things that are very important are not necessarily very expensive, or they are not public expenditure. The Flood Re scheme, which enables insurance for properties that are at risk of flooding, is due to end in 2039. That is not very far away if I am thinking about buying a property that relies on Flood Re to get my insurance. But that isn’t public expenditure. There are things that are important that also don’t necessarily score into that expenditure, but which we would want to be seen as priorities for action.

Julia Buckley: Are there any significant gaps in adaptation funding? How should the Government prioritise investment anywhere else?

Dr Richardson: There are certainly areas where we lack an understanding of the quantum needed. That £11 billion figure covers the areas where we could identify the spending need. We think those are the biggest areas. For example, we do not have a clear estimate on the energy system or the telecom system. We do not have a very good estimate on local roads, although we have pretty good data on national roads and transport.

There are definitely areas where we should prioritise understanding it better. That will no doubt lead to some need for expenditure. Again, some of those are in the private sector—things like food security. It is very hard to understand what needs to be spent through these very complicated supply chains. That will not be a matter for the Government, but it is important that the Government understand whether that activity is taking place and whether resources are going into it.

Manuela Perteghella: Regarding food security, do you think we should be thinking about climate breakdown, resilience and adaptation? Do you think we should have Nightingale-style places around the country where the Government are able to store food for those who cannot stockpile, in case of a breakdown?

Dr Richardson: Yes. We have said that is something we think the Government need to look at. Because the supply chain issues are so complex, we have not been able to reach a definitive view on that and on the merits of, as it were, national stockpiling versus private stockpiling. Different countries adopt different approaches. We do think it is a very serious issue that the Government need to understand better. They need to understand what could go wrong and where the system could break, and they need to assess whether they are going to have national stockpiling approaches, as a number of other countries do, and make conscious decisions about the best ways of addressing that.

Baroness Brown of Cambridge: We think they also need a better understanding of the potential impact of climate change on food price inflation for the public, who are already very stressed in terms of the impact of a whole range of costs.
