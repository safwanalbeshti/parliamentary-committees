Q1 Chair: Welcome to today’s session. I am delighted that we are able to welcome the Secretary of State to the Committee, along with Amanda Brooks. Thank you very much indeed for joining us this afternoon. We are about to kick off our work plan for 2027 with our big business conference in the House of Commons on Monday, followed by a national road trip and a big nationwide survey to understand exactly what it is that business, trade unions and consumer associations want us to have a look at over the course of 2027.

We are very pleased to be able to kick off that process by asking you about your priorities. Welcome back to the best job in Government. Welcome back to the best Committee in Parliament. You have been out for a bit. You have come back to a Department that covers most of the domestic economy. Give us a sense of the vision and priorities that you have brought back to the Department.

Jonathan Reynolds: Thank you, Chair. It is lovely to be with you, colleagues. As well as being two of the last few Business Secretaries, I was a member of this Committee as well, you might remember, in a previous guise. Thank you for that work you are kicking off, which is really important. This is the best job in Government. The role of this Select Committee is really important within that.

It may be worth pausing for a second to comment on the fact that it is a genuinely new Department. I know that, having done the DBT job before, people might see it in that way, but this is something new for people who are aficionados of how we have structured the Business Department in the UK in recent years. The fact that we now have a Department of 8,500 people with an annual budget of circa £17 billion a year is a real, significant change in terms of the strength of these issues within Government. It is a privilege to come back to be in charge of them.

We cover a lot. I will give you our four public objectives, and I have not spoken of these before; I am revealing them to the Committee in the proper way. The first is to lead on place-based reindustrialisation, using the industrial strategy as the mechanism for that. The second is how we make sure we have better lives and vibrant local economies, and that markets work for citizens. That is everything from our work on making work pay to our SME policy, to how competition law works in the UK and all the rest of the things the Department does.

The third—again, a reference to the new Department structure here—is that science, innovation and technology have to be at the heart of growth. We have to embrace technology to do that. The fourth is that we have always had, you might remember, a commitment to an open, connected trading economy. I would add the words “secure” and “resilient” to that, reflecting the world that we are in. There are a lot of things, but you have to put it down into those four headings for the fundamental objectives of this Department.

Jonathan Reynolds: I am not going to shock you all by telling you what may or may not be in the Budget, or what I would want to be in it. I would look at the public comments of the Chancellor that you have just referenced—the reference to the cost of doing business and the need for breathing space for business. I endorse those words entirely.

There has to be acknowledgement that there is no answer to all the things we, as parliamentarians, and our constituents want to see as priorities for the state and for the Government unless we have a stronger economy than we have had in quite a few years since the financial crisis. The last set of GDP figures is very encouraging, but it is sustained economic growth that will do that. There has to be a reflection that business is the only way to do that. What the Chancellor has said has been significant in that.

Q6 Chair: You have the chance to integrate science and technology spend with real power now that that has moved into your Department. Hitherto, that has been a little disjointed from the industrial strategy. Will the Industrial Strategy Advisory Council now become effectively a reindustrialisation strategy council?

Jonathan Reynolds: The mirror to the success of the industrial strategy was that it gave not just the Department but a whole range of other Government Departments a proper analytical tool as to where the opportunities lay. We have seen that. I thought that we saw, to differing extents, quite a few of the Government Departments respond to that. What was formerly DSIT and UKRI were part of that.

The spend split for UKRI’s budget, which is broadly a third on what we might call curiosity or knowledge-led research and two thirds more applied to the priorities of the industrial strategy, is right. That work had already begun. I think that that is acknowledged.

The premise of reindustrialisation will use the industrial strategy as the tool to do that. I would add into that, as well as the IS-8 sectors, acknowledgement of foundational industries, which reflects existing work in the Department. There is a particular story to address that everyone here will be familiar with, which is how we take our success in those sectors and have more stories of brilliant British innovation resulting in commercialisation, scale-up and significant employment in the UK. I imagine that every single Member of Parliament here has heard too many stories of, “This brilliant idea originated in the UK, but we did not get the economic and commercial benefits of it.” I now think that this Department is ideally placed to play a role in correcting that.

Q7 Chair: We will have more things to say on that tomorrow when we publish the Government response to our investing in the UK report, which was, I am afraid to say, produced under the last Administration, but quite disappointing. Jonathan Reynolds: There is obviously more coherence from having it in one Department. I am trying to say that I believe the work that needed to happen had already begun. Our job is to make sure that that is followed through.

Amanda Brooks: To be specific, UKRI had already shifted around £9 billion of funding to focus on the industrial strategy eight sectors, so that pivot had already been taking place in the way that the Secretary of State has referred to. Q16 Mr Joshua Reynolds: The Defence Secretary has said that the Ministry of Defence will play a central role in the UK’s reindustrialisation. The Chancellor has said that reindustrialisation is an opportunity to support the NEETs crisis. Your Minister for Reindustrialisation has said that reindustrialisation is maximising tradable sectors in every part of the country through innovation. What does that actually mean?

Jonathan Reynolds: For me, reindustrialisation is based on the industrial strategy and the IS-8. As I say, I would add the foundational sectors into that. There is always an ongoing debate in the UK about where the line sits between services and manufacturing. In many cases, that is artificial. Take an iconic manufacturer such as Rolls-Royce. It generates quite a lot of revenue from services alongside that from the products that it produces.

Let us be clear and remember. What are the IS-8? They are the sectors that the data tells us represent both our strengths and our greatest opportunities. The commitment to reindustrialisation recognises that there are some specific things required in policy terms to support those sectors.

Amanda Brooks: In the context of what you were just asking, defence is one of those IS—industrial strategy—sectors. That fits very much with what the Secretary of State for Defence has spoken about, and indeed the current Chancellor. If you look at some of the challenges that we have on NEETs at the moment, this week we have seen the publication of a number of jobs plans reflecting on the different opportunities in those sectors, which will enable future employment opportunities. They are making some big commitments. We know that these sectors, on average, pay about £8,000 more than employment in other sectors. There is a really important link between all those different issues, as you rightly say.

Q17 Mr Joshua Reynolds: The IS-8 include financial services and the creative industries. Is reindustrialisation the IS-8, and therefore is part of reindustrialisation creative industries?

Jonathan Reynolds: The core of this is the IS-8. Of those IS-8, five are perhaps considered traditional manufacturing. Three would be services. Can you make anything in the UK without support from the financial services sector? Can you export anything without access to insurance? Can you run your business without access to professional and business services?

This is probably something this Committee has looked at, Chair. The UK has a history of producing a new document and a new economic theory and basis every year. That has to stop. The Prime Minister’s commitment to reindustrialisation is absolutely the right one, but we should build on the empirical tools that we have, and that is the industrial strategy.

Q18 Mr Joshua Reynolds: Finally on that, the chief exec of Make UK urged the Government, when it comes to the definition of reindustrialisation, to put some flesh on the buzzwords. Do you understand why Make UK might think that reindustrialisation is just a buzzword?

Jonathan Reynolds: I always listen to Make UK because they are very wise people. That is a fair point and I would add to it. I think that we would all want to consider, across Government and Parliament, what the metrics and KPIs we would want to use for that are. I know that there are a lot of colleagues who want a straightforward jobs-based KPI. Perhaps this Committee might be able to assist in that. We might ask the Industrial Strategy Advisory Council for its wider observations. I think that it is quite helpful to bring into account things such as the degree of business investment that is going in. Perhaps we might want to consider some of our exports as part of that. It is not just about the definition; it is what we are measuring going forward.

Q19 Chair: That is very helpful, Secretary of State. You are telling us that we do need to put some tighter definitions on what reindustrialisation needs to deliver in terms of hard outcomes.

Jonathan Reynolds: I think that we all would want to know what we are measuring. What are we agreeing on? What do we disagree on? What do we want to see more of? That helps policy in Parliament and in Government.

Q20 Dan Aldridge: Thank you, Secretary of State. The Resolution Foundation found that more than half of UK postcode areas saw falling living standards during the last Parliament. How are you and your Department going to drive growth in every postcode with that in mind?

Jonathan Reynolds: That is the central challenge for the UK, not just for the Department or the Government but Parliament. Every other challenge, whether it is public services or political fragmentation, comes from the fact that we saw for the first time, and we have seen since the financial crisis, I would argue, an end to what I would call the classic British working class story. My parents only wanted one thing, which is that my sister and I have more opportunities than they did. We are all familiar with the challenge that comes from that. We put into account not just the performance of the economy but things such as the housing market. That tells us where we are at.

How do we do that? Going back to those four objectives, we need to know where our strengths lie, where we need to drive investment and where policy has to support that. That is the industrial strategy. We need vibrant, dynamic markets. That is everything. The Prime Minister’s recent announcement that we will move on non-competes is significant because you are taking a major ask from the tech sector that is, essentially, classic employment policy. This Department now represents both of those things and we can make that activity what we need it to be.

Our plan to make work pay is perhaps more contentious in some quarters. I think we would all recognise, when we are talking about growth in every postcode, that there have been times in British history where the overall national economy has grown but people have not felt it, either in their locality or because of the job they do. It is essentially a class-based assessment as part of that.

We have to put science and innovation at the heart of our economic policy and embrace technology. That is harder than people sometimes accept, because technology disrupts. There are political challenges for all of us in that. We have to remain an open, outward-facing economy—the UK has always been a trading nation—while recognising that we are not living in the late 1990s in terms of free trade and WTO rounds. We have a very different trading situation. We have to think about our national resilience within that, while remaining open and outward-facing to the world.

Q21 Dan Aldridge: One thing that always concerns me as a Member representing a coastal town is whether we are being measured, because we only act on what we measure. Is the Department assessing whether living standards are improving in those postcodes and whether there is the improvement that we hope to see in those postcodes such as mine in Weston-super-Mare?

Jonathan Reynolds: It has to be not just measured but felt. People do not always feel national statistics, and we have to recognise that. It is what Amanda has just said about why the IS-8 sectors are so important. That is because they pay wages that people feel and can appreciate.

The whole Government take into account economic statistics. That is not just a matter for the new BIST Department. The Prime Minister himself has made it clear—and those different elements of the policy menu add up to a recognition of it—that people not only need to be told the country is doing well, but they need to feel it.

Q22 Dan Aldridge: Do we have metrics to see the actual growth in postcodes, but also the feeling? That would be really helpful.

Jonathan Reynolds: You mean breaking down GDP growth on a granular level. I do not know whether the country has that, to be frank.

Amanda Brooks: We can break it down at regional level, but probably not down to the point of every postcode. Unlike Bhutan, which has an excellent focus on tracking happiness in its economy, we do not have that level of detail at the granular level you are talking about.

We do, though, track, across multiple different ways in which the Department’s interventions work, what is happening in places. We know that over half of UKRI spend is outside London and the south-east, so increasingly spread. There is the work that the Prime Minister has announced to amplify that through local innovation partnerships, but also what we do on trade deals. We have always tracked what the impact is on regions of the country and, indeed, the devolved nations. We look at that, but, as you say, at what level of granularity? What you are challenging us to is a whole new level.

Dan Aldridge: Even within local authority levels, we have North Somerset council, which is largely far more affluent than my constituency, where the deprivation actually lies. That has massaged the statistics quite significantly. Even if not postcode, constituency level would be really good to be able to identify whether people are feeling the benefit.

Q23 Chair: You are identifying a constraint. It is quite difficult for the Prime Minister to prove we are delivering growth in every postcode.

Jonathan Reynolds: I really want to stress that it is not just a slogan. I can honestly tell you that I think of the decisions we make through the lens of making sure that prosperity is genuinely a UK-wide thing. When, for instance, we had the issues around the rescue of Harland & Wolff, it really mattered to me that one of those shipyards is on the Western Isles and there is not a lot of intervention, historically, from national Government in a place such as the Western Isles. That made it important. If you look at the recent investment we made in the SaxaVord spaceport in the Shetland Islands, again that is thinking about how you make sure that this is relevant to every bit of the UK.

Q24 Sarah Edwards: The Committee is quite interested in the focus that the Government have on procurement as being a means to spread some of that money that the Government have at their disposal. If you are asking about a non-UK shareholder based in the UK, that is British jobs and growth to me. A Nissan would be a great example of that. Jonathan Reynolds: Made in Europe is a priority not just for this Department but for every Secretary of State because of the significance of what it would represent. At the speed of action with which this is happening, it could increase a hundredfold very quickly indeed.

I am nervous about this “no cards to play” realistic perspective. It sounds very defeatist. The UK is a G7 nation. We have to play our role here domestically and internationally as a convening force. I am not at all suggesting it is easy, but we have to find ways to stand up to these very large hyperscalers and to have control over this rather than ceding control entirely to them. You do not seem to see it that way.

Jonathan Reynolds: No, I would push back strongly on that, Charlie. It is not that we have no cards to play. We have some cards to play. Some countries do not have cards to play. We are the third most significant tech nation in the world. Every other country would want to be us other than the people who are one and two. We have some cards to play.

It is about being clear-eyed. We are keeping the British public safe. I do sometimes hear, not necessarily from parliamentarians but in the wider political argument, proposals that I can see might gain some populist support, but they would not keep the British public safe because they would effectively result in us losing the access that we currently have, which is the fundamental thing that we need to retain right now.

Q89 Charlie Maynard: If I may, Chair, let us say AISI says, “That model is unsatisfactory. We do not think you should release it, Anthropic,” for example, but Anthropic goes ahead and does it. What are we going to do about it? Is the answer that we cannot do anything about it and we have to live with that or do we have a plan?

Jonathan Reynolds: If I may, with all due respect, that perspective is where we are having problems with public opinion and discussing this as parliamentarians. You cannot take a model and somehow say, “That is entirely safe and could never be used for nefarious ends.” It does not work like that. It is knowing its capabilities and preparing the British state—it could be our public services, security services or businesses—to make sure that we are aware of and prepared for that. There is this sense that things come to the AI Security Institute and it says, “That’s a green light. That’s not.” I am afraid it will never be as straightforward as that.

Q90 Charlie Maynard: Models have been taken down by the companies themselves. That is already happening. That is already happening.

Jonathan Reynolds: Therefore, the key thing is making sure that we have, using British expertise, access to those models, knowledge and a feedback loop—

Q91 Charlie Maynard: I get that. What happens if they do it anyway?

Jonathan Reynolds: Given the relationship that we have and the role that we play, it would never be as binary as that, to be clear. What we cannot do—this is a challenge to all parliamentarians—is implement measures that effectively lose the things that are fundamental to keeping the UK safe.

Chair: I am keen to move us on to the EU and trade in our final 10 minutes.

Q92 Mr Joshua Reynolds: On 12 October, the Prime Minister said, when it comes to our deal with the European Union, “Sticking with the status quo is not good enough.” He said last month that we could look at what George Osborne has said about the customs union; that the Government could look at what we as Liberal Democrats said at our conference about the single market; or that the Government could “go all the way”, which I can only assume means rejoining the European Union. Where are the Government on the European Union?

Jonathan Reynolds: You have just outlined what the Prime Minister has said and how he is going to give a speech about this. If we are producing a 10-year plan, given what we have just talked about in this session, it is impossible not to have some reference to the European Union, because what the European Union does, as we have talked about, whether it is steel tariffs or Made in Europe, has significant consequences for us. He wants to have that conversation. He wants to acknowledge that.

For my part, perhaps the more productive part of the conversation is not about some of the models that you have outlined, with no disrespect to Lib Dem policy, but about the shared challenges. We have a US that is adopting a very different and more assertive trade policy than at any point since the war. We have multilateral institutions such as the WTO that are in a very weakened state because of the geopolitical pressures and tensions that go into that. We have global overcapacity in steel, as we have discussed. We have challenges around climate. The better question is, “How can we work with and have influence over the European continent in meeting these challenges?”

Q93 Mr Joshua Reynolds: You say we need to have the conversation. When it comes to the European Union, I am not necessarily sure that there is anything that has not been said in the last decade. All of the thinking that can be thought has been done on this. Everyone has a different opinion. What is the Government’s opinion?

Jonathan Reynolds: Again, I would have to disagree respectfully on that because what we do not know is the future direction of Europe. There are some very strong political currents in Europe. There is an evolving policy agenda that reacts to, for instance, the trade shock from China. We do not quite know where that will go, but we know it will affect us. One of the things that I very much admire about the Prime Minister is that you cannot say he dodges the big issues. He confronts them head on. That is his style. He is right to do so. We have to have this conversation. Again, I would steer you towards how we address these shared challenges.

With reference to the earlier conversation with Charlie, do not discount what we are working on for the UK-EU summit and its significance for us and the EU. On SPS, we do not have a policy akin to North America, Canada and the US. Our SPS policy is broadly aligned to the EU. Removing some of those trading frictions would be a massive deal for small business, farmers and fishermen. Let us recognise that we already have a significant degree of ambition. What the Prime Minister has done is talk perhaps in warmer and more good faith terms about what he is seeking from the European Union, and we will see whether the EU responds to that.

Q94 Mr Joshua Reynolds: We know what different options could look like. We have TCA, TCA plus customs union, Swiss deal and EEA region. We know what many of those different options are. Jonathan Reynolds: We want a closer economic relationship with the European Union.

Q95 Mr Joshua Reynolds: Does that look like the Swiss model or the EEA model?

Jonathan Reynolds: The Prime Minister will set out those options. I am not going to pre-empt a Prime Minister’s speech, but we have shown that we have a nexus of interests that are intertwined come what may. Q96 Charlie Maynard: The Prime Minister said that we may look to “go all the way” to rejoin, but for now the red lines exist of no single market, no customs union and no rejoin. Is that an accurate statement?

Jonathan Reynolds: We have a manifesto commitment on those. Those were his words, which you are repeating to me, on the Floor of the Commons, but he has made it very clear that, when he is looking to this 10-year plan, he wants to recognise that what the EU does will have consequences for us, and he wants to take out uncertainty by talking about what the long-term stable relationship with the EU might be. He wants to set out the options on that. That is what he is going to do as part of that. At the same time, we are working every day on our trade relationship with the EU and with the rest of the world. The UK-EU summit is a big part of that coming up.

Q97 Charlie Maynard: For now, those three red lines are bright red and standing sharp.

Jonathan Reynolds: I am not going to pre-empt the Prime Minister’s speech. You are going to have to wait a few weeks until the UK-EU summit. In terms of what we are negotiating right now, it is consistent with those red lines, yes.

Q98 Chair: We have a couple of moments when this will get clearer. One is the Prime Minister’s speech around 20 November and then there is the 10-year plan as well.

Jonathan Reynolds: I would look to the speech that he is seeking to give imminently in or around the summit.

Q99 Chair: Notwithstanding that, are there still plans afoot to open negotiations to join together with Mercosur?

Q101 Chair: The final thing that we just wanted to put to you, before we close, is around the Post Office Horizon victims. This is something we remain acutely concerned about. Jonathan Reynolds: As you will know from our previous conversations about this, Chair, I have always made this a priority both as shadow Secretary of State, supporting the significant legislative changes that the Conservative Government did at the end in terms of getting justice for people, and, when we came in, by launching the appeal scheme and getting money out the door. We have paid out £1.7 billion and 87% of all applicants have been paid. Like you, I look very closely at the figures. I believe I have them in my pack.

This has been a story. Nothing will ever compensate for the level of injustice that people have suffered. I want to say that unequivocally. From where we were pre-election to where we are now in terms of getting money out the door, we should recognise that a great deal has happened.

I have looked at some of the figures for the different schemes. Jonathan Reynolds: I would not want you to leave it and nor would I leave it. The same is true of the Horizon convictions redress scheme.

Jonathan Reynolds: I have shown a personal willingness to adjust schemes, introduce new schemes and add awards that allow people to get redress in an expedited way, if that is what they want, or go through a longer process, if they want a more significant detailed assessment of their loss.
