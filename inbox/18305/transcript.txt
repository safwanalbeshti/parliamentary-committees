Chair: Thank you very much. Let me ask Simon Evans and Roz Lindridge first. We have done a lot of work and we are obviously looking at it from the Welsh side. Can you describe to us the process of a Welsh patient being referred to an English service? Tell us about the pathway and any potential barriers or difficulties and maybe the good parts to it. That would be helpful.

Simon Evans: Thank you very much. The nature of the referral is ultimately for patients in Wales who are going to a hospital where services are delivered in England because they are specialist services or because it is the nearest hospital. The beginning of that pathway is largely the same for Welsh patients as it is for English patients. They will typically visit their primary care practitioner, their GP, and they will discuss their issues with their practitioner. The GP will make a decision there in terms of referring for specialist services that are typically required by an acute hospital.

At that point, it starts to differ in terms of what happens for Welsh patients and for English patients. Welsh patients accessing English hospitals will use a referral system, which we call the Referral Assessment Service. That is a digital system. It will enable the GP to put the details of the referral into a system that will go through to a service that is hosted by Shrewsbury—the STW ICB. That is a service that was set up after the 2015 Select Committee said that we needed to standardise an approach for Welsh patients being referred into English hospitals. That referral will then go through to the appropriate hospital in England and those specialists—typically consultants—will review that referral and check everything is there that is required in order to be able to take that pathway forward for that patient.

The hospital will then process that patient’s appointment; it will contact the patient directly. It will book that within the hospital and it will, if necessary, liaise with patient transport services in order to be able to get the patient through to the hospital. From that point on, that acute hospital will look after that patient and the rest of their journey, ideally through to treatment and then discharge back.

Ian Townend: This applies equally within England and Wales. Every time someone interacts with a healthcare service, information is generated about that interaction, whether it is clinical information, appointments or referrals. Importantly, there is no single place for that. Simon touched on it earlier: there are multiple different systems. General practices, which have by far the largest collection of individual data, hold their own records. Hospitals hold their own records; mental health, community and other services will all hold their own records. They are mostly digital, but typically they are operated in different systems. It is not unusual for a large trust to have over 200 clinical systems across different things. On the flip side of that, some trusts have single electronic patient records spanning the entirety of the services that they provide. They are often operated by different technology companies as well, so there are barriers in terms of that commercial element.

The interoperability element is the key thing about the data exchange across the borders. As was said for some of the other services, it is not that this problem is solved in England by any means. There are still issues in terms of accessing information from hospitals or between different services. General practice is largely quite accessible; other areas less so. In England, we have made fairly significant progress on some of that around our summary care record or our National Care Records Service and exposing some of that through our NHS app, which I know we will come on to.

Additionally, there is not just the actual physical exchange of the data. There is a need to understand how we make that understandable in the same way, so we have consistent coding across the data so everyone can understand what the actual record means in order to use it, as well as information governance arrangements and things like that, which are some of the barriers for us. We work quite closely with our colleagues in Digital Health and Care Wales. We have now established a chief digital information officer group in fact across all the four home countries plus Ireland, which meets at a strategic level every quarter. We are establishing groups underneath that now to start to make some progress at the working level in terms of the standards.

The standards in particular are one thing. There already exists a standards working group, which is again across all the four home countries, but it is particularly active between England and Wales to develop some of the core common standards that we will need to begin to exchange this information. That is about messaging standards as well as terminology and coding standards. Those groups are meeting much more frequently now and are beginning to publish consistent standards that we will all adopt. It is a devolved power in terms of the standards adoption, but we have all agreed that we will co-produce the materials and then they will be adopted by the various legislative powers in there.

One of the ones that comes in particularly for England is the Data (Use and Access) Act, which for the first time gives us a power to mandate standards on system suppliers. That is an England-only power—it amends the Health and Social Care Act—but the supplier base for many of these across England and Wales is the same.
