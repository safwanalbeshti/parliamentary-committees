Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Jonathan Cribb, Chris Curry and Carl Emmerson.

Chair: A very warm welcome to this first oral evidence session for our new inquiry into pensions adequacy, particularly looking at auto-enrolment for low earners. It is a pleasure to welcome our first panel of the morning. We have Jonathan Cribb, who is Deputy Director at the Institute for Fiscal Studies, Chris Curry, Director of the Pensions Policy Institute, and Carl Emmerson, a partner in London Economics. Thank you again. It is lovely to see many of you again.

My first question relates to the Second Pensions Commission, which obviously you will be very aware of, due to report in the spring, and we hope that this inquiry and the evidence that we manage to get together will contribute to that. However, the Commission has made the point that we need to be clear about what adequacy objectives are. From your points of view on auto-enrolment, what do you think that they should be for low earners? Who would like to start with that one?

Carl Emmerson: I will start if you want. I think we need to look at the system as a whole, not just automatic enrolment. I think there are two obvious objectives for pensions policy as a whole. One is that we want to have a state and private pension system that ensures that we do not have scandalous levels of pensioner poverty and pensioners living in deprivation. We have had periods in history where very large proportions of the pensioner population—extremely large proportions—are living in very low-income households for a long period of time. Thankfully, we do not have that any more. There are some poor pensioners, but pensioner poverty is below the working age poverty rate.

The other objective of pension policy in the round is to try to create an environment where we are helping individuals to achieve a standard of living that is smoothed through their lifetimes so they do not have undue hardship in working life, saving unnecessarily, and they also do not have perhaps too much to spend in working life and then get to retirement and they are disappointed with what they can afford. We are helping people smooth their living standards over their lives. It is those two objectives, I think: a minimum standard in retirement plus some earnings replacement so that people can have a living standard that relates to what they enjoyed when they were working.

That is pretty close to what Beveridge had in mind in his report and I think it is really the system that we have been getting better at and moving closer towards since about 1980, to be honest, where we have been making the flat rate component of the state pension closer and closer to universal for those who spend their whole working lives here. We have also done more and more to ensure that a greater proportion of the adult population are in a private pension scheme, particularly women.

Chris Curry: Yes, I agree with a lot of what Carl has just said. I think if you look at what we are currently using, those are the two main measures—one, in terms of the minimum standards that are used, that, I think, is very important for low earners. As Carl was saying, it is about trying to make sure that people have a standard of living in retirement that allows them to participate in some way in society, which I think is important, and not seeing big drops in living standards when people reach retirement. I think that in itself is difficult. It is a different objective from the poverty alleviation objective that the Government must have as well.

I think there are also some real challenges in how we have been measuring that up until this point. One of the things that is often overlooked and not included is the role of housing. In particular, work that we and others have done is suggesting there will be an increase in the numbers or the proportion of pensioners who are renting in retirement rather than being homeowners, as perhaps previously expected on the trends we were seeing. That in itself creates different challenges in terms of the amount of income that people might need in retirement or how you measure that adequacy.

Jonathan Cribb: Building on those points, this is all right, and these two objectives match to these minimum living standards or replacement rates as adequacy objectives. The only concern I have about the minimum living standard framework is that 20 years ago we would have looked at minimum standards broadly being around pensioner poverty. Pensioner poverty has come down so much and the state pension, the 2010 reforms and the new state pension create a much more solid basis for helping to avoid poverty than was the case before. Although in principle I think we should all be thinking of some level of minimum standard, the current levels that are discussed in the Pensions UK retirement living standards are quite high when you compare them to working age incomes.

So 23% of working age people in a working household fall below the minimum pensioner level and it is much higher for working age singles—lone parents, for example. If you think that is a very strong, important level to get people to, what you are saying is that you really want pensioners to get to a much higher-level minimum than you do working age people. Then there is a question of whether it is appropriate for the state to use resources to try to get to that level. Or is it appropriate to get individuals to save, reducing their resources in working age to get to that higher level in the pensioner period?

Chair: In terms of a hybrid approach?

Jonathan Cribb: I think there is some merit in a hybrid approach. In a sense, you can think about a hybrid approach that takes replacement rates where, roughly, the higher income you have, the higher retirement income you need, although you might need only 50% of pre-retirement income if you are a very high earner, for example. As for the ways of making a hybrid approach, broadly, you can put a cap on the amount—so anyone with an income above X is adequate irrespective of whether they have £3 million or £5 million a year—and set the bottom, below which we say that you are inadequate even if you have a good replacement rate. Broadly, putting a cap on the top does not make very much difference to overall adequacy measures unless you pull it down a very long way. The bottom cap in a hybrid measure would make a lot of difference. The only concern I have is whether integrating two very separate concepts into one is confusing.

Chair: Chris and Carl, anything on the hybrid approach, particularly focusing on low earners?

Chris Curry: It is interesting, isn’t it? I think for low earners the overriding impact would probably be on the standards rather than the target replacement rate if you are talking about very low earners as part of that. A hybrid approach does have some attractions. It really depends on what you are using it for. If you are using it as a way of evaluating policy over time, I think that can be useful. If you are trying to use it to help individuals understand better, it probably is a much more difficult concept to try to get across.

I want to add a couple of bits to what Jonathan said about the comparison between pensioners and the rest of the working age population as well. I think there are some differences that you also need to take into account in there. One is that for pensioners, when they get to that income level, that is set for the rest of their lives, whereas some of the lower groups or some of the groups we are seeing in the working age population may not be in that situation or in that low-income situation for the rest of their life. Low earnings can fluctuate and people’s positions in the earnings distribution can fluctuate as part of that as well.

You also need to take account of the additional needs that pensioners might have—disability benefits or lack of mobility and things like that. You need to make sure that even if you set a standard of living that you think is higher for pensioners than the rest of the population, there could be very good reasons for doing that but you need to be aware of what you are doing and why. I think that is important.

Carl Emmerson: I think as long as we keep clear that the broad objectives are the outlook for pensioner poverty and the outlook for the number of people who perhaps get to retirement who might be disappointed with the standard of living they have relative to their working age level incomes, I think that is what really matters. We can have a range of measures to try to get at that.

When thinking specifically about low earners, I would also add that earning do not only change over someone’s lifetime, meaning they may have more capacity to save in some years than others. Some low earners are partners to someone who is not in work or another low earner and they are in hardship today. Other low earners are often people working part-time, perhaps with a higher earning partner—perhaps they are not in a low-income household. There are issues around what we think about, as to whether the household is poor or whether this is about an individual’s circumstances. Clearly, then there are risks around what happens if that couple splits up subsequently, and there are issues for women there when often the lower earner, the part-time worker, might often be a woman. If she has a higher earning husband, for example, you might think that she would benefit from saving more, whereas another household with a low earner, perhaps with a partner who is not in work, you might really question whether they should be saving more at the moment. I think it is quite difficult to think of policies, at least in the private sector space, that can really target that well and there are lots of trade-offs here.

Chair: One last question from me for the moment. The Commission is suggesting that the pensions policy should be subject to arrangements for holistic review. What should that look like from your perspectives?

Chris Curry: It is important that policy is continually looked at in a holistic sense, whether that is done on a statutory or regular basis or whether that is done in a different way. You can think about it in a broader sense. The IFS has recently done a pensions review. At the PPI, we produce an annual report on the pensions framework which looks at sustainability, adequacy and fairness, which you may recognise as pillars. So there is work that goes on externally in this place.

I think it is really important that that independence is maintained, but if you are thinking about holistic review, you really need to be very clear about what it is that you are actually reviewing and what the output and outcome is of that. There are several different international models but there are very few where it is actually binding. It is more about whether it can be advisory, evaluating, or monitoring, and whether it is maybe an early warning system; there are all sorts of different things that it could be.

Jonathan Cribb: I think that holistic reviews are broadly a good idea and we have had a lot of success in pensions policy in the UK, partly coming from the Pensions Commission and the implementation of the reforms there.

By the time we got to the early 2020s we thought that enough economic change and policy change had happened and that it was time for another review, so if I had to say a number, maybe every 10 to 15 years you might want a good quality review to allow for both some stability in the interim but also the ability to reflect change.

Carl Emmerson: Yes, I agree with that. I think that the Second Pensions Commission is well overdue, so, having a review like that every 10 to 15 years, and I would probably wrap the state pension age review into it as well and think about that system as a whole.

Chair: Thank you. Over to Patrick Hurley.

Patrick Hurley: I want to ask about the triple lock, specifically the Government’s proposed changes to the triple lock. Could you speak to the impact of those proposed changes on the work of the Pensions Commission and specifically whether or not the predictability that we are going to see makes the job easier or whether, because it is less generous, it makes it more difficult? Is there something you can speak to on that perspective?

Jonathan Cribb: I think it does make the Pensions Commission’s job easier in most ways, in that we now will have a more predictable state pension tied in the long run to growth in average earnings, and the underpinning of living standards for the working age population, and it will provide that confident base layer to the pension system. The reform has reduced the fiscal risk to the Government that they will have to cut back dramatically at some point in a crisis, whether that be on the level or a much higher state pension age. Broadly I think it makes it easier.

If you really believed that the triple lock were to actually stay for a very long period—I think we have to put a very big question mark on that—then clearly the change now means a lower state pension as a fraction of average earnings in the long run, so you would probably want higher levels of private saving to make up for that. However, I think there really is a question about whether it was ever credible that we were going to keep the triple lock for a very long period.

Chris Curry: I think that is right. It has definitely made the job clearer. It has brought it into a starker focus. I do not think anyone really expected the triple lock to remain until the 2050s or the 2070s, which is the timeframe that the Commission is looking at in terms of where the outcomes of its changes are likely to really impact. By having the change in advance, it does make it clearer.

It does provide more challenges, though, I think, as Jonathan was saying, in that the role of the state is now probably slightly better defined but it is at a lower level than many of the projections that the Pensions Commission would have been working with were actually showing. That does increase the importance of the role of private pension saving. We have not had a chance to run all the numbers through and do a lot more different analysis, but looking back at analysis that we did at the PPI when the triple lock was first introduced, we estimated that the introduction of the triple lock would mean that people could put about 6% less into their private pensions because that would be coming from the state pension instead. It is probably not quite that big a gap now because the triple lock has been more generous than expected and has been in place over time, so it is probably a smaller gap than that.

There was some interesting OBR analysis in the Pensions Commission the interim report that suggested that on the central scenario for the triple lock going forward, we had the number of people undersaving as 15 million. If it looked at a lower triple lock, and one that is going to give an outcome much more similar to the new policy, where it was about 0.1% ahead of average earnings, that increased to 19 million people undersaving. It does actually make it clearer how big the challenge is and that might be something that is more helpful to the Commission in making some of the decisions it has to make.

Carl Emmerson: I agree with everything that Chris and Jonathan have just said.

One set of analysis we did and published last month in a report for the People’s Partnership shows that if you take someone on £40,000 a year and you had them saving at 8% of qualifying earnings with the triple lock, and then you move to the adjusted triple lock to get the same retirement income after tax, we think they would need to save about 12% of qualifying earnings. I think the change that the Government have announced makes the case for what the Pensions Commission needs to do much, much clearer because it does not need to get into the world of predicting when the triple lock will go. It has a lot of clarity there, but it also makes the case for more private saving stronger, as we have just heard.

Patrick Hurley: You have all mentioned the changes or the impact on levels of private saving. Could you speak to the disproportionate impact on how much low earners specifically might need to save—that is, people who are earning quite a lot less than £40,000?

Chris Curry: As we have already touched on a little bit, the state pension is really important for low earners in terms of what it provides as a proportion of the full income in retirement that they are likely to get. We know about, and we will talk further about this, I am sure, the challenges for low earners in saving and diverting money away from what they need to live on today to contributions to fund their future as well, but I think this does make it clearer that there is still a role for private saving for low earners. I think it does provide that longer-term level that the state will provide as part of that. We already know the level it is likely to be at, around 30% of national average earnings, which is already below the Pensions UK minimum retirement living standard, so there is already a gap there that needs to be filled.

I think it brings into sharp relief the fact that there will need to be saving across people’s working lives irrespective of what level of income they have across that working life, but it does also bring those challenges. In terms of reaching the minimum level, I think it might disproportionately impact the lower earners but it does also have an impact on higher earners, who now have to do more in order to avoid that fall in living standards that we were talking about.

Patrick Hurley: To tease that out and to make it explicit then, is it your view that the new state pension, for those who are solely reliant on it, is not an adequate income in retirement?

Chris Curry: It depends, going back to what we were talking about, on the adequacy definition as part of that. The current level of the new state pension, and what it is predicted to be when it gets to the new triple lock arrangement, will be below what is currently used and has been used by the Commission and others as the minimum income level.

Carl Emmerson: Different people will reasonably disagree about what the right level of standard of living for someone to have is. I think it is worth noting that if you have a couple where they both receive a full new state pension and they have no housing costs, they are well above the commonly used poverty line. I am not saying they have a fantastic standard of living in retirement, but they are well above the poverty line. If you take a single pensioner getting a full new state pension, they are pretty close to that poverty line. I think for them the big issue is their housing costs. If they are an owner-occupier who owns outright, they have no housing costs; if they are in the private rented sector and their housing benefit covers their rent, they have no housing costs; but often pensioners are living in the private rented sector and often the housing benefit is not covering their rent. There is an issue for those people, as we spoke about earlier, around the housing market and the costs there.

Patrick Hurley: Do you think there needs to be some mitigation for those who might be in pensioner poverty through housing benefits and other measures that could be implemented?

Carl Emmerson: In my previous role at the Institute for Fiscal Studies, when I was working with Jonathan, we put forward a suggestion—this would cost money. If you are a single pensioner or a couple pensioner, with no one else in the household at the moment, you are entitled to a rent that is based on the local rents of one-bedroom accommodation; we put forward the suggestion that maybe every pensioner should be entitled to at least two bedrooms. That would give them more money if they still wanted to live in one-bedroom accommodation, of course, and you might think there are arguments why pensioners need slightly a bit more than working age population. They spend more time at home. They might have special caring needs. There may be reasons why that is there. They may have adult children who want to come and visit. There may just be reasons why you might think it would be justifiable to suggest that a second bedroom would be justified, and in the social rented sector the policy that is commonly known as the bedroom tax—that is not its formal name—does not apply to pensioners so it would also be an equalisation between those two sectors.

Patrick Hurley: I am out of time, so I will pass it back to the Chair. Thank you.

Mr Peter Bedford: You have already touched on contribution rates in some of your answers today. I am keen to hear your views on Pensions UK and other organisations’ views on increasing the contribution rates from 8% to 12%. Perhaps each of you could give me a short response to that.

Jonathan Cribb: I do not have a recommendation for an exact number, but broadly I think that a move towards 12% is sensible, although I would be much more supportive of 12% on qualifying earnings, so that is on earnings above about £6,000, than of 12% on all earnings because of the disproportionate impact of reducing the qualifying earnings lower limit down to zero on the living standards today of low earners.

Chris Curry: There is certainly a case for higher contributions. Whether 12% is the right level or not depends on individual circumstances. I think the case for higher contributions is partly about individuals providing for themselves in retirement. I think there is also a wider general economic case that we are thinking about more generally in terms of investment into the UK and the relatively low savings rate that we have in the UK, and a way of improving that, as well as the wider potential implications there as well.

I think the real challenge, as Jonathan has alluded to, is the real crux of automatic enrolment is low earners, and we know that some people will benefit and will be able to afford 12% contributions and some will not. We have to make a view as to whether we want an inclusive system, which includes people who will benefit at the risk of some people who will end up, if I can term it that way, oversaving, or do we have an exclusive system that protects those people from oversaving, but brings in or means that people who might benefit from automatic enrolment do not get the employer contributions.

It is very gendered in how we look at this as well. Women are disproportionately impacted if we go for an exclusion system, and disproportionately benefit if we come to an inclusive system. Whichever way you want to go, irrespective of what the contribution level is, you need to look at the mitigation—so, how you either, in an exclusive system, help those people to come into saving or, in an inclusive system, protect them from oversaving. That is one of the challenges the Commission is going to have to grapple with.

Carl Emmerson: The case for going for something bigger than 8% of qualifying earnings is really clear. There is a significant minority on DWP’s own numbers of middle earners undersaving, so we are talking 40%-plus. Those numbers will go up when we move to looking at it under the adjusted triple lock. If you take the first Pensions Commission’s report, it said we will do automatic enrolment, and actually that happened, and it has been successful at bringing people in. It said we will have these 8% defaults and that will bring about a load of additional saving, and it has been really successful at doing that and I think probably even more successful than was predicted on those things. It also said a whole load of additional voluntary saving would come on top of that, and that has not happened. Even the first Pensions Commission’s report was basically saying we needed more saving than we have ended up with. We know automatic enrolment works, it is really powerful; we just need to be careful about where we are using that power.

Jonathan Cribb: I think there is a big difference here: if you go to 12%, say, is it a default or is it a minimum? If we are going to increase it, are we going to allow people to save slightly lower, or if they want to save slightly lower do they have to leave entirely and go to zero? If you default people in at 12%—say 6% and 6% for employer and employee—and you do not let them, then you are making the choice of staying in or going out much starker than you are at, say, 8% of qualifying income.

Mr Peter Bedford: You would suggest some flexibility and agility in any policy change in this space, because it would perhaps allow lower earners to be able to participate more in the process?

Jonathan Cribb: I would agree that certainly the higher you push these default rates, the stronger the case is for allowing people to save, stay in a pension scheme, retain an employer contribution and save a little bit less, and that underlies various things that Carl and I proposed a couple of years ago, either allowing in extremis people to retain an employer contribution even if they did not save themselves, and/or being able to opt down to a lower level. That does increase flexibility, and it does at least give people the potential to do something different than just go, “I am opting out entirely” if they are financially stressed by default or minimum pension contributions.

Mr Peter Bedford: I am keen to hear views on how that could be split. If it was to go to 12% between employee and employer, there are different views on this. Some suggest it could be equally split between employee and employer. I am keen to get your views on that, given that at the moment, business in the UK is under a lot of pressure—there have been a lot of increases in taxation, inflation, and all those economic headwinds. How would that work in reality without having an adverse impact on the broader economy?

Chris Curry: I will take this one. A lot depends on implementation and how you go about doing it. I think the UK and the automatic enrolment system is quite unusual compared to international systems and to the previous system we had in the UK with the split between employer and employee contributions. Generally, the employer contribution tends to be higher where there is a mixed economy system. If you go right back to the underlying economics, if you give employers enough notice of a change coming, then actually, ultimately, all the contributions come from the employee as part of the remuneration. It is not necessarily completely that—there is potentially some impact on prices, there is some impact on employment—but if you give an employer enough notice, then they will adjust the remuneration package to make sure that earnings growth is slightly subdued to make up for the impact of the contribution that the employer is paying on the individual’s behalf.

Mr Peter Bedford: On that point about notice, what would you consider to be a reasonable period of notice for businesses to adapt to any potential change in this area?

Chris Curry: If you look back to the successful implementation of automatic enrolment and when it started to go ahead, we are talking about a minimum of five, probably up to 10 years. When it was introduced, it was introduced for larger employers first, at very low contribution levels, then the rest of the employers, and then slowly over time it increased over another two to three-year period, but that was set out a long time in advance and I think that is really important.

Where it does matter, because the delivery is important, is on incentives as well. If you have a large individual contribution and a small employer contribution, the incentive to opt out is that much greater, because you get back more of a contribution and you give up less to your employer. I will caveat that by saying we are not seeing a large number of people opting out at the moment, even though we have that imbalance. We know that the automatic enrolment is very sticky at the moment, but we do not know what that might be like at higher levels.

There is something about the delivery mechanism. There is something about it being a shared endeavour, I think, as well, between employer and employee and Government being in it all together. However, I think you would probably see a better long-term sustainable impact if it was more on the employer rather than the employee, assuming that you could give enough notice to allow employers to adjust, and assuming that you did not introduce it at a time when it was already very difficult for employers. I think you do have to look at the wider economic circumstances around the time of implementation as well.

Carl Emmerson: I can add a little bit to that. I definitely agree that giving plenty of notice and then going gradually is important. I think you want to encourage consideration of the national living wage to be done alongside that. If you are increasing costs on employers of employing people in one dimension, you might want to be just a bit more cautious in another dimension and think about the overall package. The case for slightly slower national minimum wage increases could be made if you are increasing employer contributions at that moment.

The other observation, and this is easier to hope for, I guess, is that it is probably easier to do in an environment where there is relatively strong nominal wage growth, and therefore the forecasts in the Budget from a couple of weeks ago suggesting that, at least in this decade, real terms income is not growing very fast makes it a difficult time. Clearly, if the mechanism is that employers are going to run wage growth slightly slower to offset the costs, it is probably easier for them to do if there is some wage growth in the system.

Mr Peter Bedford: We are talking about employers as one holistic group of people. Actually, they vary. There are large employers, small employers, and there are some sectors that may need additional support. What do you think could happen in this space policywise to ensure that maybe smaller employers, or employers in particular sectors, can play their part but also not be put out of business by these pressures?

Carl Emmerson: That is also where the giving plenty of notice and allowing gradual adjustment helps as well, because maybe the bigger employers can handle quick change more comfortably than smaller employers. They will have the systems to do that.

We also have to be very mindful that some sectors will get hit harder than others by any change and exactly how you change the system will have different consequences for different sectors. The other side of the what about low earners coin will be what about the sectors that disproportionately employ low earners.

Jonathan Cribb: It is also about that interaction of the national living wage in different sectors because we have a much higher minimum wage now. Although I agree with Chris that the evidence points to very high pass-through into wages, that is more difficult in the presence of a high national minimum wage or national living wage. What does that mean? That means if employees cannot pass it through, then minimum wage employees become more expensive to employ. That is, I think, what underlies—I would hope underlies—Carl’s point about moderating that.

Another thing I would say on small employers is about the administrative burden of automatic enrolment. Broadly, we have a system that was set up in part to try to minimise the burden on small employers, for whom it is harder to administer these things than it is for large companies with very large HR departments or finance departments. I think if you move away from the relatively simple system that we have to any other form of complexity—although I am not saying we should not consider that—we should consider how small employers are going to actually implement it.

Mr Peter Bedford: I am really concerned about the smaller businesses because, as you say, large companies can often adapt because they have huge HR departments, they have the infrastructure, they perhaps have reserves to do so, but if you are a small family-run business, if you are running a café or a local restaurant, then it is very difficult for you, when your margins are already very tight, to find additional contributions for a pension scheme.

Chris Curry: We have talked about the notice, we have talked about the time, we have talked about gradual implementations so people have time to adjust, but it is also I think a good example of where we have to look more broadly than just the pension system for some of these challenges. The difficulties faced by small employers, in much the same way as the difficulties faced by people on low incomes at the moment, are not necessarily issues that the pension system can solve, but they are ones that the pension system needs to be acutely aware of. I think this is where we probably need a more—we mentioned “holistic” earlier—holistic and joined-up approach across different policy areas.

Also, when you talk about the implementation, you might want to think about some of the implementation being conditional in what you are looking at. When automatic enrolment was introduced, it started in 2012. It originally aimed to be introduced earlier, it was delayed, and then the introduction of the increasing contributions was pushed back because of the economic conditions at the time. It is much easier to do that when you have a timetable in place and everything set out than it is to try to bring something forward quickly when you think the economic conditions are in the right place. That communication is really important, but if we are thinking about implementing this in 10 years’ time, there is a lot that can happen in the labour market, in earnings terms, in business, in the economy, and you might want to start thinking about how all these things interact and how you work with them in order to make it work when implementation does come.

A final point on this: one of the things that worked really well was the increases being introduced at the start of the financial year, which meant that there were other changes. Back in that decade, we had things such as changes in the personal allowance and national insurance thresholds, and that offset to a certain extent some of the changes that both employers and employees were seeing when they were making that change. So think about how you can work with other parts of the system to maybe make sure that the burden is shifting towards pensions but away from other aspects as well.

Mr Peter Bedford: Thank you very much.

Chair: We are on to John Milne now.

John Milne: This question is for you initially, Chris. In your 2017 review of auto-enrolment, you recommended removing the lower earnings limit altogether. Do you still think that? What do you think about the risks to lower income individuals?

Chris Curry: A lot has changed since the 2017 review, but unfortunately not in terms of implementing the recommendations; they are still outstanding. A lot of the issues that we were dealing with then still exist, and we have talked about a lot of them, including the challenges for low earners, and in particular those low earners who probably could benefit from being part of automatic enrolment and having higher contributions as part of automatic enrolment.

It is important to remember that the recommendation in 2017 was part of a package of recommendations. It was not stand-alone. We were also recommending that contributions or automatic enrolment started from age 18 rather than 22, and that we should bring in the self-employed into saving in some way, as then unspecified and I think as still unspecified at the moment, but it was really part of a package that was aiming to build on the introduction of automatic enrolment to make it become a social and cultural norm, so that pension saving is something that is just part of the workplace. So everybody, when they go into work or even if they work for themselves, makes a contribution. At that time, it would have been an 8% contribution that covered all their earnings, and it becomes a very simple message and something that becomes very easy for people to get behind and to understand.

The situation may be different now. I would still consider it as part of a package of reforms, but it has to be part of a package of reforms, and it depends on what else you are doing as to what the benefit might be. We are already seeing it being eroded slowly over time anyway. It was, I think, £5,800 back in 2017. It is only £6,200 now, so it is not really—

John Milne: A bit of fiscal drag. We love fiscal drag.

Chris Curry: Fiscal drag does help in that.
