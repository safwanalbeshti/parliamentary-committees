Q2 John Glen: I would like to start by getting all of your assessments of the state of the economy and your views on its situation now, given what has happened with the Iran war. We now see 10-year gilts at 5.41% and 30-year gilts at 5.88%; they topped 6% for the first time since 1998. What is your overall assessment of the state of the UK economy?

Dr Beck-Friis: We have been surprised by the resilience of the UK economy, but it is important to put this in a global context. Growth has been strong everywhere since the start of the year, so in the US, Asia, Japan and most of Europe. Even Germany, which has been in a very long recession, picked up at the start of the year. This is nothing, in our mind, that is UK-specific. There are global drivers behind this, but of course it has been surprising in the context of the war. In particular, it has been surprising in the UK given the state of the labour market and the fact that fiscal policy here is tight. In most other countries it is neutral or loose.

What is the main source of resilience? AI is clearly part of that story. It is showing up in two ways. First is investment spending. We are in the midst of a global capex boom. We have seen this in Europe, but it is clearly concentrated in the US and Asia. That is spilling over into strong exports across the world. Secondly, it is showing up in higher equity prices, and in asset prices more broadly. That means that consumers in general have continued to spend. Even though interest rates have increased, broader financial conditions have not tightened that much in the context of higher asset prices.

In our outlook, there is a tremendous amount of uncertainty. We need to be humble. There are a lot of upside and downside risks, but the baseline is that this resilience will continue. It is easy to think of downside risk in the context of AI spending, uncertainty around energy prices and uncertainty around financial conditions.

Q3 John Glen: Can I follow up on that? A new assessment was made by the IMF yesterday about UK growth of 1.2% for 2027, set against the OBR’s March expectation of 1.6% for 2027. Could I draw you to comment on what you think the outlook is for next year and whether you see this resilience continuing, given what we see in the markets?

Dr Beck-Friis: Our baseline view is that growth will be slightly above 1%, not 1.6%—that would not be our baseline outlook—but that the resilience continues. We tend to think that UK trend growth is somewhere around 1%, or perhaps a little higher, so next year trend line growth perhaps slightly stronger.

Q4 John Glen: What is the reason for the OBR’s optimistic perspective?

Dr Beck-Friis: It has been optimistic for many years. It downgraded productivity growth last year, so that was marked to market to what the sell side and the private sector were expecting. There has been a slowdown in UK trend growth over the last few decades, and it takes a long time for forecasters to update their assumptions. If you look at growth in the UK over the last few years, it has been fairly soft around 1%. It is not 0%, but it is not like the US growth of 2%. Forecasting is very difficult. A baseline is to extrapolate what the recent trend is. If that has been around 1%, I think that is a fair baseline to start with.

Lord O’Neill: I agree especially with the earlier parts of that. One of the many fascinating things to me is that, I think, growth over the first half of the year is actually running in excess of 2%. Even when I point that out to people around this part of the world, and many others, they either do not believe it or dispute it. It is possibly a consequence of growth having disappointed for so long.

Q5 John Glen: Have we seen a phenomenon, though, of higher growth in Q1 and Q2?

Lord O’Neill: Part of the sell-side community is taking it for granted that that is some kind of bad thing going on with the seasonals. Whether it might be, who knows? Thank God I do not have to do the forecasting thing I once had to do for so long.

John Glen: Sorry for asking you.

Lord O’Neill: I do not dismiss the possibility that, in this case, the consensus has got it wrong. It could be something of more substance.

I will maybe come back to this, but one thing on which I have a somewhat outside-consensus view is that it is not impossible that some of that has nothing to do with AI, and that it is because the national insurance surcharge increase was such a shock to employers that it has indirectly kicked off some steps that boost productivity. It is quite feasible that that might be happening.

Q6 John Glen: Could I draw you into the other things around the gilt rates and so on? Lord O’Neill: Here is the expert for the gilt market, I guess. It is worth emphasising, and I recently looked at it again a bit more closely. It is not obvious to me that the gilt market is necessarily being penalised because of our relative shifts in fiscal things, although—I contradict myself—the absolute level of where our yields are obviously has something to do with it.

From the last time I looked, which I think was Friday, our gilt market has not been hurt as much as any other G7 bond market over the past year. When I delve into it with my own analysis, not so intensely as I once would, I think it is something more to do with the inflation outlook, inflation expectations and, crucially, the market assumption for the profile of the Bank of England, at least from a cyclical perspective. That would be my guess.

Q7 John Glen: Could I ask you the same question about your view on 2027 and the views of the OBR and the IMF? Where do you see things rolling forward to next year?

Lord O’Neill: Linked to how I answered the first question, I do not dismiss the possibility that the OBR might be right. As it relates to other things we are going to get into, the most important thing for me, from a cyclical or tactical perspective, is that this Budget does not screw any of it up, because it has been quite a while since we have had 2% growth.

Q8 John Glen: Dr Beck-Friis, do you have any response to what Lord O’Neill has said, particularly about the market’s behaviour on gilts?

Dr Beck-Friis: I agree. If you look over the last year, we have seen a lot of volatility in global fixed income, and a pretty sharp repricing in global fixed income. UK yields are higher, but they have been higher or going higher in most other countries in the world, so the US, Japan, most of Europe and Australia. It has not been driven by UK-specific factors but instead by global factors. I am sure we can get into these, but the energy shock has been part of it. The fact that we are in the midst of a global capex boom is part of it. I do not think that global fiscal policy or debt sustainability concerns have been a key driver of why rates have increased over the last year or so.

That said, if you take a snapshot of where yields are now in the UK, they are high, and a little bit higher than in the US. The reason they are high is that the front end of the curve is high. That means that the policy rate is high, and there is an expectation that the Bank of England will tighten monetary policy going forward. Four hikes are now priced in for the Bank of England over the next year, and this clearly relates to the inflation outlook. Inflation has been high for a long time, and there are inflation risks going forward.

On fiscal policy and fiscal policy credibility, yes, that is lifting yields a little bit here in the UK, but I would not overplay that. Perhaps it is worth about 20 basis points or so. The way you can look at this is to look at the shape of the interest rate curve. When markets are getting worried about debt sustainability and fiscal credibility in a country, the curve tends to steepen. The long-dated bond yields tend to go up more than those for short-dated bonds. If you look at the shape of the UK curve, yes, it is a little steeper than in the US and Germany, but again, in the context of perhaps 20 basis points or so, I would not overplay it. The key reason why rates in the UK are high is that the Bank of England policy rate is high, and there is an expectation that it will raise the policy rate going forward.

John Glen: Did you want to say anything more, Lord O’Neill, on that first question?

Lord O’Neill: I agree with that.

Q9 John Glen: Professor Valero, would you address the same question, please?

Professor Valero: I can perhaps speak more in depth on growth, productivity and those aspects. The IMF concluded that, globally, we have seen more resilience than perhaps was anticipated, but we have also seen, as Jim said, that the UK has done relatively well. While our growth is not good relative to the past, where we had growth rates over or around 2%, so far in 2026 we are top of the G7. In terms of where the IMF and the OECD have put forecasts for this year, we are second in the G7. This is something to celebrate in some sense. There have been a lot of issues and uncertainties globally to deal with.

Underlying that, to go a bit more into the productivity data, since the financial crisis many of us have been working on this productivity puzzle in the UK. It is something that is shared by other economies, but the UK’s was considered to be worse. This was an issue for us because we already had a productivity gap versus our main comparators, and our worse performance meant that the gap was getting bigger.

Recently we have seen two revisions in the data, which suggest that actually our productivity is better than we thought. In per-worker terms, since 2024 performance has been improving when we use better-measured labour inputs, so administrative data from PAYE tax records. You will see that, between 2024 Q3 and 2026 Q2, GVA per worker has increased by 2.8% over that entire period. Annualised, it is 1.6%. This is actually quite close to our pre-crisis trend in per-worker terms, which was around 2%, and much higher than what we saw in the previous methodologies.

Q10 John Glen: Has there been any attempt to retrofit the methodology to previous periods?

Professor Valero: Yes. This is now taking a restated period.

Q11 John Glen: That is from the GFC to 2024 as a whole?

Professor Valero: Yes. All this is driven by how you measure workers. To Jim’s point, it does not necessarily seem to be a shedding of lower-paid workers in sectors. The Resolution Foundation has done some work on that. We have also looked at the sectoral makeup, as it has too. It is quite broad-based across sectors as well. ICT makes quite a positive contribution, but so do other sectors.

That was GVA per worker, so one way of measuring productivity. The other, more recent, revision is to do with how we measure hours, so bringing our methodologies in line with international approaches. This is actually a big shock. That shows that our slowdown since the financial crisis is not as bad as previously thought. The previous thing I talked about was the recent uptick. Now I am talking about what we have seen in that entire period since the financial crisis. GVA per hour is a better measure because you can account for the fact that different workers work different numbers of hours. Before the financial crisis, we had around 2% annual average growth. Restated, it is now 1.3%. That is really important.

Q12 John Glen: Can I ask you about that in principle? It probably seems strange to people watching this that you are restating the last 16 or 18 years of productivity trajectory in the economy, as a consequence of a new statistical method. Is that what you are saying is happening?

Professor Valero: Yes.

John Glen: It was true anyway, except we did not measure it right?

Professor Valero: Yes, that is the thing. It is a measurement issue. Of course, this does not mean that our productivity issues are solved. It does not. Ultimately, we have seen stagnating living standards since the financial crisis. We still have an overall slowdown that needs to be dealt with. We have large-scale regional gaps within the country, and we still have that gap with our international peers, so of course we still need to focus on raising productivity. It is just this aspect.

There are two sources of optimism. One, our slowdown has not been worse than our comparators. Two, we have had an uptick in per-worker terms more recently. You can then look at the things that underlie productivity growth. We have also seen that business investment, for example, is seeing some uptick over the recent period.

Q13 John Glen: I am mindful that more colleagues want to come in. Can we look at the context for the Budget—that is what we are here for—in terms of what things are going to drive growth and productivity? Lord O’Neill has made some statements over the last few months, and we will come to them in a moment. What should be the priorities for the Government, in terms of the decisions they can make two weeks today, to improve the growth and productivity trajectory, and to continue what you say is already happening?

Professor Valero: We have this recent uptick in business investment. We have seen business confidence recovering. This is fragile, given the continued global uncertainty, so I would not want to see that damaged. This is in line with what you have talked about recently, so being mindful of raising costs for business to maintain this increased investment and positive sentiment. I think the Barclays Business Prosperity Index talks about confidence rising and stronger appetite for investment.

Q14 John Glen: More tax on banks and energy companies would not be a good thing.

Professor Valero: In general, I would prefer to avoid taxes on business, but this is a difficult fiscal context and there are many different objectives. The other thing I would want to prioritise, and this is something the previous Chancellor did with her change to the fiscal rules, is public sector investment. This has historically been the margin to be adjusted when fiscal situations get difficult, but this is so crucial for growth.

Q15 John Glen: Lord O’Neill, you have made a significant intervention against the alignment of CGT rates to income tax. It seems to me that is probably being ruled out, or at least the realignment has, whether or not there is actually going to be an increase in CGT. It seems to me that Rachel Reeves did a delicate job of increasing CGT to an optimal level to increase revenue, but not to deter people from liquidising their assets. Do you think any further increase in CGT would be detrimental?

Lord O’Neill: To refer back to Anna’s thing briefly, even without the data revisions, I think I am right in saying—Anna, you know it better than virtually anybody—that, on the old data, there also has been a productivity improvement in the past few quarters.

Professor Valero: Yes, potentially marginal. It is not too much. I have the data here.

Lord O’Neill: My overall view, which I reiterated in just the past few days, is that, because of these cautiously encouraging trends, the last thing the Budget should be doing is anything that interrupts them. Given how business feels so beaten up, over however far you want to think, any tax increase needs to be thought through more carefully than before, especially things such as CGT. In another part of my life, I am heavily involved in venture capital investing, especially as it relates to regional things. This is highly risky stuff. This simplistic view that it should be taxed at the same level as income, in my view, has simply no understanding of those kinds of things.

More importantly, and I am sure we will get into this quite a bit—quite a few of you guys have been involved in this stuff before—the whole reason why there is a fiscal buffer has got a bit lost in the way people look at it. It is supposed to be for when there are exceptional circumstances. I personally think that these are pretty exceptional, as one of you mentioned as part of your questioning, with oil prices in particular. Who knows where our wonderful leader in Washington is going next, but it is not inconceivable that, a week after the Budget, oil prices could be considerably lower. You want to use the buffer when you really need it, in my view.

Q16 John Glen: Can I draw you a bit more on two aspects? One, do you think that means no increase in CGT at all? When we talk about financial services, that is an industry you are intimately familiar with and have been a senior leader in for many years. If you think of the employer national insurance, the corporation tax, the surcharge, the levy and the fusion of those, I used to talk to bank CEOs and they moaned several years ago that it was too high. Lord O’Neill: I would be more opposed to anything on CGT. If some numbers have to be found, I would think it might default to some kind of modest move on bank taxes. I would not be a fan of that either, for the reasons that you touched on. You have to make the numbers work on something, and everybody always rules out the infamous manifesto pledges. You have to do it on something.

Q17 John Glen: One other thing that you are a great proponent of and advocate for is looking at the regional economy. Mindful of time, is there anything you would like to say in terms of stimulus there?

Chair: We will come to this in more detail later.

Lord O’Neill: For me, this is the most important thing coming in this Budget. I think a lot of people do not quite get how this Prime Minister thinks it is a pretty important lever that the country has underutilised.

Q18 John Glen: If I may, I will return to Dr Beck-Friis on the question of what measures would be most impactful for productivity growth in the Budget in two weeks’ time. How would you respond?

Dr Beck-Friis: I can speak from the point of view of what the bond market would like to see. It is easy to say from a bond market point of view, because I understand the political constraints on tightening fiscal policy, but the bond market would welcome more fiscal tightening and, in particular, an effort to bring down the near-term deficit, not the long-term deficit or the headroom. To the extent that the Government surprise the market with more tightening than expected, I think the bond market would—

Q19 John Glen: What if they do not?

Dr Beck-Friis: If they surprise with running more expansionary fiscal policy and there is more fiscal slippage, the bond market is now on high alert and that would almost certainly lead to higher interest rates, depending on what type of fiscal slippage it would be.

Q20 Bobby Dean: To follow on from that point, I appreciate the point you made, Lord O’Neill, about how the headroom is there for buffer room and this is a time to use buffer room, but it also feels like we are living through times where there is always an excuse to eat into the buffer room. Every year there is a new event that means that perhaps now is the time to eat into the buffer room. It is one of the reasons, I guess, that the previous Chancellor was forced into lifting the overall headroom, because it was being buffeted by events.

Perhaps this is also a question for Dr Beck-Friis, so please come in afterwards. Do you think the bond markets would tolerate it if they were to say, “Actually, we need to eat into the buffer again, because yet again there is a crisis in the world that we need to work around”?

Lord O’Neill: It is clearly a risk, for the reasons you say, but it could be carefully explained. The way things seem to work these days, what is also interesting so far about this Budget is that virtually nothing of what they are supposedly planning to do has been leaked, except the fact that they appear to have floated the idea of playing around with the headroom, partly to get the reaction of people in the gilt market, I assume. It does not look to have upset them yet.

It is a risky thing because of exactly what you say. I personally think we have something a little rare in recent years, as there appears to be some growth taking place, and there are genuinely wild things going on globally that have not been there to this degree, other than maybe in the first year of the Russian invasion. The Budget numbers look more difficult primarily for these reasons.

Bobby Dean: Of course, we had tariffs and wars in between.

Lord O’Neill: It comes back to the big picture things. Chair, I am sure we will get into what are often called the sacred cows, or what we now like to call golden opportunities. Personally, I think the bond market should be, and probably is, more interested in how serious a leader in this country is going to be at some point about dealing with the things that seem to petrify everybody. If they do that, I do not think it makes the slightest bit of difference whether it is £5 billion wrong here or there.

Chair: It is a call for political courage.

Bobby Dean: That sets up my next question for Dr Beck-Friis.

Lord O’Neill: It is leadership.

Chair: Leadership is good.

Q21 Bobby Dean: We often obsess about this point figure, and I think you indicated in your previous answer that that is not the thing to focus on. It is certainly not the bond market’s focus. Let us assume that the buffer level comes down, for whatever reason. What else would you be looking out for to reassure you that debt sustainability was still going in the right direction?

Dr Beck-Friis: I think the bond market will be fairly sanguine if they reduce the headroom to £15 billion. If it is £10 billion, £15 billion or £20 billion, these numbers are very small in the context of the uncertainty. We spend a lot of time at PIMCO debating where the economy is today and where it will be in three months’ time. If you are forecasting where it will be in three years’ time, the uncertainty band is so high that we and the bond market put very little weight on a few billions three years out, particularly in an election year.

Markets will discount. Of course, to the extent the Government reduce the headroom, that is a bad signal in terms of fiscal commitment, but it is not what the bond market will focus on. When I open up the OBR document, the first thing I will look at is what the gilt issuance will be this year and next year, and in particular what the deficit will be this year and next year, and potentially in two years’ time, because that dictates the amount of issuance. The closer it is to today, the more credible it is.

For us—I said this last year, and we continue to say it—it will be increasingly important to see that the deficit continues to come down. It has come down. Now it is running at around 4%. A year ago or two years ago, it was 5.5%. There has been a reduction, but there is more to do. The bond market will be looking for that deficit to continue to come down, particularly to something close to a three handle. Preferably, the bond market would perceive a mid-to-low three handle next year as very encouraging.

Q22 Bobby Dean: Moving on to other things that drive growth, Professor Valero, I know you were involved in the formation of the industrial strategy in your time with the Treasury. Could you explain to me how you think the industrial strategy is going to drive growth? Could you address some of the headline criticisms of industrial strategies in general, about whether you should just leave things to market devices and the Government should not be involved in this space because of picking winners, et cetera?

Professor Valero: There has been quite a development in the way economists think about industrial strategy in recent years, internationally and in the UK. I do not think it is a substitute for getting the right horizontal conditions for growth and investment, so economy-wide policies, whether that is planning reform or pensions reform to get investment flowing. All those things are really important. The reason I think there is a role for more targeted vertical policies, which we might think of as industrial policy, is that Governments always have such policies, even when they do not have a proper strategy setting out how they make decisions around them.

Also, given that we have limited resources and policy attention, it is worth focusing that into the areas that are most likely to drive growth. The reality is that different sectors and technologies face different barriers to growth. Some of them might be more emerging sectors where, say, R&D grants are really important and there are decisions made all the time about what should be priority areas to fund. Others are financial services and professional and business services, which are major sources of growth in the UK. It is perhaps more about talent and the regulatory environment, and less about subsidy.

There is this important role if we want to get growth. We have a number of things we are really good at. A lot of the analysis we have done at the LSE, and the Government have also done it to inform their industrial policy, is to think, “Where do we have strengths, either now or potential strengths we can leverage? Where is global and domestic demand likely to grow?” There are a number of other considerations too. What is the regional distribution of the opportunities it would bring? Does it contribute to resilience?

That resilience point is really important in this day and age, because we are beyond being able to be completely agnostic about supply chains and where things come from. We cannot produce everything here in the UK, but there are certain arguments for having capabilities in some areas, or having strong international partnerships to make sure that we have secure supply chains.

The IS-8 are all areas where we have current or potential strengths. It is not all about propping up incumbents, which is the old-style industrial policy. This has been framed as a modern industrial strategy, which is about innovation—so disruption as well—business dynamism and supporting those nascent sectors that could be big growth areas of the future.

In doing so, the broad strategy has a number of cross-cutting policies. They are horizontal in many cases, but they might matter in particular for these sectors. If you take planning reform, that is obviously very important for net zero infrastructure. It is very important for lab space in places such as Cambridge for life sciences. There are these eight sector plans, and in each of those there is a much deeper look at what the evidence and the sector suggest the issues are. Also, what are the frontier technologies? It is not simply blanket support across everything.

Q23 Bobby Dean: I have one more question on that point. It sounds like you are saying that maybe the previous laissez-faire approach was a bit naive. It has left us exposed to particular risks in relation to resilience and did not allow us to grow our competitive advantage in key industries.

If that is about getting those companies to start scaling and thriving, how do you feel about mergers and acquisitions? It now feels like, quite often, UK businesses are very quickly no longer UK businesses. Do you think that more needs to be done? You could register the fact that a foreign company wants to buy one of our businesses as a good signal for the economy, because we are producing those businesses in the first place. When you look at the rate, is that a problem for the UK economy?

Professor Valero: Competitive markets are really important for innovation and growth. There has been a lot of work at the CMA in terms of thinking about, in innovative markets, how you make sure that competition policy keeps pace. In some areas, we often slightly lament some of the big companies that have simply been bought up and have not been our own big growth stories. Perhaps, given today’s world and the critical technologies or areas where we need sovereignty, there is a stronger case for thinking carefully about who ultimately controls certain technologies and assets. This is a big debate, obviously, in AI for us and many other economies as well.

Q24 Chris Coghlan: Dr Beck-Friis, if the UK entered another downturn, how much scope do you think the Government would have to borrow more without putting debt sustainability at risk?

Dr Beck-Friis: That is clearly the drawback of having high debts. We have seen this not only in the UK but in many other countries. You can see this in response to the ongoing middle east war. The fiscal response, not only in the UK but elsewhere, has been very limited compared with the response that we saw in 2022, or certainly during the pandemic. I do not think there is much fiscal room for the Government to run more expansionary fiscal policies.

The good news is that, even though there is not a lot of fiscal space, there is a lot of monetary policy space at the moment. In the next downturn, I would expect the Bank of England to have to do the heavy lifting in terms of running counter-cyclical policy.

Q25 Chris Coghlan: If the Chancellor was to take action now to try to have more fiscal space for a future downturn, is that potentially counterproductive because you could damage growth in the process?

Dr Beck-Friis: To the extent that the Government reduce the deficit when the sun is shining, that is obviously better to have. Going into a rainy day, you want to start with a lower deficit. To highlight the game between fiscal and monetary policy, to the extent that the Government tighten fiscal policy now, there is a lot of room for monetary policy to offset the negative drag that tight fiscal policy would imply.

Q26 Chris Coghlan: Lord O’Neill, you have argued that the Government should be bolder about investment-backed borrowing, perhaps breaking the fiscal rules. Could the Government credibly borrow more to invest in the current market conditions?

Lord O’Neill: I do not think that they should be breaking fiscal rules, as I think I heard you slip in at the end. No, I do not think they should.

However, the second rule is designed to have more ambition on public investment spending, which I definitely believe in, done with one very important addition that I am trying to use any opportunity I have to push for. We need an OBR for infrastructure, so that we can get out of some of the mad game that goes on in our wonderful democracy, where we do not ever seem to be able to deal with anything that is long and might cost a few shekels. What if we had a very publicly known and credible independent body that opined on project X’s multiplier effects? Not just the gilt market, but all markets are crying out for the UK to do something like that, so people understand that we are actually investable. You could definitely borrow plenty more in those circumstances, as opposed to, “Trust me. I know best”.

Q27 Chris Coghlan: You say infrastructure. Would you include R&D as well?

Lord O’Neill: Yes.

Q28 Chris Coghlan: Dr Beck-Friis, when you came, I think a year ago, I asked you a similar question. If I remember correctly, you thought that bond markets would not find it that credible if the Government were saying, “We are going to borrow to invest. Therefore give us a lower rate”. Is that still your view?

Dr Beck-Friis: To the extent that the Government choose to borrow more to invest, that would almost certainly lead to higher yields. That could also crowd out investment in other areas, because the UK in particular is really pushing at the limits of credibility. To reiterate, the bond market first and foremost cares about the headline deficit.

Of course, the composition of the deficit may matter. To the extent the Government borrow to invest, that may improve GDP growth, and that may be a positive for the fiscal trajectory as a result, but there is a lot of uncertainty around the multiplier and when that would show up in the GDP numbers. Until that has shown up, I think the bond market will discount it quite heavily.

Lord O’Neill: I emphasise that it requires some new credible independent body. If it is just “trust me”, our history tends to suggest, “No, I am not going to”.

Volatility in the UK gilt market is now realised and has been much higher than elsewhere.

We spoke about the level of yield and fiscal credibility. I think that this volatility partly reflects the notion around fiscal credibility in the UK, because, whenever you have an adverse shock—suppose energy prices go higher—the markets start doubting that the Government will do the right thing to balance the books. The reason I say that is that, while it has picked up everywhere, the three countries where it stands out have been the UK, France and Italy, and all these countries have some concerns around fiscal credibility. The nature of the shock has not been the UK Budget or fiscal policy, but more around the middle east and energy prices.
