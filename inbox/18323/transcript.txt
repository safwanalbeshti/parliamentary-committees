Examination of witness

Martin Jones CBE

The Chair: Welcome, everybody, to our session following up our investigation into electronic monitoring, or tagging as it is sometimes called. We are absolutely delighted to have Martin Jones here. I would be grateful if you would introduce yourself and your position.

Martin Jones: I am His Majesty’s Chief Inspector of Probation.

The Chair: Thank you very much and we will kick off the questions with Baroness Hughes.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Good morning, Mr Jones, and thank you for your time today. I have a question about probation service leadership on tagging but before I get to it, could you map out for us what the governance structure looks like with the various elements of HMPPS, MoJ and the Chief Probation Officer? What does that governance structure look like in relation to tagging in probation?

Martin Jones: Thank you very much indeed for inviting me to give evidence. I am delighted to be here.

The governance landscape is quite complicated. Electronic monitoring is a service provided to multiple customers. The tagging provider provides the day-to-day running, the equipment and capturing of information. Then you have people who that service is provided on behalf of and there are a number of interested parties. You have the courts, obviously, that from time to time impose requirements, whether on bail or as part of a court order. Then you have the interest of the prison probation service for the delivery of sentence and post-release licence conditions. The police are obviously also key partners in managing people on bail. Immigration is as well, because electronic monitoring is a key part of managing foreign nationals in the community.

When we did a report on this about five or six years ago, we criticised the governance arrangements. There have been some improvements in that and in particular there is better regional governance of electronic monitoring, meaning that there is a greater operational grip on the ground on what is happening. However, my overall view is that more needs to be done to tie it all together at the centre, within government, to ensure that all the customers within the system are aware of what the issues are and can grip performance concerns properly.

There is a team within the Ministry of Justice and HMPPS that manages the day-to-day running of the contract, but what is the line of sight at senior level within HMPPS and others? We made some recommendations on this in our report. I understand that the Government have accepted the thrust of that recommendation and are looking at new governance arrangements, chaired by the Second Permanent Secretary at the Ministry of Justice, to try to ensure that there is a cross-system view of the performance of the contracts. We think that is a key weakness at the moment.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Okay. Going back a bit, are the regional directors that our papers refer to multi-agency or is that just probation?

Martin Jones: That is very much probation-driven rather than across other agencies and there probably is an opportunity to look at how that ties in with local police chiefs.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: On your proposals, and thinking specifically about probation leadership, regarding both strategy and delivery, where does accountability lie? Is it fragmented equally, like the structure? Is there a single point of accountability?

Martin Jones: I think there is now. One thing I have absolutely welcomed in recent months is the appointment of a director-general of probation, bringing back a clear operational leadership line for probation. About 15 months ago, the inspectorate undertook a national inspection of the leadership of HMPPS and we criticised a lack of clarity and accountability around that line, which we thought was confused. It has now acted upon that, so there is a clear operational lead. Jim Barton was appointed this summer as the interim director general of probation. I think that provides a much clearer focus, so ultimately that would be up to Jim Barton and then to James McEwan as the chief executive of HMPPS.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: So he is in HMPPS. Where does the Chief Probation Officer sit?

Martin Jones: The Chief Probation Officer sits underneath the director- general, providing the operational professional leadership for the Probation Service overall, but probably with a less hands-on view of electronic monitoring. It is much more about leadership of the profession, whereas electronic monitoring is probably currently considered as a contracted service.

The Chair: I am sorry to interrupt. I am anxious to bring in Baroness Berridge to pick up some bits of this as well, so apologies to both colleagues. I am very unclear from what you have said. I understand about the greater clarity of leadership in the Probation Service generally, but tagging as such involves a wide range of other bodies. I am still unclear about this: in your view, if tagging is not doing what it is meant to do—and we have a problem about what it is meant to do, but leave that aside for a minute—who would we go to? Who has the ultimate accountability? Of course you can say the relevant Minister in this case, and we are meeting him tomorrow and we can ask him, but other than that, where is the real responsibility for this?

Martin Jones: That is exactly the criticism we made in the report: the lack of clarity and certainty on that. In strict accounting terms, it would be the Permanent Secretary of the Ministry of Justice. This is a Ministry of Justice contract, so value for money within that contract must sit under the Permanent Secretary. I think the actions that have been taken around strengthening the governance are in response to our recommendations about the lack of clarity on that. It is complicated because different limbs of police and immigration are also involved, as I said, so responsibility has to also involve the Home Office within its wider remit. Parliament ultimately wants a single accounting line and that has to be to the Permanent Secretary at the Ministry of Justice.

Baroness Berridge: Is there anything you want to add to your answers about leadership and what you have described when you get out into the regions?

Martin Jones: One comment I would make is that we spend a lot of time out in the field, inspecting probation services. My one very clear message since my appointment two and a half years ago has been about the level of pressure on probation resources on the ground. We have a massive staffing deficit. Last year the Public Accounts Committee said there was a 25% staffing gap within probation resourcing and that impacts the quality of work and the day-to-day practice of a probation officer, including on the management of electronic monitoring. When I meet with regional probation directors, I hear they are putting a lot of effort into covering many different sources of the pressure. What are they doing with a PDU[1] that is desperately understaffed and has high sickness levels? How are they joining up the strategies?

Ultimately, good probation practice is about ensuring that you are investing in your staff, giving them the training and support that they need and also things such as joining up within the local area: with the local police force on information sharing and joint projects, and with local services on drug treatment and homelessness, for example. There is a lot of work going on as part of that. Generally speaking, from my experience, the regional leadership is perhaps closest to that, but their resources are pulled in many different directions at any moment.

Baroness Berridge: You said we also now have the Home Office involved in this. Can you explain regional leadership when you have the Home Office involved as well? How does that work?

Martin Jones: Again, there is a general lack of clarity about how that works in relation to the contract. There is a project group, chaired by the Ministry of Justice, that pulls the interested parties together. That needs to be improved to ensure that everybody has a line of sight on how the contract is working, how reliable it is and how the data is working. However, my main scrutiny is on the Probation Service rather than how, for example, immigration fits in.

Baroness Berridge: What pilots and developments have you seen with regard to tagging?

Martin Jones: There are a number of interesting developments, which are promising if they could be developed at scale and we could rely on the nuts and bolts of how the tagging arrangements are working. For example, there has been a domestic abuse pilot to ensure that people are monitored and tracked. There has been an acquisitive crime pilot in conjunction with the police on people who may be at risk of committing acquisitive crime, which is quite often driven by drugs and alcohol. Both pilots are promising, but alongside that is the fact that there has been a very significant increase in the number of people being tagged under the Sentencing Act.

As chief inspector, my comment would be that we have had huge change across this market in the last five years, now compounded by the changes in the Sentencing Act. The service and the related arrangements are adjusting to that very significant uplift. We are now tagging more than twice the number of people who we were tagging five years ago. The service needs to settle somewhat and there needs to be a focus on getting the arrangements to work properly, rather than on any further expansion of electronic monitoring.

Baroness Berridge: Finally, you heard me ask previously about resilience in the system and, if we are talking about the victims of crime, public confidence. In your reports, have you looked at anything to do with the resilience overall of introducing a technology-based system and the risk that that brings?

Martin Jones: Certainly, there are risks in the system. I know you have mentioned cyber-hacking risks. The criminal justice system needs to test itself on the risks of some of those critical systems going down at any point. Given the scale of the numbers of people being monitored and the importance of monitoring exclusion zones and other things, if that system were not to work effectively, that would create a huge risk of things going wrong for the public and for victims. Resilience is one of the challenges that any new governance arrangements within the Ministry of Justice need to look at.

Baroness Berridge: Do you think what happens at that level when the system goes down is clear? You need clear governance when a crisis happens. Do you think the system of leadership and governance is clear enough? If you had that moment, whose responsibility would it be?

Martin Jones: In the past, when there have been problems with electronic monitoring arrangements, it has been the responsibility of the senior person within the Ministry of Justice talking to the contractors and others. It is not something we looked at when we undertook this inspection. My suspicion is that there is a need to look at it very hard to ensure it is resilient enough but I cannot comment, given that we did not inspect those arrangements.

Lord Moraes: We have just heard from Napo’s leadership about what probation staff feel about electronic monitoring. What do you think probation staff feel about electronic monitoring and how does it compare to the views of the courts and of the tag wearers?

Martin Jones: When we undertook this inspection, we spoke to probation staff, to members of the judiciary and to people who had been tagged to get their experience. It was interesting to compare and contrast.

Starting with the judiciary, the people who are imposing many of those orders, there is a general sympathy to the fact that this is a useful tool, along with a lack of awareness of how it is used in practice. We saw a real need to strengthen information flow to the courts about how it was being used, how for example it is being enforced, and to provide confidence that the arrangements are working at scale. One of the comments we have made about regional governance arrangements is about how the day-to-day liaison with the judiciary is going. I spent seven or eight years working in the Courts & Tribunals Service. The confidence of the courts is critical to making the system work.

As far as probation practitioners are concerned, I think there is a danger. For me, as the Chief Inspector of Probation, if you look at what changes someone’s life around and turns them away from reoffending, it will not be a tag on their ankle. It will be the work with a probation officer, understanding what risk they pose to the community and what help they may need to turn them away from it, whether through alcohol support, drug treatment or help with homelessness. The tag does not do very much to solve that and that was fed through in some of the responses that we got from probation staff.

Looking at it in totality, if electronic monitoring were working at the scale that we require it to, it has the potential to be a guardrail by keeping people on the path that we would want to keep them on. Keeping them out of exclusion zones, for example, is important. Another area in which we gained some good positive evidence is alcohol tagging. At the moment, probably about 17% of the total caseload is subject to alcohol tagging. There is something like a 97% compliance rate for people who have alcohol listed as a risk factor.

I was formerly chief executive of the Parole Board. We know that for some people who leave prison and may have a history of alcohol-related violence, the first thing they will do as they leave prison may be to pop into a public house and immediately they are at risk of behaviour that may lead to them breaching their licence conditions and being returned to custody. An alcohol tag, as a relatively binary thing—have you drunk alcohol or not?—provides an effective way to deter people from that behaviour.

When we spoke to probation staff about it, they sometimes seemed to be separating out what use the information could be in managing that offender. They were more focused on what is happening—"Is the tag on?”—but there is an opportunity to build a cultural point around it, informing their picture of the risk that individual poses. For offenders, there are some fundamental questions about their trust and confidence in the equipment. Quite often, they spoke to us about alerts going off at times. Communication with the providers was really quite important for them as well. We need to look at moving away from simply compliance with the tagging condition to looking also at how we are using the information because I think that could be powerful in the future.

Lord Moraes: It is difficult to compare the courts to the tag wearers and probation staff, but going back to that comparison, briefly do you think there is more confidence in the system among court personnel than among probation officers? If what we heard about the probation staff is correct, do they have the right training and resources to deal with the increase in EM usage?

Martin Jones: I certainly think there is a need to look at focused training and support for probation officers in understanding the information. Quite often, the information a probation practitioner will get about what is happening with an individual will be quite technical and they will need help interpreting it, so making that much easier for them would be helpful.

The courts probably have a veneer of an understanding of how the system is moving, but I think it needs a little more granularity on how it will be used in any particular case; for example, with bail conditions where you might have a vulnerable victim, maybe a victim of domestic violence. Electronic monitoring could be a useful condition attached to that but sometimes people do not look at that in the totality of the case.

Lord Hogan-Howe: Good morning and thank you. My question is about what we have all talked about already this morning, which is sharing data between the different parts of the system: probation of course, the police and courts. They have a joint ambition, which is to reduce recidivism and therefore reduce crime, but I do not hear it being described as a strategy where everybody in the system works together on that data. I take your point that having probation officers working with people will achieve better long-term goals but in the short term, preventing or detecting a crime might not be a bad idea either. Where is that strategy?

Martin Jones: There is scope for much greater clarity on that. As a minimum, electronic monitoring should provide deterrence for an offender who may decide that if they break the terms of their conditions that will first be detected, then interpreted and acted upon. If that is not happening, there is no deterrent at all. Somebody may be repeatedly in a breach and exclusion zone. If that is not addressed quite quickly, there is a risk that it can escalate into quite a dangerous situation. The domestic violence pilots I talked about are quite interesting in how to pull that information together much more successfully to get a rounded view of the picture.

I think there is scope, maybe using integrated offender management and other things, to pull it together much more successfully. I think there is a lack of strategy at the moment on how we manage risk in the community. The probation service is working with a caseload of nearly a quarter of a million people. Understanding that risk and sharing with the police is critical to ensuring that we keep the public safe. At times, that sort of commonality of purpose is not understood enough.

I was speaking about this at an event last week. Some of our systems are far too 20th century for where we are now. The system is incredibly reliant on police officers and probation officers emailing each other for information. That seems crazy in a world in which each of those parties has relevant information to help understand a risk that may move very dynamically and quickly in the community. How can we have a system that relies on one party emailing another? I would certainly describe the current electronic monitoring arrangements as incredibly clunky. The provider has information, they pass it to a probation officer who comes to a view on risk to licence compliance. If the risk moves to recall, the probation officer goes to the police to ask them to embark on a licence recall.

A few weeks ago I went to Turkey to look at how they use electronic monitoring in other jurisdictions. In some countries, they have a top cohort of people for whom there is a criminal justice system focused on the riskiest individuals and a very swift criminal justice system response. We cannot possibly justify that for 28,000 people, which is the current cohort, but could we justify it for the top 5%? I think that is something to look at for the highest-risk individuals to ensure the system is properly joined up, with people talking to each other and able to respond quickly where there are genuine concerns about the risk that an individual poses to the community.

Lord Hogan-Howe: I can imagine a room in each region where probation and police work to get all the data immediately and then they have teams that they might deploy immediately to do something about what they have just seen, not necessarily to arrest but to intervene in some way.

Martin Jones: That is the potential. Across Europe, there are at least two or three examples of similar things happening with the top cohort of people. That would be an impressive opportunity to provide considerable reassurance to the public and victims that things are acted on, but of course it would need to be resourced. With probation resources, as we have heard this morning, being significantly under where they should be and police resources pared back, that is a concern. But I can see that the investment would pay dividends in the potential for reduced harm to victims and reduced reoffending.

Lord Tope: How effective is tagging and how do you know?

Martin Jones: Our report in its totality demonstrates that there are breaks in the process at all stages. One of the most shocking findings in our report was that in only 55% of classic tagging cases was the tag fitted promptly to the individual. There is a huge missed opportunity with the 45% to whom the tag has not been fitted promptly, given the risk of what that individual might be up to in the intervening period. There needs to be a much greater focus on getting that tag fitted more promptly so we can do what the public and victims are expecting.

A secondary question, once the tag is fitted, is how information is flowing through the system so that the police officer and the probation officer can act when the information comes through. Again, that system is far too clunky. It takes too long for it to reach the individual who is managing the case.

Finally, there is the analysis of that information. We saw a powerful case when we undertook this inspection. A probation officer was looking at information gleaned from a live monitoring device and identified that an offender on probation was visiting a new address that they were not known to visit. It was found that the person had started a new relationship, which they had not disclosed to the probation officer, and there was risk of domestic abuse, which they acted upon. We need to see more examples of that.

My challenge to the service on this. At the moment, it is essentially relying on an individual probation officer being like Miss Marple in following the trail. What more could we do to identify patterns of behaviour, particularly using artificial intelligence to identify particular movements that might give rise to cause for concern? That could be a helpful way forward.

Then, once you have the information, what is the consequence? For me, alcohol monitoring is the classic: one of the reasons why there is a 97% compliance rate with alcohol tagging is that the offender knows that if they drink alcohol, it will be detected and that will be addressed by their probation officer, ensuring that we know what is going on in their lives. Very quickly, they know that if they try to go drinking, they will be caught and there will be a consequence attached. Unless that is happening at scale, there is a risk that people may think that compliance with tagging is voluntary.

I go back to what I said earlier. Ultimately, almost everybody who is stuck in the current criminal justice system is there because of problems with alcohol, drugs or homelessness. Where does tagging fit within that? It may encourage some behaviour but, at the moment, about one in seven of the people released from prison every year is released homeless. How can you practically tag each individual? That is a real issue within the overall efficacy of the system.

Baroness Buscombe: There is one group of individuals who you have not mentioned in your list. From responses to Written Questions to the Government, we know that some foreign nationals who are due to be deported at the end of their term are being released into the community when the Home Office has failed to prepare their deportation papers. Can you confirm that those people are being tagged and properly monitored, please?

Martin Jones: I can be certain that some of the people currently being monitored under the contract are on immigration bail. The focus of my inspection was primarily on the work of the probation and police services, so we did not look very hard at the question of immigration. I am more than happy to write to the committee, if that would be helpful, in relation to any information we gleaned about foreign national offenders. I know that it is encompassed in the overall contract.

The Chair: Thank you. If you could write to us as quickly as possible, we would be very grateful.

Lord Empey: Our previous witnesses were saying that the purpose of tagging is unclear and that there is a degree of uncertainty. Is it punishment? You used the word deterrence. Obviously, the protection of victims comes in, but what is it?

Martin Jones: It is very confused. Certainly, the most recent expansion of electronic monitoring seems to be about trying to provide public confidence in the early release scheme. Going right back to basics, why are we doing this? Alcohol monitoring is the easiest to understand. We know that for a certain percentage of people, alcohol is a problem that relates back to their offending behaviour. That is relatively binary. If electronic monitoring is being used for exclusion zones, perhaps to monitor someone who is leaving prison on the direction of the Parole Board, the protection of the exclusion zone is an important part.

The danger is that if you have an incredibly wide group of people being tagged at any moment, all you are collecting is intelligence on what they are up to day to day. They are not breaching anything; you can simply understand where they are. It is much more useful if you look at it on the basis of what the risk is. Is there a risk, for example, of acquisitive crime? You can monitor people’s patterns of behaviour. If it is alcohol, that is another thing. Is it around victim protection? I fear, with so many people being tagged, whether we can get the full picture of the riskiest behaviours and individuals in that mix.

Lord Empey: It just strikes me that if it is thousands of people—I think we heard that the number of tags has doubled in the last number of years—we do not know precisely what we are doing. Who is actually sitting watching and monitoring all of this? It is a massive undertaking, and you are telling us that 45% of the tags are not even put on properly.

Martin Jones: It is a real risk with the value for money for the overall system. The current electric monitoring system is costing the taxpayer more than £100 million a year. There is a balance to be struck. As Chief Inspector of Probation, I want to ensure there are more probation officers doing that very important job that we need them to do, ensuring that we can turn people’s lives around. Tagging may provide some benefits, but there is a value-for-money argument about where to invest the money.

Lord Tope: Can I come back on this? You said that 45% of tags were not fitted properly. In my ignorance, that seems an enormously high proportion, nearly half of them. That is a pretty basic problem. Why is that and how can it be improved dramatically?

Martin Jones: The data that we found was that 55% of tags were fitted promptly, following the requirements. Following release from prison, we would ordinarily say that the tag should be fitted within 24 to 48 hours and 45% were not. Looking further out, we found that 20% had not been fitted within a fortnight of a person’s requirement being imposed. Gradually we catch up, but that is still a significant risk.

One of the reasons for it is the set-up of the arrangements. The arrangements have probably not changed for about 30 years. Once a decision has been made that somebody should be tagged, they essentially rely on somebody going to the place where that person is residing, they fit the equipment at their home address and quite often, with some equipment, you need to install a radio frequency device or home monitoring unit, to ensure the person is there. Of course, the second you do that, you are creating lots of vans driving all over the place, trying to find people—are they where they should be at that moment?

I think there is an opportunity here. When I spoke about this internationally a month ago, many of the jurisdictions did not recognise the problem that I described. One reason for that is that they tag at the point of source. If a court imposes the order, the person has the tag imposed before they even leave the courtroom or at their first probation appointment. We need to move towards that system.

In fairness, the Ministry of Justice has introduced a pilot in six prisons where people are tagged at that point of source. Before they leave the prison, the tag is imposed. My understanding from speaking to officials is that they are looking to roll it out much more widely. It is clearly a wise thing to be doing. For many of these tags—the alcohol tag that I talk about, for example—you do not need a base station. You simply need to ensure the tag is fitted so you can measure whether the individual is drinking alcohol. Similarly, the GPS tracking device ultimately is talking to the satellite technology about where they are, with an immediate use in relation to understanding that.

When the inspector is asked to look at release arrangements, for example, we look at the operation of approved premises, where the highest-risk individuals live immediately after leaving custody. A percentage of people who leave the prison never get to the doors of their approved premises. If they had a device on their ankle as they left prison, you would have much greater certainty about where they were. Quite often, we find they have got waylaid on the trip. Sometimes they go into a public house. They might immediately be in breach of their licence conditions, but at least it would be helpful to know where they are rather than perhaps, as happens far too frequently at the moment, having to call the police and recall that individual to custody within 24 to 48 hours of their release. Of course, that is thwarting the whole point of the release scheme.

Baroness Berridge: We have been told that since March 2026, Serco is meeting the contractual requirements. You have just outlined a load of figures saying, basically, that there are huge problems. Has there been a recent improvement or is it that the contractual requirements are not tight enough? I cannot marry the two together at the moment.

Martin Jones: I can probably help with that. It would certainly be true to say that compared to this time last year, there has been an improvement in the overall operation of the contract, but the KPIs in the contract require the contractor to make two attempts to tag an individual within 48 hours of the imposition of the order. It does not measure whether the device was successfully installed. That is why they may have complied with the KPI, because they have made two attempts, but actually in more than 45% of cases that visit has not succeeded. I think the contract needs to move on. Ultimately, what the public and victims need is certainty that an individual has been tagged so that we can act upon that. It goes back to what you measure in the contract.

Baroness Berridge: That is very helpful.

The Chair: I will bring in Lord Hogan-Howe in a minute. First, though, so we can get it clearly on the record, what you said earlier about tags not being fitted correctly or properly, some people might have thought that the actual fitting was not done correctly. Is what you are referring to the contract specification and not fitted within the timescales?

Martin Jones: You are absolutely right. What I am talking about with those figures is whether the tag has been fitted within a reasonably prompt period and if it has not—

The Chair: If you were to change the contract specification, would it not be difficult to say that the tag must be fitted within a particular time, given the examples you have used of people who simply cannot be found because they are homeless? Sadly, a very large number of people currently leave prison immediately into homelessness, for example.

Martin Jones: That is absolutely right. It is a key part of the release planning by the Probation Service to understand that. A huge amount of the activity in recent weeks and months has been the Probation Service understanding where an individual is going as they leave custody. If you have not sorted that, it makes tagging close to impossible. In many of those cases—

The Chair: Can I be absolutely clear? In the trials that are taking place, are the relevant probation staff present at the time the tag is fitted in the prison, before release, so that we have a detailed release plan agreed with the Probation Service?

Martin Jones: In the pilots that have been taking place, my understanding is that in almost all cases the probation officer would not have been present at the point when that individual is released. Generally, prison staff are supervising the release and the tag being fitted. But—

The Chair: Sorry, but that is also my understanding. As the chief inspector, would you advise that the trial should have, and in future should, include, representation of the Probation Service being present at the time and linking with the Prison Service about the release plan for each prisoner, including them being tagged and explaining the purpose of the tag and the implication of breaching the licence conditions that accompany it?

Martin Jones: As chief inspector, I do not think that the probation officer needs to be physically present at the gates of the prison when that individual is released, but in every case, there needs to have been pre-release planning and the probation officer working with the person who is about to be released, explaining the licence conditions that have been set and discussing with the prisoner offender management unit about how that will work practically on the day. That is critically important. In the system at the moment, far too little attention is focused on making successful releases. That is one reason why, for the last two quarters of published data, more people have been recalled to prison than were released.

The Chair: What is your view about Nacro’s experiment of the departure lounge, bringing a wide range of services together, including the tagging experience and everybody else, to look at the details and support that can be provided in housing et cetera?

Martin Jones: I am 100% supportive of that sort of initiative to ensure that release works as effectively as possible. Far too little attention at the moment is focused on making those releases succeed. The system should want that every individual who leaves custody has a clear plan to maximise the chances that they will be safely reintegrated into the community and not present a significant risk to the public. At the moment, some of those arrangements are not working as they should. Again, I have spoken to many charities that do really important work in helping to reintegrate people and providing mentoring services to them, but it is not being provided at the scale needed. If we had that, we would have better success upon release.

The Chair: Of course, we have the very tragic figure of the very large number of people who die very shortly after release.

Lord Hogan-Howe: I have one quick question. Can you inspect Serco or demand information from them?

Martin Jones: I have a very broad statutory remit of probation inspection services. Going back 10 years ago to when privatisation attempts were made, I had a role in inspecting those contracted services.

Lord Hogan-Howe: Today, can you go into their buildings and demand information from them?

Martin Jones: I am pretty confident that we have statutory inspection powers that would enable us to do that, certainly for what I describe as probation workload.

Lord Hogan-Howe: Would you mind confirming that, if possible? It would be helpful.

Martin Jones: Of course.

Lord Hogan-Howe: My reason for asking that question is that you indicated in your answer about the contract that they may be observing the wording of it but not the spirit of it. Are they rewarded financially for failing to fit a tag, because they are then financially rewarded for trying it again?

Martin Jones: I would have to look at the detail in the contract. My understanding is the way the contract works is that their financial remuneration would depend on meeting their KPIs within it. If the KPI does not incentivise them to focus on actually fitting the tag then—

Lord Hogan-Howe: They have had a history of creating invoices.

Martin Jones: Yes, potentially.

Lord Hogan-Howe: So you want to be reassured that, in fact, they were making genuine attempts?

Martin Jones: Absolutely.

Lord Hogan-Howe: If they have to do it four times, are they not paid any more?

Martin Jones: It is my understanding that they are providing the overall service. One reason why tagging at the prison would make a lot more sense at the moment is the price of petrol. You have vans driving all over London, all over the country, knocking on doors and attempting to tag people. It would be much smarter to go a single location, maybe a prison or a probation office, and tag five to 10 people there, rather than driving all around London when in half of those cases that individual is not there.

Baroness Bertin: Thank you for your very thorough and honest answers. I am completely shocked by the state of this situation and I want to bring it back to victims. How do probation staff work with victims in regard to electronic monitoring? A previous witness was talking about the false sense of confidence or safety that many victims may have in the whole electronic monitoring process. I want to get your thoughts on whether it is being completely mis-sold to victims, and indeed the public.

Martin Jones: The conclusion of our report was about the danger that a false assurance is being provided to victims and the public that the system is working much better than it currently is. There is a huge risk of victims not fully understanding the implications of a system in which only 55% of tags are fitted promptly.

I am also clear that if you look at data within the criminal justice system, deterrence is really important. Of course, if somebody decides that they are going to commit a crime, it sits with the individual, but them knowing that there is a chance of being detected is incredibly important. You can look back over serious further offences that have taken place within the criminal justice system. Sometimes, a break in the process increases the risk that somebody makes a poor choice in the intervening period. Maybe somebody could breach an exclusion zone or be knocking on the door of an ex-partner, because, in essence, there is not the deterrent effect there.

The Probation Service’s work with victims is the responsibility of victim contact liaison officers who, at the moment, do the day-to-day liaison with victims to understand what their concerns are and what conditions should be set around release. Clearly, things such as exclusion zones will be relevant in a high number of those cases. We currently have an inspection under way, which I am expecting to publish before Christmas, about how those arrangements have worked, particularly the early release scheme. I have seen the public concern about that and I want to assure myself, the public and victims that it is working as effectively as possible.

My one observation at this point, ahead of those inspection findings coming out, is that it would be true to say that the victim liaison officers have very high caseloads. They are supervising and working with potentially hundreds of victims and providing that ongoing discussion. Generally speaking, our inspection work shows that they are doing their very best to provide clear information to victims, but clearly, where you have a scheme being developed at pace, there is a risk that things fall through the gaps and victims do not get the clearest possible information at the earliest point. I have spoken to the Victims’ Commissioner about this: I am certainly urging that the Government do more to support victims on the release of offenders, particularly those who have been convicted of the most serious offences.

Baroness Bertin: I agree with all of that. According to your report, I think only 34% of victims in cases have some kind of liaison with the Probation Service. I do not know if I have misread that. Could I ask particularly about domestic abuse victims? Clearly, they are probably at the highest risk. To be clear, as it stands at the moment, if a perpetrator decides that they are going to break that exclusion zone at 10 pm, let us say, which is most likely, that information will only be got at when the probation officer or whoever it is gets to their desk at 9 am the next morning. There is no alert bell going off to say, "This guy has breached their order".

Martin Jones: The system is incredibly reactive, rather than proactive. The reality is that if somebody breaches the terms of their order, if they breach the exclusion zone, the most likely outcome is a 999 call to the police from that victim, rather than the probation officer being able to react in real time. That is one of the reasons why I contrasted it with some of the work that is done in other jurisdictions. Clearly, you could not justify it for all cases—I have talked about 28,000 being tagged—but if you have a number of highly risky individuals, particularly in the early weeks after their release from custody, it should be possible to do something much more reactive. We could have probation officers sitting with police officers to make real-time decisions on what is happening in those cases, but it can be justified only for a small percentage. I would certainly encourage a pilot along those lines.

Baroness Bertin: On my last point, you mentioned a pilot on monitoring tracking of domestic abuse. I think you said there was a pilot already going. Is that pilot therefore not going to be as you just described, whereby it was much quicker if there was a breach? What is the point of a pilot if it does not uptick, to the point that you made?

Martin Jones: My understanding of the pilot—I am more than happy to send the process evaluation by the Ministry of Justice of that pilot—is that it does not involve what I would describe as real-time live monitoring by the police and probation to give a reaction. It is much more around monitoring behaviour over a longer period. There is certainly scope to look at high-risk domestic abuse cases. Could we develop these things much more nimbly?

Baroness Bertin: My final point is that getting the tags on in court, as you said, would be a good idea. Do you agree that domestic abuse perpetrators should be the first in line for that kind of provision?

Martin Jones: Clearly, if there is a concern around somebody facing a real threat of violence—intimidation or stalking, for example, as part of that—they need to be top of your risk assessment. Provided the risk merits that, I certainly think they have got to be near the top.

Lord Bach: Can I ask a simple question, and probably a stupid one? Explain to me how the pilot that Baroness Bertin was asking you about differs from what would normally happen. What does the pilot do? What is its intention? Why is it special for domestic abuse?

Martin Jones: On the domestic abuse one, I am happy to circulate to the committee a copy of the report. It was a Home Office pilot, as I understand it, that was enabled as part of that. It is about ensuring that there is much closer monitoring and setting of conditions around monitoring. I am more than happy to write with further details, if it is helpful.

Lord Bach: That would be very helpful, please.

The Chair: Just before we move on, we heard from Napo earlier, and you talked of concerns about how the scheme is working. Those witnesses suggested that they were very much in favour of bringing the whole process in-house. What is your view?

Martin Jones: I have to be careful in my role as chief inspector. It is my job to inspect the system. It is not working as effectively as it could at present, and that needs to be the focus of the system: to get it working much more effectively to ensure you are making the best use of that public money. When you look at the performance within the system, I think all parties have room for improvement. One thing that certainly comes through as part of our inspection is the difficulty for the contractor if you do not have an accurate address for where an individual will be living, which again links back to the release planning arrangements. Certainly, there is scope for improvement.

The Chair: I appreciate that you are in a difficult position. Nevertheless, I will press you, because of all the people who are likely to have a view that would be helpful to this committee, and ultimately to government decision-making, it is you, in terms of the best way of packaging this together. So again, do you have a view on whether there would be merit in bringing the whole operation in-house?

Martin Jones: My wariness, based on the experience of the last 10 years of chaos within the Probation Service as a result of repeated reorganisations, is that if it was done immediately, it would be very difficult. But if the Ministry looked at that within the next two to three years, there would be merits in looking at what the benefits of such a change might be.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: When the Government announced £100 million in March of this year, they said it was for the biggest expansion of tagging in British history. They seem to have had in mind things like a presumption that all prison leavers would be tagged on release, the national rollout of the domestic abuse pilot that you discussed with Baroness Bertin, and the national rollout of the pilot to tag convicted burglars and thieves, so their movements can be tracked against unsolved crimes. I think you said both those pilots were going quite well.

At the same time, you have told us that there needs to be a focus on things working properly and, obviously, they are not working properly at all. Could you say something about this £100 million? First, is it real money? Is it new money? Secondly, is it realistic to pursue these expansions at a time when the basic job simply is not being done?

Martin Jones: Is it real money? I know for certain, because of the way the contract works, that if you tag more people the contractor will, as a result, receive more payment under the contract. The Government are certainly spending more money under the electronic monitoring arrangements as a result of the changes they have made.

As I said earlier in this evidence, last year the Public Accounts Committee concluded that there was 25% understaffing in the Probation Service. What is the long-term strategy to ensure that we have enough probation officers, and probation staff in totality, to do that really important job as well as possible? When I was in Turkey recently at an international conference, an international speaker told me that 15 to 20 years ago, we had one of the best probation services in the world in England and Wales. It feels like we are a way away from that at present, with a service that has far too few staff but also a huge dearth of experience.

While I can understand the reasons why the Government have expanded tagging, the bottom line is that they need to focus on having more probation officers doing that very important job of helping turn people’s lives around and giving them a chance to succeed. All that will happen otherwise is that electronic monitoring will create a set of tripwires that inevitably trip people up, and lots of expense for the system but very few of the benefits. By focusing on what is overall value for money, ultimately I would much rather have more probation officers.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: On the question of officer numbers, you heard the evidence that we just had from Napo. Those witnesses were asked about the intention to recruit 1,300 extra probation officers by March of next year. Of course, they welcomed that but said something which surprised me. I may be very naïve, but they said it is not a net figure. That 1,300 will be outweighed by the number of probation officers leaving the service. Is that correct, in your understanding?

Martin Jones: Yes. About 18 months ago now, the inspectorate published an important report on recruitment, retention and training. I would describe it at the moment as being like a bucket; we are running the tap really high to fill the number of probation officers, but there is a leak in the bottom of that bucket and we are losing a significant number of probation officers from the service.

From the most recent published numbers, my understanding is that there has been an increase in the overall number of probation staff by about 1,000 over the last year, but however fast you recruit, people are leaving the service. Some of that is about the pressure of the job. If you are in a probation office and carrying maybe 30% more cases than you should be handling, alongside the pressures of doing that job, unfortunately many people will leave the service as a result. You need to correct that balance.

The Chair: Before I allow Lord Anderson to continue, can we confirm one fairly obvious point, just so it is on the record? As we lose staff and bring new staff in—and we can debate whether one is bigger than the other and so on—is it not also true that the situation means that the Probation Service is increasingly filled with people with very limited practical experience?

Martin Jones: Yes. I have been saying for some time now that we have a service with too few staff with too little experience managing too many cases. The probation officer who leaves, having been a probation officer for five or 10 years, is being replaced by a newly qualified individual who has been through the training. But what they lack is the experience of working with colleagues and people on probation over a series of years, and knowing to follow your nose with behaviour that may worry you. The need to rebuild that experience is one of the key problems at the moment.

The Chair: Can you confirm that you have said that, and your predecessors have said something similar, over many years?

Martin Jones: Absolutely right. Certainly, the previous chief inspector’s views are entirely in line with mine.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: As a last question from me, going back to the £100 million, tell me if I am correct in the way I have understood what you say. This is not a pot of £100 million that could be used in a lot of different ways. It could be used to improve pay and conditions. It could be used, perhaps, to expand the pilot enabling people to be tagged at the place of custody. What you are really saying is that it is there as a pot that can be drawn on when more people are tagged and that more fees are therefore paid to the person doing the tagging. That is why it is about the expansion of tagging. If that expansion of tagging does not take place, will the £100 million not be there at all or will it go back into the £700 million?

Martin Jones: It must come within the overall ambit of the money that the Ministry of Justice has set aside for tagging. Given the early release arrangements, the Ministry of Justice has a pretty clear line of sight. So far, about 2,000 people have been released from custody under that early release scheme. The majority of those individuals would have been tagged at an earlier point than before, given the fact there is now a presumption. My view is it is almost inevitable that the number of people tagged will increase over the next two years or so, as that early release scheme comes in, so we will be spending more money under that contract.

Another thing that I would certainly applaud is that efforts are being made within the Ministry to ensure that the Probation Service has better tools to help make the impossible job of a probation officer easier. One of the things that a lot of people have spoken about is the new tool, Justice Transcribe. A probation officer, two years ago, was sitting in their office meeting with a person on probation, writing notes down, and then probably spending another couple of hours writing that up and putting it on the computer. The Justice Transcribe tool enables them to hold a meeting, focus on having that conversation with a person and then produce a crisp summary of that meeting on the computer. That is saving, according to the analysis, hundreds of thousands of hours of probation officer time, which enables them to do the job better. That is great and we just need more of that sort of thing. Smarter tools, that sort of thing, is a wise investment of taxpayers’ money.

Lord Anderson of Ipswich: That is a pretty basic and commercially available form of AI.

Martin Jones: Yes, and we developed it very quickly. Certainly, all the probation staff who I have spoken to have really applauded that as a development. They say it has been one of the best developments in probation for the last 10 to 15 years.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: Just a quick follow-up, please. Have you or has anybody done any profiling as to how far that £100 million will enable further probation resources to be deployed? If the first call on that money is the additional payments to the contractor for the additional people coming on to a tag, I am not clear whether we know how much of that will be left to feed the other parts of the system, in particular the probation resources.

Martin Jones: We have not done that analysis. I am more than happy to take that away and write to the committee with some analysis if that would be helpful. My instinct, based on what we found in this inspection, is that far too little attention has been on the staffing resources and processes needed to ensure electronic monitoring works properly. That includes the staff on the ground, giving them sufficient time so that they can analyse the information, the impact on the police of electronic monitoring on bail. Ultimately, if you are simply collecting lots of information about lots of people, but do not then have the stuff and the people to act upon that information, you are thwarting the whole point of the scheme. Certainly, attention needs to take place around that.

Baroness Hughes of Stretford: If you can do anything fairly quickly, we would appreciate it, I am sure, as a committee. Thank you very much.

Baroness Berridge: On the £100 million, I realise that you inspect probation services, but as of March this year we have a second Permanent Secretary in MoJ, which is a small department if you look at the headcount. Do you know whether the £100 million includes the costs of the MoJ expansion with a second Permanent Secretary and staff there?

Martin Jones: I do not know for certain about that, but I would be surprised if it was included within that ambit. I certainly hope, based on the way that I expect the estimates to be done, that it should be focused on the cost of electronic monitoring rather than paying for senior posts in the Ministry of Justice. My suspicion is the case that was made for additional senior Permanent Secretary resource at the Ministry of Justice is related to the demands of the department, which is under huge pressure given the prison population, among other things. It would surprise me if that was part of the estimates.

The Chair: But is it not also the case that the £100 million is not actually new money anyway? It is part of the £700 million that was announced quite some time ago, which is meant to cover a whole range of things, including, for example, helping people out of homelessness when they leave prison.

Martin Jones: Certainly, my understanding is that the £100 million is part of the £700 million.

The Chair: Yes. I think the department has confirmed that it is not new money, as perhaps some people might have thought it was.

Lord Bach: This will be a surprise question, because it was not intended to ask you this, but I would like to know which issues you anticipate might arise from the Courts and Tribunals Bill that is going through the House of Lords at present. Have you had a chance to give any thought to what consequences that might have for the Probation Service?

Martin Jones: The key question for me, as somebody who has worked in the system for some time, is that the Probation Service plays an absolutely critical role in advising the court on sentences: what is the right sentence to ensure that we meet this person’s need, particularly on community sentences, for example, as part of that. We have a thematic inspection under way at the moment looking at the quality of that work.

One of the balances that needs to be struck and perhaps has been lost over time is that, if you go back 15 years, the majority of reports prepared for the courts were really detailed, written by spending a lot of time with that offender to understand their lives. Now there is a focus on very quick stand-down reports produced on the day. The worry about that is, if you do not get the balance right, you miss lots of information.

A final concern that I have about the system is that a few years ago the inspectorate published a report on the Damien Bendall case—a really terrible, tragic set of serious further offences. One of the reasons that case went so horribly wrong was the lack of checks on the suitability of addresses as that person was leaving court. If a court is sentencing somebody, focusing on the full picture of the risk that individual poses and the needs they have is critically important.

The probation service needs to ensure that the resources are in the right place as a result of the Courts and Tribunals Bill to ensure it can meet the demands of the courts and provide them with accurate, timely information at the earliest possible point. We saw some really good practice when we inspected the Kent Probation Service, which was doing much better at trying to get ahead of this.

Many of the customers in the magistrates’ court, in particular, are repeat customers of the system; they have been there before. Trying to collect that information to ensure that the probation office does not start afresh when that person appears, getting information from the police to ensure that you know about this person’s previous convictions and their needs so you can use previous reports, is a good, sensible way forward. They are also trying to link that up with other systems, including the DWP’s, to ensure you get a complete picture of an individual. That would be much wiser to try to ensure you have the maximum possible insight into an individual before they appear at court.

The Chair: Just to pick that up, you advise that the pre-sentence report should be much more detailed and, of course, we would agree. Is it not the case that in a very large number of cases now, there is no pre-sentence report simply because of probation staff shortages?

Martin Jones: Again, I think there is MoJ-published data on how many cases reports are produced for. Generally speaking, reports are focused on the busy sentencing courts, but there is a risk sometimes that if the probation resources are not there, the court will sentence in their absence.

The Chair: The crucial thing on tagging is that, presumably, if we had more detailed pre-sentence reports that had proper assessment procedures—we know that they are changing the assessment procedures now—the sentence that would be imposed may well mean a non-custodial sentence with various licence conditions, so that, in turn, would help the problem of overcrowding in prisons.

Martin Jones: I am certain that is right. The more insight the Probation Service can provide on the problems that an individual faces will give the courts community options that they may have confidence in about what will give it the best chance of reducing reoffending. I think an investment in those court resources is really important, and the Government need to look at that as they start to rebuild the Probation Service to where it needs to be.

Martin Jones: I will be holding a meeting within the next 10 days with senior officials in HMPPS so that we can talk through their action plan that they are intending to act upon, and I will be following that up within a year. Martin Jones: I would say, in fairness, that there has been progress on a number of the reports that I have published in the last two and a half years, since my appointment.
