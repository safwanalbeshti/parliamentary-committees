Public Administration and Constitutional Affairs Committee

Oral evidence: Pre-appointment Hearing: Independent Reviewer of Public Sector Fraud Authority, HC 577

Tuesday 24 November 2026

Ordered by the House of Commons to be published on 24 November 2026.

Watch the meeting

Members present: Simon Hoare (Chair); Richard Baker; Markus Campbell-Savours; Charlotte Cane; Peter Lamb; Luke Taylor.

Questions 1-50

Witnesses

I: Richard Las, Government’s preferred candidate for the Independent Reviewer of Public Sector Fraud Authority.

Examination of witness

Witness: Richard Las.

Chair: Good morning, colleagues, and good morning to Mr Las—not our witness, but our candidate. You are very welcome. Congratulations, if that is the right word, on getting this far in the process to become the independent reviewer of the Public Sector Fraud Authority. You are now facing the Becher’s Brook of scrutiny, as it were: if you can get over this, our recommendation will be that the Government appoint you.

My first question may be the most obvious one, but it is quite useful to help us set the scene for our understanding of your position. How do you define fraud?

Richard Las: I guess that in this context fraud is mainly about illegal and inappropriate financial gain. I think it takes many forms. From my experience in the past, it goes from opportunistic fraud, and there is also fraud relating to organised criminality. Opportunistic is when somebody just has the opportunity to do it. Often the amounts are not large, but they take advantage because of weaknesses in the system. We see a lot of organised fraud, particularly in sectors where people are getting contracts or are stealing tax or other forms of Government reliefs or benefits in an organised way.

Another area, broadly speaking, is what I would regard as systemic: where the systems do not work very well, and that gives rise to people committing fraud. That often happens where you see crossovers between different agencies and Government Departments that do not talk to each other as well as they could, or where the system is not built as well as it could be.

Lastly, there is the corruption side of things. Again, that is generally for financial gain, although there is not always money changing hands. It might be about positions and the ability to get contracts—those sorts of things. There is a whole range of fraud.

Chair: Do you think there is an element of planning and premeditation that sits behind all of it, or is some of it spontaneous and then mined?

Richard Las: Even the opportunistic has to have an element of intent. It is not an error or a mistake; it is a deliberate action. Even if it is spontaneous, it is seeing an opportunity and taking that opportunity, but knowing that it is wrong.

Chair: And those who cover up for people who are taking: would they also be covered by your definition?

Richard Las: They would be. That would start to get into conspiracy. Absolutely, yes.

Chair: The Mrs Merton question to Debbie McGee: what first attracted you to the role of independent reviewer of the Public Sector Fraud Authority?

Richard Las: I think there are probably three elements to it, for me: the scope of it, the challenge and an opportunity. The scope interests me tremendously. I have worked for a number of years in HMRC, which is a very complex organisation, looking at all sorts of fraud. We might touch on those as we go through today. I was looking across the public sector and at the types of fraud that happen there. Given my experience of those touching points, I felt that it was a really interesting role.

The challenge is partly because of the scope, but it is also because we are talking about a new role—if successful, I would be the first in this role—and a new service that is just starting up and has a journey to go on. That very much attracted me. I have built investigation services in the past; I have experience of that and I find it interesting.

Finally, this is sometimes the cheesiest bit, but it is about the opportunity. I have spent all my career in public service and am very passionate about trying to make a difference and trying to reduce fraud in particular. That has been my business for a number of years. This felt like a really good opportunity to get in on the ground floor and influence that landscape.

Chair: Pretend I am a 12-year-old and that you are speaking at a school event. If I asked you, as the reviewer, “What’s your role and what do you do?”, how would you answer that question?

Richard Las: As an independent reviewer? I guess there are a couple of sides to it. It is about ensuring that the enforcement service exercises its functions, principally, in keeping with the legislation. That sounds a bit dry, but it is about making sure that all the processes are working correctly and that powers are being used appropriately.

But it is broader than that. It starts to look at when those powers are used and whether they are used in the most effective and impactful way. I have often talked to people about how you can conduct an investigation that might be perfect in how it has followed the processes—but has it made any difference in the end? Has it had an impact on fraud? Has it had an impact more broadly than for that individual? Does it start to look further than that?

I think that would be my role: to help to look at that system and to advise and make recommendations on how we can ensure that investigations, recoveries and enforcement activity are conducted appropriately and effectively and that the system works.

Charlotte Cane: Good morning, Mr Las. In your written answers, you indicated that you do not have any financial or other interests, roles or commitments that could give rise even to the perception of a conflict of interest or bring into question your political impartiality. Can you confirm that?

Richard Las: I can, yes.

Charlotte Cane: Are there any other things about your previous conduct—past incidents or episodes—that, if they did come to light, would give grounds to suggest that your appointment may not have been appropriate?

Richard Las: I am not aware of any, no.

Charlotte Cane: How did you self-audit to come to that conclusion?

Richard Las: I don’t have any incidents. I have had a role for many years that has been subject to quite significant scrutiny, with a high level of clearance, in terms of my suitability, and access to material. I feel I have conducted that appropriately and have been trusted to do that over the years.

I have also had a public life, speaking at events and in interviews over the years, especially in my more recent roles as head of the fraud investigation service. I feel that they have gone all right; I do not feel as if I have been in a position where my judgment or my suitability for those roles has been questioned in that respect.

Richard Baker: Good morning, Mr Las. Until recently, you were the director and chief investigation officer of the HMRC fraud investigation service. You indicated in your written answers that you think this makes you well placed to carry out this role. Could you expand on why you think that experience would be particularly helpful?

Richard Las: Of course. It is an experience of running a very large investigation service. There are some 5,000 people in the organisation, in lots of different professions; many of them are criminal investigators, but they conduct all sorts of investigations that I think are relevant to the enforcement service in the PSFA. As well as criminal investigations, there will be civil investigations. There are penalties involved in financial recovery work. There is investigation of sanctions, which is very different from tax. I was also the supervisor for money service businesses—again, a regulatory role, but quite different, with a lot of experience.

Some of the other background and experience I have is around training, for example. We had our own training academy in HMRC specifically for fraud. I oversaw that training activity and its development and understand how important that is to running an organisation. It was multi-professional. I think there is a lot of relevant experience when you look at the work of the enforcement service that maps straight across.

As I tried to put in my note, I have a sense of what works well. Probably just as importantly, I have a sense of what does not work well, and I can bring that with me. It does not mean I am wedded to doing it one way—the world changes constantly—but I have a lot of experience that will help me make those judgments.

Finally, I have been subject to inspections for many, many years from a number of organisations; I will not list them all. I have a sense of what a good recommendation is and what might not be such a good recommendation. A good recommendation is not always something that just agrees with my particular view on the world. It is about adding value and having an open dialogue. I feel I have a lot of good, relevant experience that will help.

Richard Baker: Having spent your career entirely within the civil service, do you think you have a sufficiently independent perspective to carry out the role of independent reviewer?

Richard Las: I do. HMRC is a peculiar Department. It is a non-ministerial Government Department, and the commissioners for HMRC are responsible for running the organisation. I am used to and expect to have a fair degree—in fact, a full degree—of operational independence without recourse to having to be directed by Ministers. That is set out in the legislation for HMRC. I feel I have that sense within me anyway about the importance of being independent.

Of course, I provided policy advice to Ministers in the past and would give my professional opinion in that sense, including the pros and cons of a course of action. Partly, it is about what attracted me to this role. I want to add value. I have had a lengthy career and feel I have achieved an awful lot. My personal view is that you do not add value by agreeing with your boss constantly; you add value by giving your professional judgment and opinion and coming up with recommendations that are well founded.

Chair: Can I just say to members of the Committee: don’t take a lead on that approach. [Laughter.]

Richard Baker: Thank you, Mr Las. That is very helpful indeed.

Chair: Could you give us your perception of the scale of fraud in the public sector, and then align that with the resource and teeth that the position has?

Richard Las: In terms of the scale, I know that the National Audit Office has brought together some of the various estimates that are out there, and I think most recently it said that it was somewhere between £55 billion and £81 billion. The vast bulk of that, according to its estimates, is tax and social security. The estimates for the rest of the public sector, which essentially would be within the scope of this area, were between £3 billion and £28 billion.

First, I would say that that is quite a range, and secondly, it is quite a low number at the bottom and a high number at the top. My personal view—which is just a judgment based on the fact that some of those estimates are based on detected fraud, as opposed to understanding the whole system—would be that it is probably a bit higher than that. I do not know how much, but it is in the many, many billions and possibly underestimates that.

That leads on to the question about teeth and how we address that. There are an awful lot of tools available, including ones that have been granted recently, across the whole of the public sector. Generally speaking, I do not think we are lacking in teeth. It is about how we use them, how we co-ordinate them and how we make sure we get the best impact from them.

Chair: How would you achieve that?

Richard Las: It is important to understand what is behind the fraud and what kind of fraud it is. Fraud will often pick on a vulnerability in our system. We then have to look back through the lens of what the fraud and the vulnerability were in how that is operated. The vulnerability might be financial, and you can chase the finances and get those quickly, but it might be that your regulatory action could quickly tighten a loophole; we could say that we need to change the legislation or the framework; or we conduct some investigations. Each one will have its own bespoke response, but whatever you do, it has to be coherent, logical and targeted at a problem. It is not a sausage machine—it is not, “We’ll just do another investigation. We will use this power.” It is about how we use it and how we convince ourselves that it is giving us the best outcome.

Chair: Is there merit in taking the approach that it is useful to provide equal weight and attention not only to addressing the result, i.e. the fraud, but to identifying and driving through speedily—to urge the speedy driving through of—the closing of loopholes and so on, which therefore mitigate against the opportunity for that particular fraud, rather than just the results of it?

Richard Las: Absolutely. You are not leading me; that is absolutely what I believe. There is a saying: you cannot investigate yourself out of fraud. Eventually, you have to think about what the causes are and you have to look at what you can do to prevent the fraud from happening in the first place, having early detection systems that allow you to do that. You also need a feedback loop—you need to speak to the policy makers and other people. You have to have that end-to-end approach. If you look at just one part of the jigsaw, it does not give you enough.

Chair: As a Committee, we are interested in public administration, in that very broad term, but would you see yourself providing Parliament or this Committee with a report of where you have identified loopholes in processes, in systems and so on, and where you are concerned about any tardiness in closing them?

Richard Las: I have not got that far in my thinking, if I am honest. If we look at the detail of the role, it is quite focused on the exercise of some of those functions. I feel it is important, though, to have recommendations about the whole system. It could be that that is a conversation to be had with the Public Sector Fraud Authority, to ask whether they are doing that; but if I made the judgment that there was not that approach, or it was lacking in some way, this would be a place where I could say, “Actually, it needs to join up more”, or, “We need to look at the whole-system approach.”

Peter Lamb: The PSFA is intended to look at fraud across a wide range of areas. What challenges might there be in trying to pursue that across the entirety of the public sector?

Richard Las: Yes. That is a really good question, and certainly one that I have thought about as well. It is a huge scope, with a lot of stakeholder relationships in that ecosystem and a whole load of challenges. One challenge would be, just looking at the work and the fraud, how do you decide where you would have the biggest impact and where you put your resources? That is a really big question, because you could easily be distracted into lower-value or less-impactful areas when a raging fire lies somewhere else, so you need to understand that position.

You are also building a service—a relatively small one, in terms of personnel—and there is a whole piece in there about training people, retaining people and the relationships with other organisations. How do you get the right capabilities when you are quite a small organisation? You probably cannot go about it all yourself, so you need to think about relationships you might have with other organisations that allow you to have a capability, whether that is technological or a particular profession. It is quite a complex ecosystem. I do not think that we can expect the enforcement arm of the PSFA to conduct all investigations of all fraud across the public sector—that is not the intention—but nevertheless there is an intention that it add value, and add value to the best way. Those are some of the tensions that I think will exist.

Peter Lamb: What would you prioritise in that situation?

Richard Las: I think you have to look at the detail.

Peter Lamb: So you can’t tell us before you get your feet under the desk?

Richard Las: It is quite difficult in some respects. I can talk in generality about what I have done in the past. Typically, I would be looking at creating what I would call a balanced portfolio of work, where you understand what the fraud is and you try to attack it from a number of different directions. Some of that could be about investigations, enforcement action and recovery work, while some of it could be about tweaks to legislation and joining up the dots. You want to avoid effort being dissipated or scattered off in different directions. You are looking for a co-ordinated and targeted approach that gives you a chance to shift the dial. Lots of people commit fraud, unfortunately, and shifting populations from fraudulent behaviour to non-fraudulent behaviour takes time and energy. You cannot investigate all of it. Sometimes, you have to change people’s perceptions, or their reality, that the system has moved, if that makes sense.

Peter Lamb: How would you manage performance in that system? Would you use KPIs or other measures?

Richard Las: You can use KPIs and measures—there are metrics. Obviously, you can look at numbers of things happening—the numbers of times that you use particular powers or the value of particular investigations. You can track that over time, and it is important to have those kinds of metrics in there. For me, drawing on my experience, it is as important to understand what problems you are facing off. If you have a hierarchy of fraud problems from the worst to the least worst, are you making sure that you are putting your resources to the ones that are potentially the worst and those where you have a chance of having the biggest impact? That is about prioritisation and fitting with your—if this is not too grand a phrase—strategic objectives. We talked a little about estimates for fraud. Ultimately, over time, you want to see some kind of trajectory where the levels of fraud are being pushed down and reduced. You will never get them to zero, but you can certainly push them down.

Peter Lamb: The PSFA is moving from the Cabinet Office to DWP. Is there a risk that you end up becoming more focused on the home issues of the DWP than on the wider sets of fraud risk in the public sector?

Richard Las: My understanding from legislation is that there probably is not. The work of the DWP is specifically excluded from the work of the Public Sector Fraud Authority and the enforcement service. You do not want to be complacent. You want to make sure that, as that transfer happens and we move forward, the relationship is clear and understood. The role is very much about fraud that is not within the purview of DWP and HMRC, so really it should be focused elsewhere.

Peter Lamb: How would you ensure that you retain sufficient specialist staff within the authority to undertake its varied work?

Richard Las: That is an important part of it. I have touched on resources and how it is a relatively small organisation that requires quite specialist staff. Important to that is a clear career path, so that people understand where they might go and what is in front of them. Common standards are really important. There is a counter-fraud profession across Government; if you cannot retain people, you want people to be able to move around who have a similar level of training and understanding, so that you can have that movement, without it hitting your organisation too hard.

It is important to identify specialist capabilities so that, as an organisation, you can say, “I’m not going to develop that specialist capability because it would be too difficult for me to do that, but I need it. Where am I going to get that from?” That will be a challenge for some areas, where it would not make sense to develop a niche specialism, but somebody might else have it and you could come to an arrangement to deal with that. The same would apply for technological solutions—using data and those sorts of things. It is about building those sorts of relationships. You do not want to be reinventing wheels that people have already well built.

Luke Taylor: We have reviewed the written answers that you sent us. In them, you suggested that the initial investigation workload would be relatively low. Why is that?

Richard Las: It is purely a function of the level of staff working on this at the moment. When you start up a service, it takes time to build a portfolio of cases. It was not intended as a critique in any way; it was just looking at the reality of it. I know that more people are being recruited this year, and that will obviously increase capacity. Over time, you just need to build a portfolio of cases. Ultimately, what I meant was that, because there is probably a relatively small number of investigations, I think a lot of the focus in the first year will be on how the organisation is built, how the process is built and how the recruitment is going—those sorts of things that I mentioned.

Luke Taylor: Do you suspect that a large amount of latent or unobserved fraud exists in the public sector, and is just waiting to be discovered when your team expands and starts lifting up rocks?

Richard Las: Yes and no—sorry to be a bit on the fence. There are probably large amounts of fraud that are not fully understood or estimated, so they do not appear on the spreadsheet or balance book. I guess most organisations will have a good sense of the kind of fraud they face. I think there is a real opportunity to better co-ordinate and work on it. It is undoubtedly larger than the existing estimates, just because of the way they have been done. Generally, people will know that the fraud exists; it is about how you tackle it, face up to it and deal with it.

Luke Taylor: When, if ever, do you expect the workload of the Public Authorities Fraud Investigation and Enforcement Service to increase?

Richard Las: I think that it will be increasing all the time. In the end, it is about resources. There will be a journey that the enforcement service has to go on. As it brings people in, it will undoubtedly increase. Bizarrely, there will be a choice to be made at some point as to whether we want that service to focus on the more complex, cross-cutting and difficult frauds—which will take more people to deal with, so the service will probably do fewer investigations because the work is more complex—or to work at a more mass-market level. Those are the kinds of decisions to be made about impact and the relationship with the other parts of the public sector. Ultimately, if you can prove this is a workable model and get success, I am sure there will be a case to be made for increasing resources, if possible. However, I would have thought that that is a conversation for quite a long way down the line.

Luke Taylor: Would you be confident making and building that case for additional resource to suit the demand that becomes apparent?

Richard Las: It probably will not be my role to do that. I can say that I have certainly made that case many times in the past—often successfully. I am sure that part of my role would be to help evidence if there is a case and say, “Well, actually, this is going really well, but there’s more work to be done” or “There’s a gap; there’s not enough resource”— whatever the conversation would be. It is probably somebody else’s decision as to where that resource would go and how it should be deployed.

Chair: Would you describe yourself as an innovator?

Richard Las: I would. You probably need to ask other people whether they think so.

Chair: As you do, can you give us an example of where you have innovated successfully?

Richard Las: I am one of those people who has worked in a particular area for a number of years. I set myself a challenge on a very regular basis—it used to be in January, but I have moved it around. I look at what I am doing and, if not quite reinvent myself, then certainly challenge myself as to whether I am approaching the work in the right way or whether it should be done differently. Over my time at HMRC—a very well-established organisation—we have transformed it and the investigation side of it. I have brought together investigators who do both criminal and civil work. Many lawyers, over many years, said that that would not be possible, but I managed to do it through introducing processes. The benefit has been the ability to do an end-to-end investigation.

A lot of it has been about investing in technology to support investigations. A lot of my focus has been on how I enable an investigator to spend as much time as possible on the bit of work they add value to: the investigation and the evidential trail. They do not need to do all the work. How do you supplement them with IT systems, data analytics and automated systems that allow them to move on? There are things about people’s roles and things about technology. It has been an interesting journey.

Chair: Which side of your brain is the strongest: “Computer says no” or common sense and discernment?

Richard Las: I do not think I have a part of my brain that says, “Computer says no”, generally speaking. It is probably the latter in the way you describe it. I look for solutions constantly, but I want them to be pragmatic and implementable.

Chair: Correct me if I am wrong, but my understanding is that a considerable portion of your professional life has been spent undertaking investigations, supervising them and so on. Reviewing other people’s work in this area is entirely different. How are you equipping yourself to make that important strategic switch?

Richard Las: It is a strategic switch. I do not think it is one I have not already gone down the road of. I have been a chief investigator for a number of years. One of my frustrations in that role is that I am a bit more detached from being able to manage investigations and be involved; I am more involved in the health of the system and ensuring it works very well. I feel that the reviewing, assurance, and checks and balances are there and that we are building an organisation that is fit for purpose in two, three or four years’ time, looking at the problems that are coming over the horizon.

One of the things I did was set up a team that specifically reports to me to review other teams’ work. I wanted that assurance from an independent set of eyes, rather than up the management chain, to give me another lens on how work is being conducted. That reports directly to me. I am on that journey. This would be more focused in that way.

Markus Campbell-Savours: The role requires you to review the exercise of the Minister’s functions and produce reports and recommendations to the Minister. If you found evidence that relevant functions were not being exercised properly or effectively, how would you address that? What would be the appropriate hierarchy of steps to be taken?

Richard Las: It would depend on what level of inappropriateness we are talking about. Is something ineffective or unlawful? That is an important distinction to make. They are both important things to address, but if activity is unlawful, it is a burning platform and needs to be dealt with immediately. The hierarchy would be initially to raise those issues with the operational leadership in the enforcement service. If they are not being addressed, there is an opportunity to escalate that further across the Public Sector Fraud Authority.

Ultimately, I can make recommendations on those matters. Having received reports in the past, I think it is important that there is factual accuracy in them. It has to be evidence-based. It has to be clear what led me to reach a particular conclusion. If there is a difference of opinion, I think it is important that I reflect that as well. I do not think that the report should be one-sided. It should reflect whether there is a difference as to what the other party thinks, but ultimately the recommendations will be mine and will point to what has happened and what I believe should happen. I do not have to wait for an annual cycle in order to address these things if they are serious.

Markus Campbell-Savours: In terms of escalation, where else could you go?

Richard Las: Ultimately, it will go to the Minister. I think that is where we are heading in a report. That is kind of where the escalation would go.

Markus Campbell-Savours: How would you ensure independence, and the perception of independence, of yourself and your office in carrying out the role?

Richard Las: I touched earlier on my motivation for the role, why I want to do it and my strong beliefs about independence and providing independent, professional advice to people. We have an opportunity in the move of functions over the next few months to embed that. It is important that the team supporting me are seen to be independent of other functions within the enforcement service and the PSFA. That is really important.

In some respects, you can be judged on your reports and what they carry. Otherwise, it is sometimes an assertion: “Well, I’m independent.” Yes, of course, but what is the evidence that you are providing that independent advice? I think that I am selected partly because that is what people feel I can do.

Chair: What do you see your relationship with Parliament being?

Richard Las: There are already a few plans in that. In the passing of the Bill several Members of Parliament either raised concerns or had thoughts about how the service should run. I think it is important to engage with people who have an interest in the Bill and have a view on it. I want to do that quite early, to get views.

Chair: I mean Parliament generically, as an institution.

Richard Las: In submitting a report each year, I imagine that there will be an opportunity at some point to talk to that and to give a report to a parliamentary Committee something like this one. I have not got as far as that, but I imagine that that is probably how it will work.

Chair: Bear with me and help me to conjure up this picture. You identify something that causes some degree of discomfort and embarrassment to the Government or to an individual Minister—not that they are guilty of fraud themselves, but there are things they might have done, such as ignoring advice, not taking advice and so on. You review and you find the ministerial or governmental response lacklustre, lackadaisical, unimpressive, not in the interests of the proper management of public finances and so on. You find yourself hitting your head against a ministerial brick wall as they corral themselves to avoid embarrassment. How do you deal with that?

Richard Las: I have a mechanism for dealing with it in terms of a report, but that report will ultimately be published by the Minister. I do not know if you are suggesting that the Minister would pull the report or not publish it. I guess if we got to the point that we were at such loggerheads—

Chair: There isn’t a timeframe, is there, for the Minister to publish?

Richard Las: No, I think there is an expectation of an annual report, but there is not a specific timeframe, as far as I am aware. I guess that, in the end, this is a matter for my own integrity.

Chair: Would you see your annual report saying, “I have submitted to Ministers 22 pieces of work, of which all 22 have been published by the Minister”, “of which 17 have been published by the Minister” or “of which six have been published by the Minister”?

Richard Las: If there is an annual report, I think that would be the published annual report. There will be an ongoing dialogue, I am sure, about issues as they arise. I would absolutely hope—and my experience in the past has been that you get to this position—that if I have a report that is accurate and the recommendations are practicable and implementable, we will be in a position, even if it is uncomfortable for some organisations, that it should be published, because that is the purpose of it.

Chair: That is the expectation, and it is a perfectly legitimate one to have. My question is about what happens if your expectation is not met.

Richard Las: I would not want to be at loggerheads with people for too long, if that is the case. I am in this to make a difference and to fulfil a function. I would not want to be frustrated in that for very long.

Chair: How would you deal with it?

Richard Las: I don’t know. That is a very hypothetical question. It would probably depend on what the issue was. The ultimate sanction is that I say, “I can’t fulfil this job because I’m not being allowed to,” but that feels like it would be a very long way down the line.

Chair: Do you see yourself as a servant—I use that word but I don’t mean it in the traditional sense—of Ministers, or a servant of the taxpayer? At the end of the day, this is taxpayers’ money.

Richard Las: I see myself as a public servant, absolutely. That is where I have always been. There is a function to advise and support Ministers as well as I possibly can, and to provide options and impartial advice. Public service encompasses all those aspects.

Charlotte Cane: In your written answers, you identified three areas where your performance could be judged, so I would like to ask you a few questions about them. What do you think will make for a good report or recommendation from the independent reviewer?

Richard Las: I have touched on some aspects of that. It is very important that whatever I put in the report is factually accurate, and that if there are disagreements, they are laid out and not hidden, so that we are clear about what the landscape is. From my experience of receiving reports, I feel that ideally, recommendations should be implemented in a short period of time—say, 12 to 18 months. If it is much longer than that, it becomes a little less impactful. It is about this being something where we can move from here to there in a relatively short time—it should be possible to do that. Again, my judgment on what is possible or not possible will be based on my experience, but that is important.

A good report will have recommendations that have impact; sometimes, less is more. Having 50 recommendations on low-level processes may not be as helpful as having a small number of recommendations that are a little more strategic and impactful. It will depend on what I see in front of me, but that is how I imagine it working.

Charlotte Cane: It is obviously important to be factually correct. With fraud, however, it can be that it is quite difficult to establish the facts, but there is a bit of a sniff that is not quite right. How do you see yourself building that into your reports?

Richard Las: You are right that fraud, by its very nature, is usually hidden, but there is an understanding of it. When I talk about factual correctness, that is about issues where maybe investigations have occurred or actions have been taken, and we need to be very clear about what has happened. I have been around the area long enough to know that we cannot always be certain about what we are dealing with—sometimes you turn over a stone and something completely different from what you thought is underneath it.

It is important that if I make a recommendation based on my perception of how things are operating, it is clear why I have made that conclusion. If somebody has a different perception, it is about asking, “Why do you come to that conclusion?”, and then we can move on from it. Ideally, you get to a position where you both have the same view of the world and agree that the recommendation makes sense, but that does not always happen.

Charlotte Cane: What about when you have not turned over the stones yet, but you have looked at them, and something about how they are spaced makes you a little curious? How would you tackle that in a report?

Richard Las: It probably leads to a slightly softer recommendation, in terms of highlighting that as an area that needs further investigation because we do not understand it. There will potentially be indicators of fraud or high risk; it is about whether it is being dealt with appropriately. Richard Las: That is absolutely the kind of conundrum I was trying to highlight. Part of that—in the reviews of the casework, the referrals and the way that investigations are being handled—is looking at the other opportunities that were available. Why were they not selected?

Charlotte Cane: If you are looking at it and they are meeting the objectives that were set at the beginning of the process, how do we know that there is not something—as you say, the raging fire over there—that was not one of the objectives but is a clue that they ought to have gone hunting?

Richard Las: That is about looking at things from a case-by-case basis on that level. I will give an example. In the past, I have had an officer or a team who might want to investigate what might be a money laundering or a tax offence of some sort and they have chosen a very low level of fraud or something with a low offence level, because it is easy to get evidence—“You’ve done this; you should’ve done that. Richard Las: This very much becomes qualitative, I think. Charlotte Cane: In answer to another question, you talked about the need to build a culture where people do not want to commit fraud. Richard Las: There is a lot in that question. I agree with most of what you have said. Chair: We are coming towards the end of our questions, Mr Las. I have two further ones, if you will forbear. The first is about your assessment of the importance of whistleblowers in the processes and whether you have any hinterland of experience in that area.

Richard Las: Whistleblowers are important and, yes, I do have experience. In HMRC, I was responsible for internal governance and professional standards—the part of the organisation that would take in some of the whistleblowing and investigate it. Principally, that would be in relation to fraud as opposed to whistleblowing. In that context, it can also be about people’s conduct and things like that, which would be dealt with in a different part of the organisation. But absolutely, as a recipient, it is vital to have that open channel.

I think there are quite a lot of barriers to people whistleblowing, so it is about how you try to lower those barriers. In HMRC, for example, recently we looked at whether we could provide a bigger incentive for people to whistleblow. If you really are aware of a very large fraud, often you are in quite a senior position in an organisation, potentially, so if you whistleblow on that organisation, the chances are that you will not be able to work for the organisation—the chances of that are very high—and that you might not be able to work in the sector again. That is unfortunate, but it is the reality of it. So it is about how you encourage someone.

We looked at increasing rewards, which will give people a bit more reassurance about what might happen if they successfully whistleblew for us. Depending on the circumstances, we can look at protections for people around their anonymity. I think that needs to be looked at. There are different circumstances of whistleblowing. Sometimes, anonymity is very important for an individual, because they would not do it otherwise and we need to give that assurance. At other times, if we are providing a reward, we might think about that a little harder and say to someone, “Actually, we’d like you to be a witness, because you are making these accusations, so anonymity does not form part of it.” Those are all part of the picture.

My understanding of the process and how the Act went through Parliament was that a commitment was made to look at whistleblowing procedures and procedures more generally across that landscape.
