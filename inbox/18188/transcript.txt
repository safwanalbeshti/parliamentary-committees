Dr Mosello: Good morning, everyone. I am Beatrice Mosello. I am a senior research fellow at Chatham House, a think-tank based in London, here in the UK.

Dr Clack: I am Tim Clack. I am associate professor and director of the climate change and security programme at the University of Oxford, and a multidisciplinary researcher.

Laurie Laybourn: My name is Laurie Laybourn. I am executive director of a think-tank called the Strategic Climate Risks Initiative, and I am a fellow at the University of Exeter, at the Institute for Public Policy Research and at Chatham House.

Q43 Chair: Thank you very much for coming. We are going to have about an hour for this evidence session, if that is all right. I will start off by asking the first question, but other Members may have questions as well. We may not ask everyone to answer every question, because we may not have time; we want to use our time as efficiently as possible and get as much evidence from you and from your huge expertise. We are proud and pleased to have you before us. Thank you very much for coming.

The first question is a general one. The Foreign Secretary has argued that climate change and nature loss should be viewed as national security threats, and there are campaigns on that issue. Do you agree with that framing? I will start with Beatrice Mosello.

Dr Mosello: Thank you for the question. It is an important framing and an important recognition that climate change and nature loss are not just environmental issues. The framing of them as national security issues is important to move them beyond siloisation in how they are treated and therefore responded to. It helps to elevate them on the political and decision-making agenda. It highlights their systemic nature and how the impacts of climate change and nature biodiversity loss have consequences for everything that underpins our economies and societal functions—our security, essentially.

From food production to health and water security, such as our drinking water, we have seen the impacts of climate change very vividly on our doorstep this summer, and we will see them even more going forward. This framing is an important recognition of the systemic nature of the threats and impacts and therefore of the need for systemic responses across Government.

I also think that the framing helps highlight the fact that it is a security issue not just at home, but in the impacts of climate change on us abroad as well. That is how it becomes a foreign policy question. It will have repercussions for the UK at home in terms of the cascading risks and impacts of, for example, a drought in a key food-producing region, which will cause food prices to spike, with repercussions for the cost of living here in the UK.

It will also potentially have repercussions in terms of climate impacts—actually, we have evidence of that in many contexts—driving the social, economic and political factors that cause conflict and violence in specific regions and contributing to more regional and global instability. That obviously has implications for the UK in terms of its development, in its humanitarian, stabilisation, peacebuilding and conflict prevention engagements.

Q44 Chair: Laurie Laybourn, I understand about climate change, but the arguments are not so front and centre when it comes to nature loss being a national security threat. In answering the more general question, could you also focus on that, please?

Laurie Laybourn: Of course. I agree with you that nature has been, as it were, the poor sibling to climate when it comes to our understanding of the security consequences. That has been unfortunate, because, at the end of the day, we are facing an Earth system crisis. The planet has a number of different systems like a human body does—the heart, the respiratory system and so on—and they all have to work in harmony for us to have stable and secure societies. All those systems—the climate system, and water, as Beatrice was saying—are being knocked out of that stability, so really what we need to be talking about here is an Earth system security threat.

Nature is, of course, a huge part of that Earth system and has been relatively under-focused on in the past. That is, of course, changing, and the latest, greatest example of that was the seminal report from the UK Government on the security implications of changes in biodiversity and potential ecosystem collapse. That really is a world-leading document that has clearly shown the vectors by which we would have national security threats here in the UK. The food system is a very good example of that because, where we rely on importing food from places that from are facing ecosystem changes—changes to their nature and biodiversity—that could fundamentally threaten our food security here at home.

For me, the overall national security threat framing is increasingly appropriate just because climate change and the loss of nature have now reached such a severe extent that they are beginning to press up against our areas of national security concern. Food is the latest and greatest example of that, with our domestic harvests so far this decade—in the 2020s—being some of the worst on record, and there are these growing threats abroad that could echo back to us through supply chains.

Of course, as I am sure we will get into, the question is: as those national security threats begin to play out, what type of securitisation will we end up developing here in the UK, and what implications will that have around the world? To throw one thing in before we go into that in more detail, that has to understand that security is a shared construct when you are facing Earth system threats; we cannot pull up the walls to the loss of biodiversity and the breakdown of the climate system. Our securitisation is inevitably going to happen as climate change and nature loss graduate to national security threats, so the type of securitisation—what it is and how we define that in Britain—is crucial for what comes next.

Q45 Chair: The problem might be that if we talk about it in terms of a security threat, you end up with it being framed as that, and it could end up being just about short-term crisis management and not about looking about the issue in more depth. Does anybody want to address that issue?

Janani Vivekananda: Yes, I agree with the framing, but I also agree that there is this perception risk from the terminology of security. That perception risk is separate from, but also very related to, the resourcing risk. Framing climate and nature loss as a security risk does not necessarily mean that it will lead to militarised responses, but there is a perception and a strong lobby that that could be the case. There is a risk that perceptions and the reductivity that come with that framing could lead to these kinds of responses, because perceptions can then shape policy. Unless the Government are explicit in what responses look like, it is likely that we could fall into this path of securitisation, leading to very short-term risk response and perhaps risk management, but certainly not prevention.

The security label, if we leave it unqualified, does invite a harder security reading with things like increased defence spending and militarised contingency planning. But if we ensure that this framing is paired consistently and very visibly in the public space with investment in what is essentially soft security responses—mitigation and adaptation investing, continued investing in loss and damage, and finance—this can be the corrective to the risk of militarised responses.

I would very much underscore what Laurie said about ensuring that this is a systemic response, but also making sure that the security framing is very much people-centred and certainly ecosystem and biodiversity-facing. It is about including these systemic risks, but in order to ensure that we do not fall into the trap of a harder security pathway and path dependency, also ensuring that human security and people-centred approaches—alongside nature, nature loss and biodiversity—can be a corrective to the potential securitisation of climate responses.

Chair: I think Aphra has a follow-up on this.

Q46 Aphra Brandreth: It is actually a follow-up to something that Dr Mosello said about our food security and the national security threat to our food resilience that has been identified. I wanted to get your perspective on whether we should be treating our ability to produce food here in the UK as more of a food security priority and have a proper national strategy on that.

Chair: Do you mind if I ask Dr Clack to answer that? We have not heard from him yet, and it is also within his area of expertise.

Dr Clack: If I may, I would like to answer two questions. First, on the securitisation question, it is important to recognise that security apparatus is already engaged in this space to a degree, if we think about environmental crimes such as illegal logging, illegal fishing and wildlife trafficking. There is a security dimension there already. Yes, we would want to avoid an over-securitised response—think of the example of the war on drugs and the problematic outcomes on the counter-narcotics side—but there are hard security dimensions to a lot of this and those need to be analysed, assessed and, if appropriate, mitigated.

With the drive towards conflict and the rise of violence, we need to recognise that militaries have a significant carbon boot print. Stabilisation, conflict prevention and negotiation are all crucial responses, and obviously diplomacy has a key role in delivering those things. Think about the dual-use opportunities that exist in this space as well in terms of defence engagement, defence diplomacy and security diplomacy. Things like rotary-winged aircraft—helicopters, effectively—are useful from a national security perspective because you can move equipment and forces around, but they are also very useful in accessing areas that have been hit by a natural disaster: they can move aid around and help with communications and logistics.

An even better example than that is what is becoming known as defensive rewilding, which is effectively where you take areas of critical natural infrastructure and restore them—you re-wet marshlands, peatlands and so on. The Baltic defence line from Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania is a good example. Poland’s Green Shield is another really good example of where man-made fortifications are being enhanced through the re-wetting of these landscapes, which obviously act as an obstacle to the movement of armour and logistical vehicles. The reason that is happening in those areas is clear, but the dual use is that these areas trap carbon and create biodiversity corridors, so they are a good thing. Investment in those kinds of areas are outsized because they are dual use.

On the question of food security, this is a massive problem at home and abroad. Ultimately, people are fed largely through their own soil or through trade, so supply chains and soil need to be preserved. There are all kind of issues, currently—

Q47 Chair: Just for the record, what percentage of our food do we grow ourselves and what percentage do we import?

Dr Clack: That is a good question; I do not have those figures, but certainly it is more on the supply side.

Chair: The Committee thinks 60%.

Dr Clack: On food security more generally, what we are seeing around the world—partly because of water security, but also because of soil degradation, biodiversity loss, the loss of pollinators and pest predators and so on—is a reduction in viable agricultural land. That is both horticulture land and grassland for livestock.

Because of that, populations are already starting to respond. You either die, move or secure access to those resources. We are already seeing the colonisation of new territories, the annexation of agricultural land, the creation of militias to protect resources, and over-extraction as communities try to create a surplus, which is obviously problematic because you have over-extracted.

Another point that is often missed is buying food production infrastructure in other countries. Lots of countries have been very good at this quietly. China in particular, over the last three decades, has been buying up a huge amount of food production infrastructure all over the world—in Africa and Asia, but also in North America—to the extent that Smithfield Foods, for example, was bought out by a Chinese company a few years ago and effectively bought out by the Chinese state. That gives China control of one in four pigs in America, so quite remarkable things are happening, largely unrecognised.

Countries are quietly securing access to food. One of the things we need to be conscious of is that we need to do a lot of friendshoring around our own supply chains—making those supply chains more robust—because when stress and crises hit we will need to be able to ensure our access to food.

My final point is that that is not without issues, including diplomatic issues—as we saw with the covid crisis over PPE, for example. When you start extracting from an area and then there is a change to supply and demand, and the host community does not have access to those things any more because they cannot afford the inflated price, that causes problems.

I will give you some examples. Ethiopians have not forgotten that, during the famine in the 1980s, Europe was still importing huge amounts of sugar snap peas, for example. That has not been forgotten; it still shapes diplomatic relationships. If we want to think intergenerationally, we can think about the Holodomor in eastern Europe, in which the Soviets were extracting food and creating famine, and the Indian famine during world war two. These things are diplomatic realities that we still live with to this day.

Q48 Jessica Toale: I want to go back to what Laurie and Janani were saying about national security as a framing. I can see how that can create a sense of urgency in terms of international co-operation or national action to respond to a challenge, but can any of the experts reflect on what impact that framing would have on public support for the actions that Government need to take to address the big issues?

Chair: Who do you want to ask that to?

Jessica Toale: Let’s go back to Laurie on that one. Laurie, you were talking about how the potential to draw up the walls could be a negative consequence of this type of framing. There is obviously a risk when you talk to the public about a securitised approach to climate change and nature loss, because it is something so big and quite disempowering most of the time that the instinct is to retract inwards.

Laurie Laybourn: I think that question gets to the heart of the matter. It will be a delicate tightrope that we have to walk along, because on the one hand there is that potential knee-jerk, emergency, hard security, tactical response, where you see threats to food security and seek to score supplies in a zero-sum way from others, as Tim was implying. But on the other side we anticipate a near future in which climate change and nature loss have become so severe that they are automatically national security issues because, simply, they are adversely impacting the fundamentals of our national security. We will have to get a balance that toes the line between responding to the symptoms of that world and ensuring that we stay focused on what is causing all of it.

Our concern, and the concern from others, is about a potentially militarised or authoritarian securitisation. Fundamentally, we are worried about that because, alongside the harm and de-democratisation that it could cause, it would mean that we were overly focused on the symptoms of the climate-changed world that we are in, at the cost of dealing with its causes. That would lead us into a potentially catastrophic situation globally, because it would mean that as things got worse we would increasingly fail to focus on what was causing that and therefore on the need to reduce emissions and so on.

We face a situation in which securitisation is in some ways inevitable because climate change has been allowed to get this bad. The question is whether we can fashion some kind of progressive or networked idea of what that securitisation could be. For the public that would mean that you are in a situation where you say, “Okay, the story that you have heard for decades—that if left suitably unchecked this could become so bad—has unfortunately now come true and our food security is stressed, and the systems that give us the ability to ensure that there is geopolitical co-operation globally have been stressed, not just by decisions of various leaders but by climate change. That means that it is now going to have to be the priority that a number of campaigners have always said it has to be.”

I think there is a way of telling that story that ensures you mitigate the potential for a more hard security response, but that is coming down the line at us, unfortunately, because as we go over 1.5°C key elements of our security and economy—such as food and water—will experience such severe impacts that they will automatically become national security concerns.

Q49 Edward Morello: I will direct this at Dr Clack, but I am also interested in other takes. Climate change is obviously driving political instability, conflict and food security issues but it is also driving irregular migration, especially towards rich countries such as the UK. We then also have the whole food security issue that we are talking about. I wonder whether the cuts to the UK international aid budget—large amounts of that budget were spent on stability and climate resilience in developing countries with the aim of giving people a place to stay, rather than them feeling they need to move—are causing more irregular migration and greater issues in-country. Does it impact our ability to carry out climate diplomacy when our contribution to combating climate change is now so constrained?

Dr Clack: Yes is the answer, clearly. Prevention is better than cure. If ecosystems, landscapes and terrain become uninhabitable or if the carrying capacity of that land is compromised in some way, people have to move. Studies demonstrate that lots of people first move internally—which is internal displacement—but there are motivations for moving more widely. That is politically problematic in lots of countries.

When it comes to migration there is also the issue that migrants usually have a sense that they will return home at some point. In a kind of scorched-earth environment that has been desecrated by climate change that is not possible. There is no prospect of going home and that creates a kind of intergenerational obligation. We have seen lots of issues in the past from the infrastructure in urban areas becoming overwhelmed. There were lots of complicated drivers of the Syrian civil war but it is no coincidence that before it happened there was a multi-year drought that saw lots of people removed from their rural communities and moving into cities, with grievances and so on. Food inflation reached something like 26% in Egypt before Mubarak was overthrown, so there are lots of examples that we can speak to.

In terms of ODA funding and infrastructure, there have been serious cuts to UK programming and reach. I have an example I can speak to in terms of climate security. From January 2024 to the middle of this year, I was directing a project in Jordan building the world’s first climate security intelligence capability, which was really highly regarded and highly sought. It had lots of really good impact and lots of access. It was developed in engagement with the highest levels of the Jordanian state, including the Hashemite court, municipalities, Ministries and so on. After the strategic review settlement, climate security was taken out of play, so effectively the programme had to end, despite this thing having been built and there having been huge amounts of buy-in locally and huge amounts of value, both for the British embassy in Amman and elsewhere. There was regional interest in building a network of these things in Syria, Lebanon and Iraq—all the kinds of places where this kind of insight would be really viable.

The tap for the project was effectively turned off, and the project had to end. We were able to keep a level of support going through some funding that we are able to find through the University of Oxford, but that is a sub-optimal situation to be in. The implication of taps going on and off is that you lose access, diplomatic trust, engagement and access to different parts of the state that could be useful from a security perspective but also from a development perspective as well.

Migration in Jordan was one of the biggest issues that they were dealing with. One of the grievances that was repeated often through many of the individuals me and my team were working with—I will not name names—was that the UK had committed to support the influx relating to Syrian refugees over a sustained period of time, and again the taps were turned off, so there was a frustration there and there was a diplomatic fallout from that.

The quality of the answers is extremely high, and I am very grateful for that. I think this has been a very useful part of the session, but I ask that we pick up a bit.

Q50 Sir John Whittingdale: I am not quite sure how you will answer this question briefly, but I’m sure you can have a go. The consequences of climate change are made more complicated by all the phenomena that will be affected; I am thinking of AMOC, El Niño and so on. If we look ahead and get to the worst-case scenario, which is now becoming more of a possibility, where in the world will be worst affected? In which regions is it going to become so acute that we are looking, for instance, at really mass migration as a consequence?

Dr Clack: There is a brilliant map in one of the IPCC reports, which basically forecasts areas that will effectively become uninhabitable because of water scarcity at the point of 2070. As you would expect, that map has a lot of red around the equator, sub-Saharan Africa, central Asia and so on. Those are really densely populated areas that have high population growth currently. Those areas are effectively going to see large-scale movement. There are lots of estimates of what those numbers might look like—António Guterres at the UN said a billion people by 2050. I think that is probably oversizing the kind of risk, but we are talking about a really high level of unprecedented human mobility that we have not seen previously, and those individuals will effectively need to go somewhere.

Laurie Laybourn: Alongside that, if we are talking about worst cases, there are now a number of wildcards that we cannot rule out, unfortunately. One was just mentioned: the AMOC—the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation—which is why we have vineyards in England at roughly the latitude at which Canada has polar bears. We now cannot rule out that it could stop moving heat northwards, which would be disastrous for the UK. The climate refugees would then be people from Britain, Ireland and north-western Europe.

If we are talking about the range of possibilities now on the table because climate change has got this bad, and about migration, there are a number of dimensions. One is the story of the tropics that we heard there from Tim, which is, of course, an area of significant institutional concern all around the world. But we must also remember that migration within Britain, and potentially out of it, is something that we increasingly need to have on our minds.

Q51 Sir John Whittingdale: My constituency is now full of vineyards, which I regard as a very small silver lining in all of this. Dr Mosello, did you want to come in?

Dr Mosello: Yes, I have just a quick addition to what Dr Clack and Laurie have been saying. On the hotspots that we should be concerned about, the recent situation with the Iran conflict and the closure of the strait of Hormuz has made it very clear that it is not just about the climate change impacts, which would be bad enough; it is about how those climate change impacts interact with geopolitical conflict and fragility dynamics. A lot of research has gone into understanding where the different drivers of vulnerability overlap, and essentially all the fragile and conflict-affected contexts are also the ones that are most climate-vulnerable. You have compound risks that need to be taken into account, which shine the spotlight on regions such as the horn of Africa, the Sahel, Lake Chad and others that are deeply in conflict, where the humanitarian situation is already extremely dramatic and where, on top of that, you have the impacts of climate change.

Janani Vivekananda: Could I quickly add that, along with those stresses, we are also already facing lethal heat and humidity? It is not just El Niño and the potential AMOC implications. This is affecting South Asia, parts of the middle east, north Africa, the Amazon basin and sub-Saharan Africa, and it is only projected to increase. That is making outdoor temperatures—wet-bulb temperatures—unliveable from a human health perspective, and those areas also cover so much of the world’s food production. It is not just about thinking about what this means for the people living there in terms of migration and mobility.

To underscore the point, most of the research that we have now shows that people are not moving as a result of climate change regionally. It is predominantly from rural areas to urban areas, or within the same country or just over domestic borders, but not in terms of what UK-based constituents might be concerned about; that is not so much what the research on drivers of mobility is pointing to. It is not sub-Saharan Africa into Asia or from central America and Latin America into the US, for example. We are not seeing that. I think we need to look at some of these compound risks in terms of what they might mean for regional fragility in the contexts that I just mentioned, and also for food production and access to fertiliser, things like this. Those compound and cascading risk pathways are definitely something that we need to better understand.

Q52 Alex Ballinger: I am interested in the ranking of the risks, because you have all given very good examples of terrible things that will happen around the world, from water scarcity, to the UK changing its temperature, to food insecurity all along the equator. Has any work been done on ranking those risks, and on which ones we should be most concerned about and most focused on as the UK? I know Laurie has done some work on this, but perhaps I will go to Dr Clack first.

Dr Clack: Partly, it is a question of prioritisation, isn’t it? Effectively, it is to do with perspective. So, what would be a priority for a state in sub-Saharan Africa might be different from what would be a priority for a climate migrant in the middle east, and for the UK.

I would suggest, with a sense of the kind of political landscape that there is, that the economic security consequences of this are right up there for the UK. Studies have shown that we are looking at costs of approximately 20% of GDP per year within the next three decades. That is a huge amount of money that has to be found by the state in order to—

Q53 Alex Ballinger: And that is due to climate change, or these nature loss things—

Dr Clack: That is to do with system restoration; it is to do with adaptations that need to be put in place, with hardening of infrastructure that suddenly becomes unviable at certain temperature levels, and so on.

Climate-induced shifts to the cost of capital are already occurring. The insurance market is becoming very concerned about what these realities will look like, both in the short term and in the longer term. Appetite for investment is shifting. There is also the issue of capital flight, which is particularly problematic in parts of the global south that are very vulnerable to these changes. Ultimately, commercial enterprises need to recognise that they are not vulnerable and that they are going to be able to make money. But actually, if they are going into areas that are particularly fragile, that is not going to be possible, with huge diplomatic consequences, because who will these countries start to blame in those situations? So, there will be economic impacts.

Obviously, conflict is the primary national security issue. Climate change is a driver, but not a singular driver. When you mix in sectarian, economic, identity-type situations, and you mix in food security, water security, energy security and migration, you’ve got a real constellation of problems there, which are going to amplify the amount of conflict that we are going to see around the world.

We can point to a longitudinal study that demonstrates that. A few years ago, the University of California, Berkeley and Stanford conducted a study looking at incidents of civil war in sub-Saharan Africa; I think that it was over a 30-year time-slice. For every increase in temperature of 1°C, incidents of civil war went up by 4.5%, which is a huge problem. And that is just sub-Saharan Africa.

We can talk about pressures over fresh water and agricultural land in those contexts; I will not do so, just for reasons of time. However, there are lots of studies that could be pointed to in that regard.

I think you have the economic situation at the top. You have then got the conflict situation—the stabilisation situation. Then, you have also got what you might call grey zone threats, which is things like the theft of green intellectual property, the weaponisation of ESG reporting, and lawfare creating problems linked to legal liabilities and risks. There is the disinformation dimension, as well, and the FIMI—foreign information manipulation and interference. States are already very active in this space, suggesting that climate change is a form of colonialism or neo-colonialism, that the west is responsible, that the west has poisoned wells, that it has stolen water, that it is growing cash crops and degrading irrigation systems.
