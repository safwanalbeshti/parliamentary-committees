Q716 Chair: Welcome to the Treasury Select Committee on Tuesday 13 October 2026. Today, we are delighted to have the Financial Conduct Authority in front of us. We have regular sessions, of course, with the FCA. Today, we are looking particularly at one of the pillars of its review and its forward plan, which is being a smarter regulator. I am pleased to welcome, as ever, Nikhil Rathi, chief executive of the Financial Conduct Authority. He is joined today by Jessica Rusu, who is the chief data, information and intelligence officer at the FCA, and Johan Sekora, who is the chief operating officer at the FCA.

Before we go into the main session, Mr Rathi, I just wanted to raise with you some of the challenges that the FCA has had in dealing with whistleblowers. In particular, the FCA has decided to appoint Lea Paterson, who is one of your new non-executive members of the board, to undertake a review of your interactions with the whistleblower Simon Andriesz, following his untimely death. First, why did you appoint somebody internal, or a member of the board, rather than using an external person to undertake the review?

Nikhil Rathi: It is a terribly sad situation. I have written to Mr Andriesz’s representative to pass on my condolences and those of the whole organisation. Mr Andriesz was a whistleblower. He had engaged with the FCA and a number of authorities, both in the UK and the United States, over a number of years.

When a very sad situation such as this happens, it is important for us to reflect on how we have communicated and engaged with the individual in question. The board is independent of the executive. They are independent non-executive directors appointed by the Treasury. Lea Paterson is new to the organisation, having only joined the board on 1 October, and has relevant experience here. She will look at the way in which we communicated and whether there are any reflections for us.

I would also note that whistleblowing work is some of the most sensitive work that the FCA undertakes. We receive around 1,300 whistleblowing reports every year. That is up 20% on the previous year.

Q717 Chair: Was that for 2025-26?

Nikhil Rathi: Yes, that is right. Dame Harriett will recall that I wrote to this Committee back in July 2023 about the Mr Odey case, which has now concluded before the Upper Tribunal. I will not get into the details of the case, but in that letter I explained how challenging it can be when we are dealing with some very serious allegations. We are not always able to speak publicly about the nature of the work that we are doing, nor are we necessarily able to communicate to the people who are making those allegations to us the nature of the investigations that we are undertaking.

Over recent years, we have established an enhanced feedback mechanism following a pilot, where we seek to provide feedback to whistleblowers. Some 40% of whistleblowing allegations result in some supervisory action. Around 50% inform our wider work. There will always be cases, though, where, having examined the evidence and considered it carefully, it is not of a nature where we are going to be able to take regulatory action, or sometimes criminal action, which is what people are seeking from us.

Q718 Chair: Is the review limited to your interactions as an organisation with Mr Andriesz, or does it go wider than that?

Nikhil Rathi: It is focused on the interactions with Mr Andriesz, because this is a specific and very sad situation that has occurred. Q728 Chair: Once that is published, the board still has the option of going for a wider, more independent review if it chooses to. Is it within Lea Paterson’s remit that she could recommend a further review, if she felt that she came across something that showed that there needed to be a wider review?

Nikhil Rathi: Lea is able to recommend anything that she feels appropriate, and the board will consider it. The board has a wide range of powers and options available to it. As I say, the work that we do on whistleblowing is some of the most significant and sensitive work that we do. We take every report that we receive seriously. It is engaged with carefully and thoughtfully. We also have an obligation to look at all evidence that we receive objectively and dispassionately. Sometimes, after detailed examination, we may not be the appropriate authority to take something forward, or, indeed, there may be action that has already been taken in another jurisdiction in relation to specific allegations.

In terms of some of the most challenging situations for us, I talked about that particular non-financial misconduct case where the tribunal has now published its judgment. It is there and it speaks for itself, so I will not go into the details. In non-financial misconduct cases in general, for example, we are sometimes the last resort in terms of what whistleblowers are seeking from us. They have been to the police, and there has not been satisfaction there because an investigation has not proceeded. They do not feel that they have had satisfaction in the employment tribunal.

While we can consider the matters and look at things within our remit, we are not necessarily going to be, in those types of cases, for example, the authority that is going to secure a criminal conviction or, indeed, an employment outcome. Nikhil Rathi: This was a review commissioned by the Lloyds Banking Group and its board, so it is independent of the FCA. In many respects, it engages activities that were outside the regulatory perimeter at the time. Nevertheless, we are very interested in the process and in the outcomes of the review.

As I understand it, Dame Linda has said to you that the investigation stage is complete and the report is now being finalised. There will then be a period in which Lloyds needs to engage with the report before the findings are made public to this Committee, hopefully during the course of next year.

It is clearly disappointing that an exercise such as this one takes the length of time that it does. I do not have anything to add beyond the explanations that Dame Linda has given to you as to the nature and complexity of the investigation into that many individual cases and the time that it took to gather the evidence that she sought.

Q730 Chair: Of course, she did not have the statutory power to compel witnesses. If it had been under section 166, you would have had more power. You are saying that it is outside the perimeter, and we understand that, but are there lessons to be learned more widely for the sector about an internal review versus your role as the FCA? People will have died along the way. Businesses will have gone. Any lessons learned will not really apply to the people who were affected in this case. Nikhil Rathi: Whenever these situations arise, one of the challenges that we have is that there are multiple mechanisms for the system to seek to secure answers, and for affected individuals or firms to secure answers. In this case, there have been many court cases that have worked their way through the system. That can sometimes complicate other processes, because, when something is before the court, that process necessarily takes precedence.

There have been multiple Select Committee inquiries, and the Select Committees have very wide-ranging powers if you feel that you need to access information. There has been some regulatory work, some work by boards and, indeed, some wider criminal cases related to HBOS in particular and some of the activities here.

I recognise that that multiplicity of processes can, when you add it all up, lead to quite an unsatisfactory overall outcome, because each is looking at different things. That may be an issue that we can come on to when we talk about the smarter regulator pillar and how we ensure institutional effectiveness and answers in situations such as this, at speed, within the system and framework of review and judicial scrutiny that we have.

Chair: Thank you. That is a very fair comment.

Q731 John Glen: On 24 March, which was one of the last times you were before the Committee, we had a conversation about Palantir. I asked you about the effect of excluding Palantir from the process, and you said, “We think that we would have a lower-quality proof of concept relative to the winning bidder. That is the choice that you face”.

I was wondering if you could update the Committee on what your experience has been with Palantir. We are very mindful of the fact that there has been a lot of public commentary around the effectiveness and availability of alternatives, and, crucially, whether you will have discretion to remove them in future, given the way that they are, by some, deemed to operate and create a function that cannot be removed. Would you like to comment on what you have done? I would acknowledge that you have done a lot since you came into office on the transformation programme, and this is the latest stage of it.

Nikhil Rathi: I will turn to Jessica in a minute, and she can go into more detail. The opportunities that the UK has in terms of growth and productivity with new technology are enormous. The financial and professional services industry could be right at the forefront of securing those, and we have been leaning in very hard to make sure that we can support innovation.

What I would also say to the Committee this morning is that the risks to the integrity of the financial services system and, to use a shorthand, market integrity are right now as serious as I have ever seen them, driven also by the very same technology that is enabling opportunity, as well as by the shift in market structures, with 24/7 trading, digitisation, tokenisation and enormous cross-border flows of capital, and both of those trends being amplified by the activities of networks of financial criminals, money launderers and fraudsters operating across borders.

Key to our work to tackle financial crime is our ability to join up the intelligence and data that we have within our organisation—and we are not perfect and, whenever things do not work out right at the FCA, that issue of joining up data and intelligence is right at the heart of it—with the data and intelligence that we receive from our partner organisations, that is available in the public domain and that we can secure from around the world. That is why we are investing enormously in our data and technology capabilities to be able to match the scale of the risk and the harm from crime, in particular, that we are seeing.

I will now turn to Jessica, who can talk about the specifics of that proof of concept and where we are now.

Jessica Rusu: As Nikhil set out, we have been investing heavily in our data science, technology and infrastructure engineering departments over the last five years. We have now grown the data, technology and innovation function to over 600 colleagues. It is very important that we understand how much we have invested in data science, and yet how much further there is to go.

In particular, financial crime has increased to £14 billion per annum, so the challenge that we face as a regulator is how much time and energy we want to invest in the creation of our own frontier AI models and where we have the opportunity to leverage vendors. In particular, we have over 100 technology suppliers that we work with. Approximately 35 of them are tier 1, 2 and 3 suppliers.

It is really important to calibrate the decision around fragmentation of suppliers across multiple small vendors, as well as the benefits of scale. This particular proof of concept was seeking to evaluate the benefits of intelligence at scale, looking at the ability to integrate the data layer with the technology and intelligence layer. That proof of concept has now concluded. It is finished, and we are reflecting on the lessons that we have learned from that as well as our wider technology ambitions.

Q732 John Glen: Could you tell us about your reflections and instincts? Have you successfully retained full operational knowledge? What has happened with intellectual property in terms of running these tools yourself or independently? There is significant public debate about this matter.

Jessica Rusu: Yes, there is.

John Glen: It is important that we have clarity from you about what you have learned, and what your assessment is of Palantir specifically, from this proof of concept.

Jessica Rusu: In particular, the concept of sovereign data and the arrangements that we have for our own cloud data are really important. I gave evidence previously about this matter in terms of the data controls that were established for this particular proof of concept to make sure that we remained the data controller at all times, as well as the security and encryption keys remaining with the FCA throughout the entire proof of concept, as they would do under any supplier arrangement.

Q733 John Glen: This is a dynamic environment. There are lots of new options and providers available. Do you have any assessment yet of whether you will have a recurring commercial relationship with Palantir, or will you need to procure again in light of the developments that exist?

Jessica Rusu: If we decided to invest in an intelligence platform, we would, of course, go to open procurement. Typically, we warm up the market ahead of any procurement exercise. We outline very specifically what we want to see. We invite firms in to do demos with us. We have a pre-engagement phase, and then we go through a full procurement phase.

Q734 John Glen: Would it be fair to say that Palantir does not have a universal lock-in in terms of future work at the FCA?

Jessica Rusu: That is correct.

Q735 John Glen: Could I turn then to the Mills review? This was published in July with seven recommendations, one of which is about building an AI-enabled agentic supervisory model. Could you tell us about what concrete operational steps you have taken to implement that? I recognise that it is only three months ago, but these things are urgent.

Jessica Rusu: Becoming an agentic regulator is part of our overall ambition of being a smarter regulator. As I mentioned already, we have been investing heavily in our data science functions. As we transition to enabling AI across the organisation, we find that, in particular, AI handles unstructured text very well and supports our case officers in their decision making.

As we consider the various ways in which we can use that for intelligence gathering and case optimisation, we have been able to look at ways to embed that in authorisation, supervision and enforcement work. As you can imagine, we handle a lot of intelligence in unstructured text, and it really speeds up the case officer’s analysis.

At this point in time, we remain with a case officer, or sometimes what we call a human in the loop, with every decision, but our ambition is further than that, which is to automate wherever we can, to intervene at scale, and to shift left any harms that we can identify at the gateway.

Q736 John Glen: To put it simply, what you mean is that you could have AI dealing with some of the complaints and issues that come before the FCA instead of a human operative. Is that fair?

Nikhil Rathi: I will give you an example, and Jessica can expand on this. We get a billion rows of data a week in our market surveillance. The UK is home to some of the largest global markets in the world, across a range of asset classes. One of the first use cases for our agentic tools has been to clean that data, and to go back, potentially agentically, to the firms that are submitting, and say, “This does not reconcile. There seems to be an error here”, at pace and at speed, and potentially also interact with the firm’s own agents and automated platforms, so that our people, when they get to it, can get to the highest-risk issues significantly more quickly, and we use our resources significantly more efficiently.

Q737 John Glen: Further to the Mills review and its recommendations, what assessment have you made of what additional statutory powers you might need versus supervisory action? We have spoken before, and I will come on to it later, about the legislative blocker that sometimes seems to exist—and those are my words, not yours—from the Treasury. If you need new powers, have you identified those yet so that they can be fed into the system?

Nikhil Rathi: Our thinking is evolving on this issue. My approach, as I touched on last time I was with the Committee, is that it is very important that we use the powers that we have to the fullest extent that we can. Those powers are anchored in FSMA around the perimeter, but we have powers under the Enterprise Act and the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act. We have money laundering powers that are being extended under the current Bill. I am not going to come here and suggest that we need lots more powers until we have really demonstrated that we have used our powers to the fullest extent.

Later on, Jessica and I will be at the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum, where the boards are meeting. It is working in tandem with everybody in the system—the CMA, Ofcom and the Information Commissioner all have powers in that context. We need to co-ordinate between us to make sure that we sometimes do the work, and another agency uses its powers on the back of our work.

What is as important as powers—and there may well need to be discussions on some of the issues around national security, frontier AI and operational resilience—is that we are devoting a huge amount of resource to that as part of our overall work on market integrity. Where the Government decide to draw the line on what is relevant for national security—economic and national security are increasingly intertwined—we are looking at the questions of AI and advice. At some point, we may need a perimeter shift there, but it is not completely straightforward, because there is a particular blurry boundary there. The question of AI and advice goes beyond financial advice. It is also about medical advice.

It is a societal choice as to how Government and Parliament wish to interact on those issues. That brings me to the bigger point that I make, which is that, more than anything, what we need on AI is that societal and parliamentary debate around risk, and where you would like to see us calibrate some of these choices as regulators.

Q738 John Glen: I take your message about risk to every fringe event or opportunity to speak, and it is a reasonable one. Initiating and getting a conclusion on that debate is important.

The last question that I have relates to what you said previously. In the March 2025 session, you committed to delivering a streamlined single digital interface for regulated firms interacting with the FCA. Of course, many colleagues on the Committee and in Parliament are lobbied, by smaller firms in particular, about the costs, bureaucracy and anxiety of an uncertain interface, which, though well intentioned, causes a lot of cost and additional strain. Could you say something about that single digital interface and where you have got to on your aspirations to provide it?

Jessica Rusu: We launched My FCA in March 2025. We have had really positive firm feedback. The intention of this one front door is so that firms can submit both their regular returns to us as well as their ad hoc collections in one place, and see fees, upcoming submission or deadlines that are relevant to them. That is the front door for the FCA in terms of its regulatory returns.

We recently extended operating hours until midnight every night. That was another request that firms asked from us. We have also streamlined reporting requirements. We have decommissioned eight returns and reduced the frequency of some of them from quarterly to annual, et cetera. We have also reduced the volume of ad hoc requests by over 3,000, year over year. All in all, we estimate that we have reduced firms’ reporting requirements by about 50,000, which is about £27 million in firm benefit.

Nikhil Rathi: We are not done yet; there will be further changes, but I would underline that we also need data to do our job. I sometimes hear the messages that you receive from some of the small firms in your constituencies, but I would also draw to your attention that some of the most significant harms and risks that we have had to intervene against have sometimes been individual financial advisers and small firms that were perhaps not so willing to co-operate with us and give us data.

We took 30 enforcement investigations on the British Steel pension scheme, which was a problematic advice issue. We intervened through a very significant joint operation with the Met police a few weeks ago, which involved 800 officers. We have intervened against up to 30 firms, many of which are quite small, where we suspect that there is potentially very serious organised criminality, and we will see what happens as that investigation proceeds.

We want to be proportionate and careful with the admin burden, but we also need to be probing, particularly in this area of money laundering and financial crime, to protect the integrity of the system.

Q739 Chair: Before we move on, Ms Rusu, you just mentioned that you had reduced the number of ad hoc requests. There are 3,000 fewer ad hoc requests now for firms. In your annual report, you talk about an average cost of £3,700 per request, but that can vary widely. Do you have a figure for the highest and lowest costs? Q740 Chair: So you are trying to come up with other solutions, rather than going to the firms first? Are you then looking at the highest-cost requests, and is that what is driving you to go to third-party providers?

Jessica Rusu: Yes, absolutely. If we think that there is a disproportionate firm burden, we will push back on that request. We will analyse it. We will look at whether there is any other way that we can get that information. We have also increased the length of time that we give firms to respond up to eight weeks, because that is something else that helps to reduce the cost and the burden on firms.

Q741 Chair: You have not disaggregated the figures when you have reported them. You have done it as an average. Is that something that you could consider?

Jessica Rusu: I am sure that we could come back with some information about the range of costs.

Q742 Chair: As Mr Glen says, we all get lobbied a lot about the sometimes disproportionate costs for firms. Mr Rathi has explained very clearly why you still need to investigate. Jessica Rusu: The important point to make is that we have introduced 32 new data-sharing arrangements that help us offset the cost of that burden, which I estimate to be just under £1 million of burden savings to firms.

Q743 Bobby Dean: Ms Rusu, you spoke about the FCA’s ambitions in terms of using agentic AI, which I am sure is probably shared by regulated firms and consumers as well. Could I tease out a broader risk associated with agentic AI that a lot of people have? In terms of the way that these models work, they are not necessarily coded line by line and crafted specifically to do a job. In other pieces of software, there is a whole line of inputs, and we expect the outputs to be repeated as designed, whereas these models iterate over time and are almost growing. They have an internal logic that we do not see all the workings of.

Is there a danger that, in pursuing agentic AI of this type and becoming potentially reliant on it in the future, when these models are updated by the core firms, whichever firm you go with, it creates a mind of its own and we start to lose grip on what we initially thought we got those agents to do? How do you protect yourself against that software update coming in, and your agents suddenly taking an internal logic in the future that is different from what you thought they were doing?

Jessica Rusu: That is the most important live debate around the evolution of AI and the idea that the agent can act on your behalf, so there are really important questions to work through. We have started to investigate some of those in our open finance work. For example, as you say, there is a field of study called KYA, or know your agent. Can you authenticate that particular agent? There are risk management frameworks, sometimes called harnesses, being evaluated for continuous conditional assurance of an agent. How do you understand the amount, the consent, and the revocation and duration of the consent? All those things are actively being worked through by industry.

As you know, our AI lab is open, and applicants are actively welcome to apply with agentic solutions. We have at least three agentic payment solutions being worked through at the moment in the AI lab—for example, personal planning, personal inventory management, household management of groceries and food purchases. Other kinds of agentic solutions relate to continuous compliance checks, for example.

Q744 Bobby Dean: There is a challenge that might come back. I like the concept of know your agent, but is it possible to know it? We have seen test cases recently where the software is, essentially, trying to evade instruction and escape sandboxes, for example, or showing behaviours that look like manipulation. I am not saying that these things are sentient, but it has pursued its own internal logic, which obscures to the user what its intentions are. Do you feel confident that you are able to know your agents?

Jessica Rusu: Those are really important risks that are being evaluated by the frontier labs. There is a very live debate about the concept of testing and security around the release of frontier AI models, as you know. Many of the applications that firms are adopting are less action-oriented or less risk-oriented. One such use case is security patching. For example, a firm might identify an agent to continuously monitor for vulnerabilities, identify those patches and deploy them at pace. That is an example of an agentic operation that would operate within a security guardrail or harness, and many firms are building those kinds of applications.

As you mentioned, there are very serious concerns about frontier AI models, the testing that they deploy on their agents, and then the wider adoption by the UK market and the global market.

Nikhil Rathi: As it stands right now, I do not think we have the assurances that you are asking for. That is one of the reasons why the AI Safety Institute is very engaged on these issues. It is not just individual agents. What you saw with some of those incidents, such as the Hugging Face incident—I do not know if the Committee has been briefed on that—is that it is about swarms of agents and how they interact with each other to perhaps move beyond the protocols that they were established for.

That requires not only our AI security institutions to understand it, but it is heavily cross-border. That is why the work around this within the G20, under the UK’s presidency, is going to be so important. If there are going to be standards before such models are released, it is only going to work effectively if they are genuinely global and there is deep global co-operation around these models.

Q745 Bobby Dean: Could we move on to your supercharged sandbox? I know that is something the FCA has been testing for some time now. Could you explain to us what has been achieved by that process so far? Could you also pick up on the integrity bits of the sandbox that we have just been discussing? How can you have assurances that nothing will get out from your testing environment?

Jessica Rusu: The AI lab and the supercharged sandbox are pilot environments where firms can test, using synthetic datasets as well as some of their own real-world datasets. We have had a pretty significant uptake and interest in these, with about a 50% year-over-year increase in applications to our sandbox. We have run two cohorts thus far. We have learned a lot from those interactions about the good and poor practices for AI implementations.

Alongside that, we have also done supervisory visits that are called AI live testing, where we go on-site to firms that invite us to look at the evaluation harnesses, risk management, product development and deployment approaches that they have taken internally. Through that effort, we have learned a lot about good and poor practices, which we can share in about a week’s time, when they will be published.

In terms of the sandbox environment, these are very secure environments that are completely air-gapped both from the FCA and from any other system. The use cases that we tend to test are beneficial use cases that support consumer education or real-world applications such as compliance.

Q746 Bobby Dean: The latest cohorts of AI live testing started in January this year, and we expect an evaluation report early next year. There are some concerns about pace. If we are testing over the course of a year, but these models are iterating so quickly, are we getting the results quickly enough to inform live activity on the ground?

Jessica Rusu: I think so. The first cohort was at the start of this year, in January. The second cohort started in the summer and is in flight. We will have the results of that cohort through the end of the year. The important thing is that this is real testing, and it takes time to do real engineering work. These are not conferences. These are development environments where the firm has the opportunity to develop and deploy products and test them in a real-world scenario, and it does take time to do that testing properly.

Nikhil Rathi: I would make one point around pace. We are going to have to grapple with the way in which this technology is moving so quickly. Nikhil Rathi: I would have to check the numbers. Certainly, during the Brexit period it was very high because there was so much work going on. It has abated somewhat since then.

I would also make this point. When I was appointed and reappointed, you asked me whether I would be open with you if there were any disagreements with the Treasury. Of course I would be.

If you think about the issues that we need to be considering as a system, you hear this discussion around the deficit of trust in institutions that the public has across the board. It varies for different institutions. To me, that is a question around institutional effectiveness. Do our citizens feel like they can get things out of us at the speed and pace that they quite legitimately expect? We are looking at how technology can help us deliver that.

When you look at the overall accountability framework and the judicial framework, we have something like 40 to 45 accountability mechanisms. Across the landscape of our work at the FCA, we have 80-plus “have regards”. To go back to Mr Dean’s question on the referral mechanism, if you watch the passage of legislation—much of it is created in the Treasury and then ratified by Parliament—every single one adds more mechanisms. The idea is that we can deliver an operational objective through a bureaucratic device. Each one of those mechanisms has to have a process around it and can be judicially challenged. At the back end, when we want to enforce, if we want a criminal trial, we now have to wait three years in the courts before we get a trial date. For the civil cases, it is different.

That is the system question that does not get enough attention. That is why we are very concerned about the referral mechanism: because we think that is just adding to system blockage, which does not deliver for consumers or for the industry, frankly.

Chair: That is a very useful critique of the legislative process.

Nikhil Rathi: I have had a bit of experience with it.

Chair: It was delicately put.

Q779 John Glen: Can I ask you about expertise? We have discussed some really complicated topics. The world is moving very quickly. Are you convinced that the Treasury and indeed the FCA have enough cutting-edge industry understanding of what is happening in terms of some of these changes with AI and in other respects? There is a massive premium to be paid. A number of former officials pop up working for banks at the appropriate time, after a process, et cetera. Does that not mean there is a risk that you do not have the right expertise in the FCA and the Treasury to deal appropriately and swiftly enough with the things that you need to deal with?

Nikhil Rathi: You have three people before you who have all worked in the financial services industry. We are sometimes criticised for that. Johan has vast international experience. He relocated his whole family from Sweden to come and become chief operating officer of the FCA. He used to run the tackling of financial crime for large Swedish institutions, and he has vast experience with money laundering; Jessica worked in fintech, and in financial services beforehand.

One of the best professional decisions I made, notwithstanding that I was doing well at the Treasury, was to step out and get some commercial operational experience to build some of that understanding.

Q780 John Glen: Is that true in the ranks below you?

Nikhil Rathi: Over half our directors have commercial and professional experience outside. I am vastly impressed by the expertise of our colleagues all the way through our organisation on the technical issues, the legal and legislative framework and the technology. We publish a lot of work on emerging technology. Do we have all the expertise we need? No, absolutely not.

Q781 John Glen: When you interact with the Treasury, are you satisfied in the same way?

Nikhil Rathi: That is a really sensitive question.

John Glen: I know, but I have to ask it. That is what we are here for.

Nikhil Rathi: I would say it is very challenging.

Q782 Chair: Let us be honest. There is a high turnover of officials at the Treasury. There is a young cohort that comes in and moves through quickly. It is a legitimate question. You do not need to be too delicate in answering. It is not a criticism of their abilities; it is just the system, partly.

Nikhil Rathi: Part of our role and part of the Bank of England’s role is to assist the Treasury on technical matters, because we are market-facing. We are the people in the system who are supposed to contribute that expertise.

Q783 John Glen: Given that change is going to come from the Treasury Minister to Parliament, and then Parliament is going to ratify it, surely it would be in the interests of our country to have more expertise closer to that legislation-generating function within the Economic Secretary’s office.

Nikhil Rathi: Yes. We are lucky to have an Economic Secretary who has consumer protection expertise, financial services expertise and financial crime expertise. I have been very lucky with previous Economic Secretaries as well.

John Glen: Thank you.

Nikhil Rathi: The Chancellor had a very generous meeting with regulators and the Business Secretary last week. Ministers are really listening.

What is the expertise that I would like to see a focus on? It would be really understanding the operational consequences of legislative decisions and rule making. That is fundamental. The issue in the United Kingdom, in my view, is a deficit of operational execution. Let us not make our lives more difficult at a moment when the opportunities are great and the risks are enormous. We shoot ourselves in the foot time after time after time.

Chair: Legislation does not solve things.

Nikhil Rathi: We need depth of technology expertise, but that is where the AI Security Institute and the AI Economics Institute that have been established are very important. No single Department is going to have that expertise. That expertise is for the entire collective. You need to get the mechanisms right so that it is shared systematically across the system and into Parliament as well.

Q784 John Glen: Are you saying that it could be improved?

Nikhil Rathi: Absolutely, yes. The knowledge sharing, intelligence sharing and data sharing can all be improved structurally and systematically.

Q785 Ms Minns: Can I move us on to non-financial misconduct? You will be familiar with the Committee’s 2024 report Sexism in the City. That found a very stark gender pay gap; widespread sexual harassment, bullying and misogyny across the industry; a broken system of whistleblowing; and widespread misuse of NDAs.

In response to that report, you said you would be prioritising non-financial misconduct. I understand that you published some new rules at the beginning of last month on the issue. Are the FCA’s rules and guidance ever going to be sufficient to change the working culture in this sector?

Nikhil Rathi: No, not on their own. We took the step—it was heavily contested, as the Committee heard—to publish guidance on non-financial misconduct to extend the coverage of our rules beyond the banking sector to a broader population of firms because some of the most severe cases that we had seen were in the investment management industry or in other sectors beyond the banking sector.

We want to be very clear about what our role is. We are not going to be the organisation that can oversee every potential employment grievance in relation to non-financial misconduct. Some of the conduct that is of most serious concern is a criminal matter, which is for the police to investigate.

We did say that this is relevant to fitness and propriety. The way in which these issues are overseen is important and the way in which whistleblowing allegations on these issues are overseen is important, particularly when there can be such an asymmetry of power between the people against whom the allegations are being made and the individuals who are making the allegations.

We have taken a number of cases in this arena, more than any other financial regulator around the world, with significant numbers of prohibitions. If we need to, we are not afraid to go after, based on the evidence, the most senior people in the industry and prohibit them.

No, we cannot do it on our own. There has been other legislation that has come through and come into force, for all employers, around the prevention of harassment. At the end, the tone on this comes from the leadership of organisations.

Q786 Ms Minns: When you published the guidance last month, I understand that you said that it was essentially your policy work on it being brought to a close and you would now be looking at how firms tackled it in practice. What does that look like? What are you looking for from firms?

Nikhil Rathi: We are looking at how they address whistleblowing allegations when they come. It is important to stress that getting a higher number of whistleblowing allegations may not, in and of itself, demonstrate that there is an issue. It might demonstrate that the speak-up culture is working and that previously individuals who felt they could not talk about their experiences are now able to use the systems within firms to do so. We are not automatically going to say, “That is a problem”.

What we want to understand is, when those issues arise, how they are dealt with through the governance of the organisation. We have been periodically collecting data on that. We get our own intelligence coming in, including through our whistleblower channels.

As I say, these are some of the most harrowing and sensitive cases that we deal with, and some of the most difficult work that colleagues at the FCA have to encounter, which gives us some insight into some of the most severe situations.

Q787 Ms Minns: I am glad you brought up your monitoring. Can you tell the Committee how many whistleblower disclosures you have received this year relating to non-financial misconduct?

Nikhil Rathi: I cannot give you the exact number for this year—I would have to check that—but it is typically one or two a month. As I say, all of those are looked at carefully.

I will repeat what I said earlier. Sometimes people come to us as the last resort when they have not been able to get the satisfaction that they wanted from the police or another authority. These can be the challenging cases where, when we look objectively and dispassionately at the evidence, it is not always possible for us to take the kind of action they want us to take. We always seek to explain that, but I am not sure that we are always going to satisfy everybody who comes to us.

The other significant issue that I would just draw your attention to, Ms Minns—it is early days because it has only just come into force on 1 September—is regulatory referencing. This is an issue that was a perennial problem in the financial services sector. When there were known non-financial misconduct concerns about an individual in one firm and they left as a result of a resignation or a settlement agreement, the new employer did not know about the history of this alleged behaviour, or indeed the history of disciplinary action, at previous employers. That now needs to be included in regulatory references, which go from one firm to another, so we can tackle that movement around the industry that was previously going unaddressed.

Q788 Ms Minns: Just quickly, where is sexual misconduct trending? Is it up, down or the same?

Nikhil Rathi: I do not have explicit data. It goes a little bit to what we were asked earlier about data collection. We have to be quite proportionate about the data that we collect and how much we collect. We have done periodic surveys, but we are not doing systematic data collection as part of our standard regulatory reporting on every type of allegation.

We are also not the only authority here, and there is also going to be a new—I cannot remember the precise name in legislation—fair work regulator that will look at compliance with employment law across all employers in the UK. I know this will be one of the areas that they will be looking at. It is important that we do not duplicate what is happening in other regulatory bodies.

Q789 Ms Minns: That is very helpful. I will just move us on to another topic, which is investment platform fees. Your review of disclosure documents for investment products found that only 6% were written in plain English and none of them met the benchmark for intelligibility. The Financial Services Consumer Panel argued that these disclosures should be tested with real consumers. What are the current arrangements for testing disclosures with consumers?

Nikhil Rathi: We do not have mandated rules on testing disclosures with consumers. Different firms will take different approaches. Through our work here and elsewhere, we are increasingly seeking to highlight what is good and poor practice and where testing has led to improvements to encourage others to follow.

Q790 Ms Minns: That would be a no to targets.

Nikhil Rathi: We would not be setting a target for the entire industry on testing with consumers. We have to be quite careful before trying to mandate that. How would we implement that target? Different firms will have different approaches. We want to make sure that there is a degree of flexibility in the system.

Q791 John Glen: Could I turn to CP26/24, the rules around disclosure on cash interest? I have the actual rules for website disclosure, pre-sale disclosure and post-sale disclosure. As I understand it, the proposed FCA rules require firms to disclose the rate of interest paid to customers but not necessarily the amount of interest retained by the platform.

I am personally very supportive of your point that there are trade-offs between risk and reward and so on and an inevitable risk with financial products, but I do feel that there is a practice now, particularly in a higher interest rate environment, where platforms are receiving a higher interest rate on cash within someone’s portfolio and allocating to the customer a portion of that interest, but they are not obliged to be totally transparent about what they retain. They use that retained surplus of interest to cross-subsidise a lower platform fee, thereby giving the impression that they have a lower fee.

Unless these rules are really clear, you are in a situation where different platforms are potentially going to misrepresent the actual fees they are taking. If part of the costs they are taking from consumers is retained interest on the cash element—for some investors, that will be a significant part of their portfolio—we do not really have transparency, do we?

The counter would be that this is more bureaucracy, complication and all the rest of it, but to me this is just a basic transparency issue. Consumers should be able to see what they are actually paying and what interest the platforms are retaining, and therefore stopping them from having. How would you respond to that?

Nikhil Rathi: I know that Dame Harriett is always interested in my file and I was trying to resist accessing it, but on this one I feel I have to because you are going straight to a consultation. The briefing tells me that we are considering carefully the feedback to the consultation and intend to publish by the end of the year. That is not very helpful to you.

Double-dipping is not acceptable. Retaining some interest and then also charging a fee for holding cash is not acceptable. We have dealt with that using the consumer duty.

This issue of disclosure of retained interest is one of the most contentious in the consultation. Opinion is split in terms of the feedback that we have had. When we proposed the rules, we were not convinced that it was particularly going to enhance consumer decision making. What should consumers know? Consumers should know the interest rate they are getting and the fee they are paying for the managed service or the execution-only service they are party to.

We will look at all the feedback. This also ties to wider work that is going on, including some that the Treasury has been looking at, about the incentives for holding cash in some types of accounts and whether the tax incentives are in the right place. We will look at what comes out of that as well, as we think about the final steps.

Q792 John Glen: With sincere respect, I do not think that second point is so relevant. If you have a definition of fees that covers only one form of charging, the platform fee, but does not cover the requirement to disclose the level of charges that are taken through retained cash interest, it is not transparent. That seems like basic logic to me. Just make it clear.

The other thing that they say is that, when some of these big platforms take all their cash, they hold it in different environments, and therefore it is difficult to compute where the interest is at different points in the different spread of accounts. That is a red herring too. In the end, we just want to know about the growth rate, the interest, the fund manager’s expertise and what the fee is to the consumer in cash terms. I will leave it there.

Nikhil Rathi: I see the strength of your perspective.

Chair: “Transparency” is the key word there.

John Glen: Transparency is my motive, nothing else.

Q793 Dame Harriett Baldwin: Can I test the thickness of your folder with a couple of other questions that are unrelated? Have you seen the report from Peel Hunt that says that Britain is “being bought, not built”? It says that, over the last three years, 166 UK firms have been taken over to the tune of £180 billion, and 11 have IPO-ed to the tune of £6 billion. That looks like regulatory failure to me, to some extent. What do you think, Mr Rathi?

Nikhil Rathi: I am aware of that report. On the listings regime, we have reformed radically in the last few years. We have a Listing Authority Advisory Panel, which brings together a number of practitioners. I am not hearing from the market that there is friction in the listing or regulatory regime. When we received feedback on the IPO research window, for example, we moved within months to amend that. We have reformed the prospectus regime as well.

Sometimes with these issues it is quite easy to say that it is regulatory failure. This is a system-wide issue. What drives the pipeline of IPOs? The underlying growth in the economy, the dynamism of the economy, the speed with which businesses are created and the path from private into public. We have established the PISCES regime, which allows intermittent trading. As you know—and it is not for me to comment on them—there are debates around tax and the pensions system.

There is also the realistic issue about the UK’s position in the global economy. Going back 20 or 30 years, UK business was a very significant share of global business. If you look at the top of the FTSE 100 now, most of them will be earning most of their revenues from outside the United Kingdom. Q794 Dame Harriett Baldwin: Is no regulatory change needed at all?

Nikhil Rathi: We have put through enormous volumes of regulatory change. If you are hearing specifics about the listing regime, I would be interested to hear those. Q795 Dame Harriett Baldwin: My second unrelated topic is around the use that you are making of the Cost Benefit Analysis Panel. Have any recommendations come from that panel that have made you think, “Hang on a minute. Why are we doing this? It looks like the costs really exceed the benefit”?

Nikhil Rathi: It does challenge us. For example, you asked about the equity consolidated tape at a previous Committee hearing. Q796 Dame Harriett Baldwin: Are you likely to change anything?

Nikhil Rathi: We will. We are looking at it, and there may well be improvements that we can make.
