Chair: Welcome back. Before the break, we had just heard from our panellists. I will start with you, Mr Croucher. From your perspective, do the Treasury’s tax and fiscal policies provide your sector, the motor sector, with a coherent set of incentives to invest in the transition to a lower carbon and more environmentally sustainable economy while supporting the growth and competitiveness of the sector?

Matthew Croucher: I guess in a word, no, but it is challenging. We have had some positive support from the Treasury. We have DRIVE35, which is set to provide support for the shift to electric vehicles. On the manufacturing side that is welcome and helpful. Then when you look at the cost of doing business in the UK, it is very expensive. Energy costs in particular are very high. We welcome things like the British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme coming along, but that just brings our costs down from exorbitant to very expensive compared with our international competitors.

Then you have measures on the vehicle side. The transition to electric vehicles and healthy markets is critical for the industry to support investment into the UK. On that side, we have seen quite mixed messages. We have had CO2-based taxes for some time, which have been encouraging people to transition. We had zero-rated vehicle excise duty for electric cars up until 2025. We did have the plug-in car grant. That was removed; the market then collapsed. Now we have the electric car grant back in place, which is welcome. About a third of the models in the market get that. We have seen battery electric vehicle demand uptick quite sharply, but a lot of that has really been driven by the manufacturers as well—a lot of product and a lot of discounts going into the marketplace to try to shift it from its natural demand levels.

Then the Budget reconfirmed that we have eVED coming in 2028. That sends quite a conflicted message to the customer that they are going to be paying what has landed as an EV tax. When I speak to my friends and family, they all ask me, “I was going to buy an EV and now I have to pay this new tax on it?” You have to go through and explain to them all about the difference between fuel duty and eVED. They might be paying lower rates for eVED. It just came as the wrong message at the wrong time. That is the real challenge for the sector: how you manage a successful transition when you have a lot of other changes going on in the sector, a lot of competition particularly from overseas, and particularly now Asia coming into the marketplace. How do you ensure that the UK industry is as effective as possible in meeting those challenges?

Chair: For anyone who does not know, your organisation represents not just car manufacturers but people right throughout the motor industry. Presumably, as a representative body you have some who want to see Government doing more to incentivise electric and as a result potentially make diesel and petrol vehicles more expensive. You have others making the opposite case. How do you represent that panoply of views?

Matthew Croucher: I think everybody wants the sector to transition to zero-emission vehicles. All the manufacturers are bringing out product, some quicker than others. Some are looking at intermediary technologies in the first instance rather than fully electric vehicles, but everybody is moving towards electrification and zero-emission vehicles.

In terms of ICE vehicles, we still have a large business in that. As the manufacturers in the UK, most of what we produce is actually for export; 80% of what we produce is exported. Those international markets and their regulations are quite critical in terms of what we will be producing in the UK, but having a healthy domestic market and a Government who understand the industry and enable you to be able to sell what you produce in the UK is still critical, and really critical for investment decisions. That international picture is important with the pace of transition.

Chair: Yes. Clearly, every organisation that comes in front of us would like the Government to tax them less and give them more incentives—we understand that—but realistically, the Government would say electric vehicles, even after eVED, will still be cheaper. The ownership of an electric vehicle will still be cheaper than ownership of a petrol vehicle. We are also conscious that we get lobbying from your industry and others on not increasing fuel duty.

What should Government be doing, accepting that there will be revenues one way or another? Would you like to see them scrap eVED and try to recoup the money from fuel duty? Would you like them to stick with the freeze on fuel duty and accept eVED? If you had to accept the Government are going to take money somehow, where do you want to see it coming as a sector?

Matthew Croucher: I think the Office for Budget Responsibility says that currently there is £24 billion brought in with fuel duty. It is actually set to rise to £27 billion by ’28-29. It would only begin to fall thereafter and by 2030-31 I think it is down to £2 billion less. Fuel duty revenues are not going anywhere in the short term and the eVED comes in before the revenues begin to fall away. That is why we would have called for better engagement with industry and stakeholders to try to design something that was more attractive.

It is really the timing of it. It comes in in 2028. That is when you have the biggest uplift in the ZEV mandate trajectory. It goes from 38%, the battery electric vehicles zero-emission vehicle target, in 2027 to 52% in 2028. You have a 14 percentage point jump. At the moment, the members say natural demand for battery electric vehicles is around 16%. The current market is 26%, so you have to basically double it in a couple of years. That is a costly thing to do to try to transition people. We would have said it is the wrong message—the wrong time to do it.

We certainly think EVs have to pay their way and we understand that completely, but there could have been a much simpler system introduced. We would have personally preferred to have discussion and engagement with Government and then come up with something that changes everybody’s taxation system, so everybody is on the same system.

Chair: I appreciate that. Let me turn to you, Mr McDermott. The starting question is the same. To what extent do the Treasury’s tax and fiscal policies provide your sector with a coherent set of incentives? We are very conscious that colleagues in this place like Gareth Snell are often making the case about the pressures on the ceramics sector. Give us the ceramics industry’s perspective on whether Treasury tax and fiscal policies are providing a supportive environment for the ceramics industry.

Andrew McDermott: Again, the headline answer is no, not a coherent set or an effective policy framework. Tax policy should be making industrial decarbonisation economically attractive, maintaining and growing the UK’s domestic output. There are two sides to that. The first is the environmental aims, and the second is making sure that we maintain our international competitiveness and can invest for the future. A lot of emphasis goes on the former and not enough on the latter. A lot of policies basically drive the environmental aims by adding cost, and that cost then adds to our cost base and makes us less competitive when we are competing in fiercely competitive international markets. Perversely, we can only spend the money once, so if we are spending it on tax, we cannot then spend it on investment in decarbonisation.

Yes, it is a mixed bag. There are pockets of positive developments. I would point to the £120 million industrial decarbonisation fund for ceramics when we had the industrial energy transformation fund—again, positive. It is about supporting that investment case. There are things on capital allowances that are positive. On the other side of the coin, the negatives are high electricity costs—the highest in the world. As a sector, we get very little support on those high electricity costs. Historically, gas was more competitive, but over the last 20 years we have seen us becoming less competitive. Gas prices are high and volatile, and generally we have been becoming less competitive. High carbon prices, the cost of labour, the cost of capital—all these compound together to make a cumulative impact.

Chair: Let me stop you for a second. When you are talking about international competitiveness, which are the key markets that the British ceramics industry is competing against?

Andrew McDermott: We are quite a diverse sector. On the one hand you have tableware, giftware, clay construction products, bricks, and roof tiles. You also have refractories, which are used in steel and glass and cement making. Each facet of the industry probably has a different key market. On the brick side, there is a lot of focus on the domestic market. There we are competing against imports that are coming from the EU, but also from further afield. There the trade routes are establishing, and we are competing against lower cost, higher carbon competitors, so there is not then the level playing field. As our costs go up, you can see the radius of where you can import products into the UK becoming ever more expansive.

Chair: I accept exactly what you are saying about the breadth of the industry and it being difficult to pin down, depending on who you are talking about, but if you were to pinpoint the key aspects of the challenges that you listed there, where would energy and environmental policy rank in comparison with, for example, the cost of labour and others?

Andrew McDermott: The cost of energy is at the top of the pile. As a sector, our energy costs probably account for up to 50% of our total production cost, so just that one chunk is energy and climate.

Chair: You mentioned gas. Given that we have seen gas prices go up across the globe, has there been a reason why in the UK that has had an impact on our competitiveness relative to other countries, which are also buying gas on the same global markets as us?

Andrew McDermott: I guess historically we were more self-sufficient in gas. It came from the North sea. That has transitioned over time. Over the last 20 years, we have seen a slow shift. We were at the cheaper end of the international spectrum and over time we have been moving more towards the middle and towards the wrong end of the distribution. That has been linked to greater exposure to international markets. Having it on our doorstep made it historically cheaper, but now we are sourcing significant amounts of gas from the international markets.

Chair: Mr Berman, these industries are saying that the cost of energy is one of the key factors that they are facing. Just talk us through from your perspective the extent to which you think Treasury tax and fiscal policies are supportive of the energy sector and to what extent they are coherent.

Adam Berman: Yes. I think it is clear from a Treasury perspective that it recognises the economic and industrial potential of the energy transition, but when I look at the gamut of the energy sector—all the different technologies and transmissions through to everything that gets served to customers—historically the supply end of that has been fairly well served by Treasury policy. I look at policies like the contracts for difference, which have enabled a generation of large-scale renewables to come to the market. I could have difficulties with any given policy. I could say it is frustrating that I am not given a long-term auction calendar saying I am going to procure x gigawatts this year and x gigawatts the next year. None the less, over the last few years, over different Governments, we have generally seen a large degree of success when it comes to the supply side.

The demand side is the big open question, particularly when we are looking at a complete rewiring of our economy where any plausible net zero trajectory or climate trajectory calls for vast-scale electrification—electrification as the lion’s share of decarbonisation. That has largely been running in the wrong direction over recent years. We actually need to increase demand now. When I look at, for example, the recent autumn Budget, yes, there was a small reduction in electricity prices, particularly through the cut to VAT for domestic customers, for households, but nothing for non-domestic customers, or very little for non-domestic customers, and significant cuts.

We saw £800 million worth of cuts to the warm homes plan. We saw a cut to a VAT exemption that exists for energy-saving materials, things like insulation, heat pumps, etc. I think we are getting a lot of mixed signals at the moment where on the one side it is clear that the Government recognise the potential of this. On the other hand, they are not only cutting grants to support households to electrify but making the technology needed to electrify more expensive.

I think there is a bit of a left hand/right hand problem going on at the moment. I think the demand side is perhaps not as well understood from the policymaking perspective. Ultimately, it all comes down to energy costs. If we are going to electrify, you can have the best technology in the world, and I am sure other sectors will tell you—I have an EV myself; it is fantastic—ultimately, if the electricity prices are not low enough to foster that generation of electrified technologies, it will not get the take-up that is consistent with either the Government’s own targets or the net zero targets that we have in legislation.

Chair: You say that. The reality is, and the evidence is showing us, that right now electric vehicles are cheaper. I accept on an industrial basis the truth of what you are saying, and we have just heard from two sectors affected by that, but is there not a danger that even when the reality is that the sums add up, the public still are not necessarily convinced?

Adam Berman: You are seeing significant take-up of EVs and increasing take-up, which is positive to see. It is probably the biggest area in which electrification is going well, despite the issues that I am sure colleagues will allude to. The fundamental problem when we look across every bit of the economy that needs to electrify is that the incentives systemically do not stack up.

EVs are a bit of an exception there, but when I look across the heat transition, whether you are a business or a house transitioning to electrified heat, if you look at lifetime costs you can probably get the numbers to add up, but it is a significant outlay in order to get to that point. When I look across industrial electrification, again once you have taken into account the complexity of getting your grid connection soon, which is taking far too long, and the complexity of getting that technology in place, the financing required, and then finally the electricity costs, we are not in the right place.

I think a big missing piece for us in the autumn Budget and in general in Treasury thinking is electrification, particularly when it comes to non-domestic customers, so businesses. Where we have previously had things like the industrial energy transformation fund, which did help with electrification, and we have had proposed schemes like the targeted electrification discount, which never eventuated—it was going to be a targeted mechanism specifically for larger businesses that needed to invest in these technologies but on the operating costs were struggling with it—it just remains a missing piece.

I think that the challenge here is that in any given Budget it will always seem like the cheapest option not to invest in these technologies. If we look at just the last five years, the UK as a whole has spent £100 billion in extra costs procuring gas. We have not received a single extra molecule for that money. What is the cost of not electrifying? That is what is not present in, I think, Treasury thinking and in recent Budgets from, in fairness, successive Governments.

Chair: Yes. In fairness, this Government have invested very substantially in the energy transition. I take your point about that specific area. Each of the witnesses we have heard from so far has effectively said that if Government either spent more or forgo more revenue, we would be better off. Can you point to areas where Government money could be better spent rather than just more spent?

Adam Berman: Just to take electricity bills, as we have been on that, it is very clear that there is a long-term saving by intervening now to lower electricity costs. We have done work on it with the CBI. PwC has done recent good work on it. The economic multiplier of lowering our electricity costs closer to either European levels or G7 median is massively higher than the forgone revenue, but inevitably there is some forgone revenue. I don’t think we can hide from that fact.

Beyond that, the real challenge is that I think we see quite a lot of short-term thinking. The energy transition is a transition. We have had a cut of £800 million from the warm homes plan, for example. We have also had a cut in the technology that people go out and buy as part of decarbonising their home.

If I were Government, I would say, “I know that you want to decarbonise your home. We hope you would decarbonise your home”. You are going to put in a heat pump, let’s say, or a heat network connection. You need to look at the selection of things that underpin that, from the outlay of the actual technology itself for the capital expense to the operating costs, and think what is a glide path? What is a pathway where we can get over the next five to 10 years to a point where we know that we can bring, let’s say, subsidies for heat pumps down over time because we know that electricity prices are also going down?

That is not what we see. We do not see a coherent, long-term pathway toward that. We see small policy changes here or there. If I am a customer—and I have a heat pump because if I am going to talk about this stuff to folks like you, the least I could do is get one—we are still at the point where the sums largely do not add up for a lot of households, at least in the immediate sense. What we would like to see from Treasury is if it thinks that electrification is the long-term future of this country—our view is that that certainly is the case, and I think most other OECD countries are heading in that direction—then we need to have a long-term plan. We have carbon budgets. We have lots of abstract targets.

Chair: I will stop you there because there is other stuff we want to get on to, but thank you for that.

Barry Gardiner: Mr Berman, you spoke about the benefits for gas. The IMF says that we are subsidising our fossil fuels in this country by £59 billion a year. The OECD puts it at a much lower figure, at about £26.7 billion, but it also references the £14 billion for gas that you mentioned. Given that we have signed COFFIS, the Coalition on Phasing Out Fossil Fuel Incentives Including Subsidies, and that we have signed target 18 of the Global Biodiversity Framework, why do you think the Treasury insists that we do not have any perverse subsidies in this country?

Adam Berman: I wasn’t aware that I had alluded to being in favour of gas subsidies. Was that what it sounded like?

Barry Gardiner: No, not to trade on gas subsidies. You talked about the money that the Government had already paid out for being able to rely on gas.

Adam Berman: Right, yes.

Barry Gardiner: That is, of course, a subsidy.

Adam Berman: Yes. In that case, that £100 billion number is not actually the Government paying. That is all customers have paid simply to get the gas that they need for industrial or household purposes. Gas prices have done this over the last five years so there is a direct impact from that. There have also been subsidies, as you say. Arguably, the £100 or £150 that households are getting off their council tax is a subsidy—

Barry Gardiner: My question to you is this. Why do the Government say that they do not subsidise fossil fuels when, in fact, the IMF, the OECD and pretty much everybody else in the world says that they do?

Adam Berman: I am not sure I have an answer to that. It feels—

Barry Gardiner: You are the guys, all three of you, who lobby for subsidies for your industries, quite rightly; that is your job. Now you are quite rightly lobbying for subsidies for the just transition to renewables, clean energy, and that is great. What about the subsidies that were going to fossil fuels that were lobbied for in the past and are still there?

Adam Berman: I can only speak for what we try to talk to policymakers about, which at the moment is to move as quickly as we possibly can to a largely electrified economy with electrified technologies that do not require fossil fuels. In the meantime, when prices are high, like at the moment when we have an international gas spike, yes, the effect particularly on low-income households is such that it is warranted for the Government to intervene. I could see how you could interpret that as a subsidy for fossil fuels, but we are very keen that we move ourselves off volatile fossil fuels as quickly as possible.

I don’t think I am out here advocating for any more subsidies. In fact, where there are de facto subsidies, or at least taxes on clean energy that gas does not receive—for example, on our electricity bills, where we have a disproportionate amount of levies that sit on the electricity part of the bill versus the gas part of the bill—that is a problem that needs to be addressed. That is in many ways a de facto subsidy. I am here trying to tell you that we need to address that problem.

Jonathan Davies: I have some questions for Mr Croucher of the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders. I would just like to step back almost from where we were with the Chair earlier. We have committed, as many countries have, to an electric vehicle future. Would it be the view of your sector that we maybe should have followed the Japanese lead and pushed more towards green hydrogen?

Matthew Croucher: The industry is a bit divided on hydrogen. At the moment, the hydrogen market in the UK for hydrogen vehicles is pretty much non-existent. There have been a couple of manufacturers that have brought hydrogen vehicles to the marketplace, but because there is no infrastructure it is just a zero-sum game.

Jonathan Davies: Do you think the Government should have prioritised the hydrogen infrastructure?

Matthew Croucher: Because the rest of the world seems to have gone much more down the electrification route, I think if we had gone to a hydrogen route specifically and not to electrification, that would have been challenging. We tend to be more saying that we should be aligning with the global direction. Particularly in the UK, we tend to be more aligned with the EU regulations because the global market is almost split into Europe, Japan, the US, and I suppose now China—into those specific regions. Generally, there has been the push towards electrification.

We tend to be a bit more technology agnostic. In the EU, they have more targets around CO2. In the UK, we have the ZEV mandate, which means you have to have zero-emission vehicles, rather than a CO2-based approach. That enables you a bit more flexibility in what technologies you can bring to the market and deliver the savings. That gives you a bit more scope to work with what the customer wants, what the global market wants, etc. If you go down a very specific route, that reduces some of your flexibilities.

I would like to come back to Mr Perkins saying that electric vehicles are cheaper. In terms of the price of the vehicles, it is closing the gap, but there is still a gap. In terms of the running costs, those are only based upon if you are charging at home. If you are facing public charging, then the costs can be prohibitively more expensive. You still have to sell the vehicle at the end of the day, and the residual values of electric vehicles at this point in time are relatively weak. I think when you look at the total picture it is not quite as clearcut as you said, where EVs are cheaper.

Chair: Okay. Thank you for making that case.

Jonathan Davies: If the Government were to flinch, or introduce the full requirements of the ZEV mandate later, what would that mean for your sector?

Matthew Croucher: I think the industry would generally welcome a slowdown in the rate of the trajectory. We have just had the consultation. The SMMT response to that was that we would like to see a move towards the lower options provided because it is a very costly transition. I think everybody is wedded to electrification. The industry, as I said, has brought more than 180 battery electric vehicle models to the market, so there is a huge array of product out there. The consumers are shifting towards it, but at the moment they are still being heavily supported in that transition. If you have a trajectory more in line with where we see natural demand moving towards, then that would mean that it would not be quite so costly to shift towards that.

The concern is that at this point in time if you push the market too quickly, you end up having to potentially reduce volumes or restrict choice to the consumer, and that ends up being more costly and more damaging to the industry in the short term.

Jonathan Davies: I guess this would not necessarily help your members, but do you think the Government should be pushing harder on biofuels and sustainable fuels, e-fuels, which we have seen being used very successfully in motorsport? That would allow existing vehicles to be kept on the road for longer. There is a case that a lot of steel goes into manufacturing new cars. It is a massive carbon emitter as a sector. Lithium mining is a huge problem. Should the Government support that transition? Should they be pushing on biofuels, e-fuels and sustainable fuels?

Matthew Croucher: I think new cars are generally shown to be much more efficient so reduce their carbon—there are carbon offsets compared with vehicles in use—over quite a short period. Could more be done to decarbonise the fleet that we already have there? Yes, certainly so. The industry and the Government are already working on whether or not you can raise the level of biofuels in petrol and diesel and the existing fleet would still be able to run on that. There are concerns that if you raised it too much certain vehicles would not be able to use that because they were not designed to run on that level of biofuel. That could have limitations for either the reliability of the vehicle or the longevity of the vehicle.

There is certainly a lot more that could be done. It is worth remembering that those types of technologies could still be very important in the commercial vehicle sector as well and support on that front.

Jonathan Davies: I need to bring it back to the Treasury since it is the focus of this session, but that is useful information. We know that through the uptake of electric vehicles, which many people celebrate, we have seen a decline in vehicle excise duty because they have had the preferential terms. We will ultimately see a decline in fuel duty coming into the Treasury. The benefit in kind system has also been very generous to company car users. That has meant there has been a hit to the public finances in those respects.

Matthew Croucher: I think vehicle excise duty levels probably have not changed as much as you might have thought. The first year rates doubled in 2025, so that was a significant uplift. Then the standard rate generally goes up by the rate of inflation. There tends to be more vehicles on the road—as you mentioned earlier, vehicles last longer—so you have that element to it.

Jonathan Davies: I guess as a sector you are looking for certainty in terms of what the Government do.

Matthew Croucher: Yes, definitely.

Jonathan Davies: Do you think we have managed to strike a balance of fairness between different groups of motorists as we have tried to maintain revenue while also supporting the transition to net zero?

Matthew Croucher: It is clearly a challenging situation. We understand that fuel duty is set at a level, and it was expected to increase. It has not because there have been a load of external shocks and inflationary pressures on goods being transported, etc. Because fuel duty has been low, as SMMT we do not normally comment on fuel duty. It is not really our thing because it is more like how the general public use their vehicles, etc. But you always have to look at the delta between running an electric vehicle to running an ICE vehicle. If there is support on one side and there is not the corresponding support on the other side, that could create a differential. Those are some of the concerns that we have around eVED at this point in time, where eVED is still set to come in but the fuel duty rates have been frozen and eVED rates are still set as they are.

Yes, everybody wants long-term certainty and for electric vehicles to have that support moving forward, so things like vehicle excise duty and a longer-term vision of where that is going without the big shocks that we had. Company car tax rates are set out for a few years in advance, but they could certainly be set out for longer and electric vehicle rates could be more supportive moving forward.

Jonathan Davies: I will ask about eVED, but I am also very mindful that for many people on a low income, living in fairly ordinary housing, they do not have the benefit of the off-road charging point on their home, and they pay much higher charges to use the public points. I am conscious of time, but one of the reasons the electric vehicle sector has grown so well in the last few years is the hire companies have been very proactive in taking up electric vehicles. You talked about pay per mile. That is going to be a challenge for them, isn’t it, in terms of how they monitor and report those mileages back to the DVLA or HMRC, whoever it will be?

Matthew Croucher: Yes. I think if you have any large organisation with a lot of vehicles, then multiple reporting of information, mileages, etc. will be quite challenging.

Jonathan Davies: How will they do that? Obviously, they do not have an MOT. Will it be voluntary reporting of how many miles a vehicle has done?

Matthew Croucher: The Government changed the rules for the first couple of years so you would not have to take the vehicle in to have a mileage check. That would have been very expensive for the user and inconvenient, so that was a welcome change. After the three years, then you have your MOT so you would have a check then.

Ultimately, the Treasury would get the money for the mileage the vehicle has done. Typically, vehicles are normally accessed on a three-year contract period. In a lot of cases, the first owner would be paying the rates for the first three years on the vehicle. I think what the Government did on that side was pragmatic and quite helpful, but clearly if you have a large business with a lot of reporting to be done, that is going to be challenging.

I know the Government have looked at eVED Connect. They announced in the Budget that they would use the connectivity on the vehicles to do this, but we have not had much insight into how that will work, what information will need to be provided, and how that will align with consumer rights, GDPR and all the rest of it. There are a lot of issues around that.

Going back to eVED, if there had been a lot more consultation and discussion with industry and other stakeholders at the beginning, I think we would have come up with a much smarter and smoother system.

Jonathan Davies: Thank you. I am conscious that we have a lot of questions, so I will leave it there.

Sammy Wilson: The evidence we have had from the Treasury is that the various environmental taxes, carbon pricing, aggregates levy, etc, are designed to influence behaviour especially in energy-intensive industries, leading to changes in the things they invest in and reducing energy consumption. I suppose this is mostly to Mr Croucher and Mr McDermott. What evidence is there in your industry that the taxes that have been enforced on your businesses have actually led to the change in behaviour and investment that the Treasury claims they are designed to lead to?

Andrew McDermott: From our perspective, the added cost makes us uncompetitive. It is pushing us in the direction of decarbonising by deindustrialisation. I think there is too much focus just on territorial emissions, the emissions within the UK’s boundaries. There is not enough looking more broadly than that at consumption emissions. If you factor in the goods that we import into the UK, are we actually seeing overall emissions coming down, or are we just reducing output in the UK?

That production is then just transferring abroad and we are importing it. Often then it is a double loss. It is a loss for UK plc—all the jobs, the output, and the growth from that—but also for the climate too. Certainly, from the perspective of ceramics, we are carbon efficient. Therefore, if we are competing against lower cost, higher carbon production, they may be using coal, oil, tyres, or whatever it might be that they can burn to get the heat. We are doing that much more carbon efficiently using gas or other fuels.

What it boils down to is we see from our perspective there are a lot of targets seemingly being met but that is being met by output loss. Coming back to the first question, policy needs to drive us so that we get decarbonisation, but we stay competitive. At the moment, our costs are going up, and we are just seeing that. We are not seeing enough decarbonisation being delivered by investment and innovation—I would say genuine decarbonisation. There is a lot of costs going up here, output goes down here, and we import product from abroad.

Sammy Wilson: In the case of your industry, is it because, first of all, you have a big export market anyway and carbon taxes are not being imposed in the countries where you are exporting to, and secondly, a lot of the goods that are sold now in the UK are from foreign companies that do not have the same tax burden on them?

Andrew McDermott: Exactly, yes.

Sammy Wilson: What would have to happen then? The Government believe that the taxes, by putting your costs up, should change what you invest in—the technology you invest in. What would have to happen, alongside the carbon taxes, to change your investment decisions and make companies invest in more efficient methods of production?

Andrew McDermott: We need a more level playing field. We have carbon taxes in the UK. We compete against countries where they do not have carbon taxes. They can then undercut us. As it stands, we are in the emissions trading scheme. We get free allocation, which reduces but does not eliminate our carbon leakage exposure. I think where we need to move to longer term is to introduce things like carbon border adjustment mechanisms. Those are complex. It is easy to understand the concept, but operationalising and implementing them is very difficult. The European Commission and the UK Government have kicked the tyres on applying CBAM to the ceramics sector and it is not as straightforward as—

Sammy Wilson: Even if CBAM were introduced for products coming into this country that are competing with the products made here, that would not solve your problem of making it competitive overseas because—

Andrew McDermott: Exactly. That is the downside to CBAM. CBAM works purely on the domestic market. On your export markets there is no benefit; in fact, it makes carbon leakage worse because you start to lose free allocation, which is the mitigation you get for your export products. You are actually making your carbon leakage on your export market considerably worse. It needs complementary measures to do something alongside for your export products, too.

Sammy Wilson: Is it some financial incentives? The carbon taxes are imposed on you. That gives revenue to the Government. Are you saying that for your industry there needs to be some recycling of the money that is collected to give the incentive to do the things that the Government say are required to change investment behaviour?

Andrew McDermott: Yes. We need much more.

Sammy Wilson: Does any of that happen at the moment?

Andrew McDermott: We get bits. We get bits of it, but it is a bit hit and miss. In the EU ETS there is a much clearer mandate for recycling the revenues into supporting decarbonisation. That does not apply in the UK. There are elements; there are bits and pieces. There was an industrial energy transformation fund, but that has now gone. Recently announced was a ceramics sector support scheme, but it is pockets; it is here and there. We need to see on a much broader scale much greater recycling of revenue from carbon taxation to support industrial decarbonisation.

Sammy Wilson: Mr Croucher, is the experience of the motor industry any different from what has been described with the ceramics industry? Are there perhaps dynamics that are different from what happens in ceramics?

Matthew Croucher: It is largely exactly the same. We do reports. We have sustainability reports. We have done it for 25 years plus. We are reporting about performance. You can see that the industry is getting ever more environmentally cleaner and reducing emissions, because that is a cost, so industry has to reduce its costs to be competitive.

There needs to be a fundamental change in the price of energy to make it much more competitive in the UK. At the moment, I think our electricity costs on DESNZ figures were 115% above the EU average. You just cannot compete with those costs, especially as you move towards ever more electricity-intensive production techniques.

Certainly, I would agree with everything Andrew said about there being a lot of money. It is a bit like fuel duty. A large chunk of the price of your petrol is tax, a bit like your electricity costs. I think only a third of the electricity costs pays for the electricity. The rest is all taxes and other costs on top of that. There need to be big changes to make things more fundamentally competitive.

Yes, I definitely agree with recycling more of the moneys that industry pays to support the industry to transition. For our sector, we have some technologies that are challenging to decarbonise. The paint shops typically in a vehicle automotive plant are the largest energy users. They are gas powered. That is what you use to dry the vehicles, etc.

Electrification of those is challenging technology-wise but also cost-wise because electricity just costs so much more than gas. To decarbonise that process is extremely expensive. A paint shop itself is hundreds of millions of pounds and you tend to only invest in them once every 25 years or so. They are big investments, so you have those cyclical challenges. At the moment, the backdrop that we have in the UK does not make it super attractive to do it here.

Adam Berman: The one example where this has worked well is the energy sector. It is just worth highlighting it. If I look at electricity generation, we have reduced our emissions by 80% since 1990. That is in large part due specifically to carbon pricing, a combination of two carbon prices, the emissions trading system and then the carbon price support, which was a sector-specific one for electricity.

I think there are some lessons to be learned there. The first is you need a balance of carrot and stick. It cannot just be stick. I think that is completely reasonable. For the energy sector, we saw sector-specific subsidy mechanisms like the contracts for difference, and the renewables obligation before it, to bring those new technologies to the fore. The signal that you should not invest in the dirty stuff was not strong enough by itself. It needed to be enhanced through other mechanisms.

To the challenge that my colleagues have put around carbon leakage protection and recycling revenues, I think these are good challenges, and I would completely agree with them, but the difficulty here is that we hear a lot from politicians and industries that call for an end to various different taxes, carbon prices, and other mechanisms. I would say that from a decarbonisation standpoint if we are going to take a carrot-only approach to decarbonisation, it will be exceptionally expensive. It will be more expensive than any other country on this planet to decarbonise. You need that balance and we have to make sure that it works for these industries, with support as well so that they can decarbonise where they need to.

Sammy Wilson: I have one last question. The Government obviously have set this as an objective: carbon pricing and environmental taxes to change behaviour but also, at the same time, not to impact on competitiveness and not to detract or diminish investment. What criteria would your industries use to make an assessment of whether or not those objectives on decarbonisation, investment and competitiveness are being achieved?

Matthew Croucher: I think you could have one assessment that could be whether or not the industry is still there and still functioning and still going. We have done a study around international competitiveness. You could look at different metrics to see what is supportive there. On the environmental performance, you have reporting going on there to see where you are moving to, but I think as we have all said, you have to balance everything out and ensure that what you are doing on one side is not meaning that the industry is just disappearing.

Sammy Wilson: Since both of you have given evidence that there has been an increase in offshoring of your businesses, as far as the investment and competitiveness element is concerned the current policies are not working and are actually having a detrimental effect, and the Government are not achieving the objectives that they set out.

Matthew Croucher: I think they could certainly do a lot more. There is still a lot of investment going into the automotive sector. I think in September there was £1 billion announced by three companies in the UK. We have announced £14 billion since covid times, largely to do with electrification and the shift to new products.

There is a lot of investment still going in, but at the same time you have had big announcements that have not happened. You have generally lost manufacturing and volume in the UK quite considerably. That is putting the supply chain under enormous pressure. It has certainly been very challenging. There is a myriad of factors at play here. It is not just HM Treasury policies on environmental taxation that are going on, but certainly some of those have been contributory factors.

Andrew McDermott: From the perspective of ceramics, if we look at our emissions since the turn of the millennium, they have halved. If you delve into the detail of the numbers, then half of that reduction is genuine decarbonisation, and the other half is output loss. A significant amount is being driven by deindustrialisation.

Coming back to the question about the metrics, I mentioned earlier the consumption emissions. We need to have a much greater eye on consumption emissions alongside territorial emissions when evaluating and monitoring policy. We also need to be looking at import data. HMRC import data, certainly across the different ceramic products, are all invariably showing quite rapid increases in the amount of imports. If we compare that to our output, our outputs are going down, and the imports are going up.

It is about looking at import levels, looking at jobs, and looking at public and private investment. I think there are more complementary metrics we can use rather than just looking at territorial emissions alone. Just using territorial emissions completely masks the offshoring picture. It is a massive blind spot.

Manuela Perteghella: My questions are for Mr Berman of Energy UK. Do you think that the current tax and fiscal framework provides effective and sufficiently stable incentives for investment in clean generation networks and other infrastructure needed to deliver the Government’s objectives on clean power, electrification and, of course, net zero?

Adam Berman: When I look at the supply side, it is a slightly better picture for networks particularly. We have a very well-worn road through what is called the RIIO price control framework, which runs through Ofgem and governs the way that that works. We are also seeing more competition being introduced into those areas, which are traditionally monopolies.

Beyond that, generation is a slightly trickier area where we have had these good revenue support mechanisms like the contracts for difference. They have been really successful, but you have seen a lot of chopping and changing every year. It was not that long ago; we are now on to AR8 this year, allocation round 8. In allocation round 5 there was a massive game of chicken between industry and Government, where Government said, “We think you can do it this cheaply,” and industry said, “No, we can’t.” They went ahead anyway and, shockingly enough, no one bid into the process. It has been a slightly messy affair. We have got it back on track but now, even just in the last day, we have heard from one of the Ministers in DESNZ that they are going to be pausing AR9 because they want to have reforms to the programme.

The issue here is there is a presumption from successive Governments that the supply chain and worker base is a rubber band that you can bend and just have snap back when you want to make significant changes. For example, with AR5, where we had essentially a stunted year with no offshore wind being built, you saw workers literally just move out of the UK because there was not enough work for them.

You saw developers being stuck with a significant amount of DevEx. You would have, let’s say, certain offshore payments you had to pay to the Crown Estate just to keep your licence going, even though you did not know whether you were going to bid into the next round, or whether you were going to get a contract. There is a bit of a lack of a pathway to exactly what the Government want. It is not to say that I think the industry will disagree with it, just that I do not know what it is; I cannot see it today.

Beyond that, when I look across the gamut of electrified technologies, I would probably go back to my original comments and just say you can see broad support from the Government. They do get the opportunities that this stuff brings. They get that it takes us off gas at a time when gas is very expensive. At the same time, if I am a customer wanting to get a heat pump, for example, I have to make sure I can get a good, accredited installer.

I have to sort out the accreditation bodies, and I have to make sure it is robust and I have routes to redress. I am probably not going to have the money to buy this stuff outright, so I need a financing mechanism. Just in the last few weeks, we have had some government finance for the first time called the warm homes loan. That has taken two years to come in since this Government were elected. They had previously promised it. I have to make sure that the technology is available. Sometimes I have to get through some planning hoops.

There are lots of pieces to that puzzle. I think the Government and the Treasury are serious about attacking them, but at the moment it is not quite joined up enough that it is making enough of a difference. While we are seeing a slow but sure increase in the uptake of things like the boiler upgrade scheme, which underpins heat pump uptake, we are some distance away from where both the Government and the carbon budgets would like to be.

Manuela Perteghella: I am grateful that you mentioned the reduction of funding from the warm homes plan because I wanted to ask you whether you think the warm homes loan scheme and the boiler upgrade scheme are going to be enough to decarbonise our homes, especially in rural constituencies like mine where I have off-grid homes relying at the moment on heating oil and LPG.

Adam Berman: Yes.

Manuela Perteghella: Of course, we have this £1 billion reduction. What else has to be done on that? What would you like to see done so that we can get people taking up heat pumps and retrofitting their homes easily and motivating them to do so?

Adam Berman: I should make the Government’s case and say there has been a recent increase to the boiler upgrade scheme specifically for off-grid homes, which is fantastic because those are quite low-hanging fruit in terms of decarbonisation. Without a substantive change to essentially the spark gap, the difference between electricity prices, the ratio between them, it is going to be a real uphill battle to get any large-scale decarbonisation of heat. If you look at any graph that shows the spark gap on one side and uptake of clean heat on the other side, you will see that essentially as soon as electricity gets cheaper, people start to put more of these technologies into their home. I think that has to be paramount.

I don’t think there is a massive problem in the subsidy availability, and we are probably in a similar situation sometimes to colleagues on the EV side, where the amount is, I think, very fair. If anything, over time—not yet—we should be decreasing the amount because it is not sustainable to offer £7,500 to every single house in the country, clearly, but I think that there is a problem in that the numbers are so low. At the moment, about 2% of households have a heat pump, for example. The numbers are so low that you have to build a lot of trust in the nascent years of these technologies as you get them out. That is working over time.

To give you an example, I signed up for a scheme run by Nesta where you can visit a heat pump. I signed up so that other people can visit my heat pump. You might think it is a bit silly. I have had three inquiries this week from people who want to visit the heat pump. They are interested in this technology. They want to know about it. It is an unknown. Do you stay warm? Is it expensive? Is there much disruption?

Some of this is in the public understanding and engagement with this issue. Once we have things like electricity prices sorted, we then need to get to a point where you know someone, a friend, a neighbour, or a cousin, who has had a heat pump put in. You know that it is not going to require massive changes in your home. It is going to lead to a better experience, a warmer household. There are not enough people who have them at the moment that we have that widespread cut-through with the public, if I am being really honest.

Manuela Perteghella: That takes me to my next question. The Budget announced several measures to reduce energy costs, including changes to electricity policy costs and gas standing charges. What impact might these have on incentives to switch from gas to lower carbon technologies, to get a heat pump, to get the warm homes loan and to decarbonise your home? What further action can the Treasury take to make electricity more competitive?

Adam Berman: Broadly, what they did on electricity prices is welcome. They removed or continued the removal of VAT from household electricity bills. They also did this change to the gas standing charge, where essentially there are a bunch of costs that are recovered through the standing charge on gas. That is going to be moved to the unit rate. That will be a process that will, I imagine, take a year or two.

It is not without its complexities, I should add, because what is essentially going to happen is that the unit rate for gas becomes higher. Often high gas usage households cannot help it: it is a large family with a badly insulated home; it is an immunosuppressed household that has to have the heating on the whole time. While I am generally supportive—and the sector is generally supportive of that move on the gas standing charge—most people will be winners but there will be some losers, and the impact will be quite significant for them. What we did not see as part of the Budget was any attempts from the Government to say, “We’re going to try to help those people who lose out. We know there will be some losers. Every policy comes with pros and cons; we all understand that. There will be some distributional consequences. We are going to get better at our targeting to make sure we can get help to those households.”

All things being equal, those two mechanisms should shift the electricity to gas spark gap in favour of electrification. There are some longer-term measures around trying to get bills under control and things like that, which are all welcome. These are all things affecting costs that might have arrived on your electricity bill, not costs that are currently there today. We still have further to go, and I completely recognise the constrained fiscal circumstances of the Government.

All I would say is that the mechanism that they are now using to provide £100—I think it is—to households within certain council tax bands is expensive. The amount that people have to pay for increased gas bills is incredibly expensive, and largely electrification is still seen through the lens of “This is a cost. It is new infrastructure. It is an investment.” It is an investment, but the payback is significantly larger than the investment itself. The electricity changes they have made will help, but they are more of a first step, I would say, on that journey.

Manuela Perteghella: The Government have approved the Jackdaw gas field and announced this new energy wealth fund. Can revenues from fossil fuels be ringfenced and used to finance the transition away from fossil fuels themselves or does that create tensions between the Government’s climate energy security and their fiscal objectives?

Adam Berman: That is a very good question. I do not think it creates tensions. I would certainly be supportive of hypothecating any revenues that come from the upstream oil and gas extraction in the North sea and using them—well, we do not have any clarity on how they are going to be used, but using them for some part of the decarbonisation process or, indeed, for the just transition process in places like Aberdeen to help people transfer from the oil and gas sector to offshore wind and things like that.

If I am being really honest, these are probably relatively small sums in the context of the entire energy transition. They are welcome, but the amount of investment that is required now is staggering. It is largely happening, but this is private capital. If the Government were to pay for the entirety of the net zero transition themselves, it would be like an additional defence budget that they had added on. We know that 70% or 80% of the costs of decarbonisation will be borne by the private sector, and that is how it should be. In terms of the tension there, my understanding is that specific criteria were used for the Jackdaw approval. I imagine those same criteria—balancing out emissions, energy security, the economic impact, etc—will be used for Rosebank or for any future projects, so I won’t comment on those.

I do not think that it necessarily incentivises them, not least because of the two fundamental things that essentially stop any large-scale projects beyond what is currently within the licensing pipeline: the ban on future licences and the taxation regime for oil and gas. The UK, for better or worse, is not conducive to investment in upstream oil and gas extraction, particularly for new licences, even if you could get them, which you cannot. I do not particularly think those two things are in tension. If we are going to get money from fossil fuel extraction, absolutely let’s use it to decarbonise our economy.

Manuela Perteghella: The energy profits levy is due to come to an end by 2030, and the Government are proposing to replace it with the oil and gas revenue levy. Would that provide greater investment certainty in the net zero transition?

Adam Berman: Yes, it would. Certainly, the way the EPL works would mean that moving to the oil and gas price mechanism would enable higher certainty. I think probably if you go and talk more on the specific upstream side, they will still say that they will have some issues with it. But yes, because of the way that it kicks in, that it is a 35% tax above a certain threshold—which I think is $90 a barrel or 90p a therm on gas in 2026-27—that seems like a reasonable place to end up.

Ultimately, regardless of the tax element, I think any Government need to decide whether it is in the best interests of the UK to have these projects for energy security reasons, for industrialisation reasons, and for economic growth reasons. If they decide that it should have them, then you should have a taxation regime designed to be able to attract investment. If they do not want that investment because they think it is not in the best interests of the country, then of course you just tax it to nothing. It feels like we are in a bit of a middle ground at the moment where we still have a taxation regime that essentially says we are not interested in any future large-scale oil and gas extraction. That is a legitimate policy aim; it is just that we have perhaps heard some slightly mixed signals.

Chair: Did you want one final comment on that, Mr McDermott?

Andrew McDermott: I was just going to add a quick comment from an industry perspective on electricity costs. There is a lot of ambition there in terms of electrification, but the environment is not conducive to investing in electrification technologies. As a sector, very few of our member companies get benefits under the British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme, BICS. That would reduce electricity costs by 25%. Only a handful of our members get that. Only a handful get British Industry Supercharger, which drops it by 50%. Because of that, the economics of electrification do not stack up. It is a key decarbonisation avenue for us, but the economics simply do not stack up at the moment.

Chair: Thank you very much for making the Committee aware of that. We will certainly include that in our evidence. Mr Croucher, Mr McDermott and Mr Berman, thank you very much indeed for an excellent, informative session.
