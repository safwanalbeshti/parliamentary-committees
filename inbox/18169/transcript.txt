Examination of witnesses

Andrew Carter, Stephen Jones and Councillor Vince Maple.

Q130 The Chair: Hello, and welcome to the ninth evidence session of the Economic Affairs Committee inquiry into fiscal devolution in England. We have three excellent guests with us today: Andrew Carter, chief executive of the Centre for Cities; Stephen Jones, director at Core Cities UK; and Councillor Vince Maple, chair of Key Cities and leader of Medway Council. Thank you for being with us today. I should state at the beginning, this session is being broadcast on Parliament Live TV, and a full transcript will be taken and made available shortly after for any corrections of fact.

Without further ado, we have quite a few questions to get through and will get straight into it. First, do you expect fiscal devolution to boost economic growth? How strong or otherwise is the evidence for this? Related to that, do you expect devolution to prove equally beneficial to all areas?

Andrew Carter: There is some evidence to show that more fiscal devolution supports economic growth; work done by the OECD highlights this. We see that wages are slightly higher in places that have more devolution and GDP per head productivity is also slightly higher. But it is important to say that it is a mixed picture.

To get a little nerdy almost immediately, causation is a problem. We cannot definitively say that one thing causes the other and so we should be relaxed to a degree about the matter. We cannot claim that this is the thing that will revolutionise or transform the performance and make-up of our economy, but we should think of it as one of the interventions that will give more places more reasons to support growth. That would be my observation.

My other point is this. The status quo—as I am sure you have heard over time—which is highly centralised, does not seem to work very well for a great number of places. When we think about what else to do, we should be mindful that what we are currently doing is considerably less than perfect.

Stephen Jones: Can I pick up from that last point? The real difficulty is working out what the counterfactual is; that is true of all the international evidence. We are trying to capture the marginal benefit of decisions being made locally rather than nationally, rather than asking whether some places grow faster than others. Often, those things are too conflated in the discussion. A lot of attention is rightly placed on Greater Manchester, having had a successful first quarter of this century. How much of that was related explicitly to the devolution journey versus changes in the sectoral mix and the overall macroeconomic position? Equally, in places where growth has been sluggish, that is not necessarily a consequence of decisions made locally.

Where the evidence is more sophisticated, as Andrew started to point to, is when think about what the factors are. What is the ability of local leaders? Can they respond more quickly? Are they more aware of what is going on? Are they able to respond to what they hear from their business community? Can they hold relationships with their research institutions? The active process is where the evidence is stronger when it comes to impact.

Will devolution have equal outcomes across all the country? No, but as Andrew says, we do not have equal outcomes across the country anyway. It is a matter of what the relative improvements are and where we think there is good evidence, but it is really hard to prove it.

Councillor Vince Maple: To add to the excellent contributions from my colleagues, in one sense the answer is, “No, but”. It is not the act of fiscal devolution itself that will lead to growth; it is better local decisions leading to better investment into communities, better conversations with public and private institutional organisations, and the ability to make sure the conversation about a particular area is not dealt with by somebody in SW1, who I am sure does a great and laudable job, but the ability to know a place cannot be underestimated in those conversations.

It is also about preventive approaches: who can see the truck heading towards you? It is the local communities, first and foremost. It is important to have direct funding available to you and the methodology behind that, but, again, no one thing by itself will result in fiscal devolution leading to growth.

The other thing—Stephen spoke about this—is that we are not starting with a blank piece of paper where every community is the same. Some communities do very well today without fiscal devolution, and growth is happening; there are other places where that is not the case. Things such as deprivation are long-term, systemic issues. Fiscal devolution may help to pull some levers more quickly, and that would be a good thing, but that by itself is not enough. There are much wider issues to be tackled, and ultimately communities want to tackle them; they want to move on, but sometimes those challenges can feel too far away. Having some of those financial levers closer to home will help those conversations as well.

The Chair: Following on from that, you pointed to other reasons why you may pursue devolution, such as local accountability or improving service provision. Do you perceive those as complementary to a growth-driven form of devolution, or are there trade-offs potentially involved in what your aims might be with devolution and how you might then pursue it?

Councillor Vince Maple: We may speak about this later on, but, as we speak, councils are meeting to discuss the issue of planning and the balance between a strategic overview on planning, and the economic growth that can often come from good planning decisions, and the ability of locally elected members and local residents to have a say in their community. There is a tension between those two, which we are working through at the moment. As I say, in real time, there are councils meeting this week and decisions are being made around the structures of their local governance processes for planning. That is a good example from my perspective. Certainly a number of Key Cities members feel that perhaps the discussion around devolution, in its broadest sense, is moving powers away, rather than bringing them more closely to the community.

Andrew Carter: At this moment in time, we are talking about fiscal devolution in England in relation to the mayoral strategic authorities and not local authorities. That is a critical point to make, because a lot of the conversation conflates those two issues. When thinking about service provision, there is a degree of universality in our expectations to do with common delivery elsewhere, which by definition is not true when it comes to MSAs. Think about bus services. There is no argument that we should have comparable bus services in all parts of the country, but buses are potentially critical for some MSAs. Thinking about service provision and uniformity across them applies slightly less, in some respects, to the MSA model than it does when we get into what local authorities do. That would be my first point.

Secondly, giving responsibility to the mayors and strategic authorities for services that the public think they are already responsible for actually enhances accountability, awareness and transparency. We did work on this, polling the public in different places that have mayors, asking, “Do you know that you have a mayor? Can you name the mayor? Can you tell us what they are doing?” Recognition, awareness, understanding and engagement with mayors is materially higher in those places relative to the council leaders or to the local MPs.

Think of understanding and awareness—I know that accountability is much broader than that—as part and parcel of whether the public know what is going on. Over time, places with mayors have proved demonstrably that their public know them better than they necessarily do other public leaders in their area. There is a relationship between giving the mayors increasing responsibility for things and that inducing, informing and developing, as it should, stronger forms of public awareness.

Then we have related questions about holding them accountable for the various things that they do and the appropriate format for that. Should it happen at the local or national level? The answer is probably a bit of both, but those are details to get into.

Stephen Jones: To your question about complementarity or trade-offs, the answer is probably both. There are certainly lots of examples of things that we at Core Cities are keen to do with our respective strategic authorities to improve service delivery, in particular joined-up service delivery in our cities, around housing, mental health, worklessness, education and skills. If we can join up those services and deliver a better offering to the individual, will that boost growth? Probably. It is more likely to get people out of long-term sickness and worklessness and into new employment opportunities.

As Councillor Maple said, there are definitely options where there will be trade-offs. Purely in a budgetary sense, do we spend more money on X or on Y? Do we invest in the long term versus the short term? Do we think more about prevention or dealing with acute? In all those challenges that all Governments face, there are naturally trade-offs between the services you offer and your long-term growth strategy.

The opportunity of fiscal devolution is, first, that it builds a feedback loop into those choices, more so than currently is the case. If a particular area does not choose to invest in its long-term growth potential, it will not necessarily see the proceeds to increase tax revenues. There is that feedback and that circle, which we think is critical.

Secondly—this goes to Andrew’s point about accountability—on the choices made by mayors and elected councillors, if they prioritise one thing over another, or they put forward a manifesto that says, “We’re going to spend our flexible budgets on this activity and not that activity”, people can choose whether they want that. That is fundamental and at the heart of what we are trying to achieve in devolution, in particular with the fiscal dimension.

The Chair: You have expertly moved us towards our second question from Lord Young.

Q131 Lord Young of Cookham: Can I follow up the answers you gave to Baroness Penn’s first question about whether fiscal devolution leads to economic growth? Is what is on the table actually fiscal devolution, or is it simply redistributing central government grant but using a different formula?

Andrew Carter: It is more than that, but I understand why you ask the question. Essentially, we will remove government grant from all or some of the MSAs—I am sure we get into that. In the first instance, that will be replaced with a share of income tax, as you know. In some respects, you could rightly say that this is a degree of replacement of one form of money over the other. I understand that, but I would suggest that, due to the nature of the money that then is available, if their place does well and begins to grow, because it is related to a tax base or bases that are growth-related then they will get more money to invest and spend on different services.

That is not how the system currently operates. Most MSAs get a form of grant from government—one year, three years, whatever it might be—that is relatively fixed. In some respects, their performance during the period of one to three years bears no relationship to the next grant negotiation that they go through. The Government, whoever they might be, then decide what the next round of funding is. In this newer system, there will be flexibility and autonomy so that, if the tax base grows, they will get receipts from that tax base to allow them to invest in the ways they want, and will be less beholden to negotiations with government about exactly how that money is spent.

We should think about the terms and conditions that come with the money, as much as the nature of the money itself.

Lord Young of Cookham: Does it not flow from that that they will not know for two or three years whether they do indeed have any more money? In the short term, what effect will it have?

Andrew Carter: When we get into the process of planning it out, the programmes offered are essentially over a rolling period of two, three or maybe four years, and they are getting the money from that. Our proposal, in the first instance, in the current spending review system, is to take the integrated settlements, where we know what that money is for the next two to three years, and replace it with an initial share of income tax—we think corporation tax, but we can get into that as well. That gives certainty over that period. If you give them a share of their income tax that is broadly comparable, they get certainty for the next two to three years, and then they can think about what happens after that.

Ultimately, you are making judgments about whether your tax base is growing or not. Look, as we have, in detail at the tax base of some MSAs. There has been variation, but they have all grown over the last 10 to 20 years in nominal terms. You get some confidence and certainty. Year on year there will be vagaries, but that is also true of the way that MSAs and local government are currently funded.

Lord Young of Cookham: The other two might like to have a go at answering my supplementary question.

To what extent will you be able to distinguish the extra efforts that a local MSA may have made that increase revenue from the overall performance of the economy, which has, I would argue, perhaps a bigger effect on income tax than anything an individual MSA might do?

Councillor Vince Maple: There are a couple of parts to that. First, it is worth recognising—I say this in my capacity as leader of Medway Council—that there are lots of parts of England still without an MSA. I know that is a long-term ambition. There is a question mark around how to ensure parity between those with and those without. We will probably get into that as we go through this afternoon.

You pose an interesting point. We have talked about Manchester already today. It is not a member of Key Cities; it is a Core Cites member. Rightly, it has said time and again that it has had the fastest-growing economy out of all the major regions. It is an interesting counterpoint to say, “That is great, but how much of that is because of the national growth?” People with far greater economic brains than mine will probably say that this bit is definitely down to a local decision and that bit is definitely not and this other bit might be.

There will be tests and challenges as we go through this. It will be reasonable to ask whether it is around the local GDP, employment rates, tackling NEETs, quality of housing, or all those things and more. The answer is, probably.

I am a fan of KPIs and think they are a good thing. The challenge will be that you could have an almost unlimited amount of KPIs to ask whether fiscal devolution is better or worse for a community. It will be better but by how much? I accept that it is difficult to tick, say, five boxes to show that this has made it better than if you had not ticked those five boxes or had made five different decisions”.

Lord Young of Cookham: You cannot impose an overnight visitor levy, is that right?

Councillor Vince Maple: I definitely cannot, at time of speaking.

Lord Young of Cookham: Is that a disadvantage from your point of view compared with what other authorities can do?

Councillor Vince Maple: Certainly. Not all 22 members are within MSAs. The previous chair was from Salford, but the majority of Key Cities members are not currently in MSAs. However, they are from big, important communities, such as Bournemouth and Plymouth. They play an important role in our country’s economic regeneration as we move from a point of austerity, and they also places where people visit. I suspect those council leaders, often but not always unitary authorities—that is another part of this conversation, certainly from a Key Cities perspective—will say that every part of England should have access to those powers. I am very happy for the default to be that if it is an MSA then it sits with the mayor. There is always a conversation about local government as well.

Q132 Lord Prentis of Leeds: My question is about equalisation mechanisms. I understand the conviction that fiscal devolution may well boost economic growth. As Andrew said, it is one of the interventions. I also understand that, if you redesign the fiscal system, others will argue there has to be an equalisation mechanism. It may be for some of the reasons you have already given—they may not have the revenue-raising powers that another authority will have; there may be underperformance; it may well be the cost of providing public services. Equalisation is an inherent part of the discussion when talking about fiscal devolution. My question is very simple: what sort of equalisation mechanism would you propose to assist devolved authorities which have only limited capacity to innovate or to promote growth?

Andrew Carter: We should start from the premise that all MSAs, to varying degrees, will have some ability to see and promote growth more than others. The advantage of doing it at the MSA level is that, in itself, it provides some form of aggregation and equalisation across a bigger geography. That is an obvious statement. There is no reason to start from the position that any MSA will not be able to promote growth, but we should also accept, understand and then deal with the fact that some will grow more rapidly than others—some because they do things deliberately that might enhance growth even at the margins, and others because they will be the beneficiaries of legacy decisions that they ride on the back of. That is an important first point to make.

Secondly, we should think about redistribution within the system but not necessarily fundamental and basic equalisation. We do not have an equal system now; no one can claim that to be realistic. We have to ask ourselves whether we can create a system where there is some variation—as there is variation already—that encourages all places to do better than they currently are and become better versions of themselves, which importantly offers enough incentive in the system for places to make decisions about growth. I think we can do that.

We have examples from elsewhere. We looked at the Swiss style of doing things. Essentially, you set a benchmark, threshold or baseline, whatever you want to call it, and, when places grow more rapidly above the benchmark, through different mechanisms you take a proportion of the growth—not the base—put it into a pot and allow that to be redistributed to places that are growing less quickly or less strongly.

We do this in other formations, for example, in part through business rates, although it is devilishly complicated, and your specialist adviser can give you chapter and verse on that. But it is not impossible. In fact, it is entirely possible to have a system that encourages, rewards and supports growth but also provides revenue from those that are doing particularly well. Let us be honest and clear here that what we really mean is London. London will provide enough surplus to compensate for lower growth—it is still growth—in other places, to the point that you can say that we will not let any place drop below a threshold of, say, 90%. That is relatively straightforward. That is what the Swiss do and it seems to work there. That is what we are advocating here.

There are decisions to be made about where you start, but these are not fundamentally impossible things to reconcile. That is important.

Stephen Jones: To build on Andrew’s points, which I agree with, there are two things I would add. First, there is a difference between what the funding is for and what services or actions are provided with that money, and how important equalisation is in that regard.

If you are delivering care services or universal services, equalisation in the funding model is quite important because people need those services no matter where they are. Often, you then need to build deprivation metrics into the way those indices are played out.

If you are talking about the investment side or growth side, the reality is that different parts of the country need a higher or lower level of that fixed money. A metro public transport system requires more money than if you do not have such a light rail system. If you are generating growth, you will then need to reinvest to build more housing.

Depending on the function of the funding, that will determine how important the equalisation dimension is. There will always be some—you do not want to create vicious and virtuous circles—but you will probably have a little more tolerance for equalisation on the economic investment side than with universal services.

The second point I want to highlight is this. If you are designing an equalisation model, how do you build in something that is predictable and ongoing rather than something that is time-stamped? One difficulty is that, where there are reset moments—in five years we will look at this and rejig it and then start again—that removes huge amounts of long-term incentives. We are talking about investment decisions that we want places to make over five, 10 and 20 years, particularly where that funding is, we hope, used to leverage private capital to have a revenue stream against. If you build an equalisation model in that space that ultimately feels like a spending review, you remove one of the key opportunities of that long-term certainty. However, designing for equalisation at a 5% top slice each year or a set floor target in the way that Andrew described, you can build into your modelling something that reduces those risks and the dampening of the incentive and certainty. That is one of the key arguments.

Councillor Vince Maple: One of the things to recognise, as Stephen touched upon, is the importance of not leaving things for ever. At the moment, councils up and down the country have to deal with a council tax system based on evidence from nearly four decades ago. That is not in anyone’s interest, but that is where we are. That means that, ultimately, any review will be much more difficult than if it had been done after five or even 10 years. Key Cities is supportive of using indices of multiple deprivation. We see that used in calculations such as the fair funding formula for local government.

It is about trying to be fleet of foot as well, recognising that, even if you create a system today, in five or 10 years that system may need quite fundamental review because the world has changed quite a lot, and so service provision and service requirements have changed.

For example, we are having a national conversation about adult social care. Whatever the conclusions, that almost certainly will play into this conversation as well. We need to have systems, including those for equalisation, that are fit for purpose both on day one and day 10,001. We also need to recognise they do not exist in isolation; other things are happening. Service delivery, either through MSAs or unitary authorities or other local government bodies, needs to recognise that as well.

Lord Prentis of Leeds: All three of you recognise the need for an equalisation mechanism. What if the shortfall in a devolved authority was caused by bad decision-making or underperformance? Would you still move money from the growing authority to the one that is in difficulty?

Andrew Carter: That would be a judgment for the system to make: to identify where there are shortfalls, however we define those, and then to understand the reasons behind them. If the conclusion of some due process is that there is unlawful behaviour, or whatever it might be, then that place should be compensated for it. But if due process has been followed and bad investment decisions have been made, places need to accept that there will be a funding implication on account of that.

If we went into a large-scale economic shock such as a recession and/or a Covid-related event—let us hope not—and the economy shrinks quite dramatically, we already have stabilisers in the financial system to compensate for those kinds of things. We can deal with those shocks as and when they arise because we have systems in place to deal with all of that. We are not trying to design a system that can deal with all those things internally, because that is just mad, and we do not do that anyway. We need to have a system that, all things being equal and in normal circumstances, creates the conditions that we want, accepting that sometimes things come along that need extra special interventions.

Can I make another point? It connects to a question that Lord Young and Lord Prentis asked. My conversations with MSAs, particularly leadership, both politician and officer, tell me that they are prepared to accept and embrace some degree—“some” is doing a lot of work there—of uncertainty on funding because the flexibility that they will get through the new system, and the potential upside, more than compensate for the concerns that others might have around some of that uncertainty. That is the decision that we are considering. The MSAs I talk to say, “Yes, we are not blind to this situation, but we accept it and we want to move forward never the less”.

To Lord Young’s point, this is where we probably need to test this out, through running it over a period of two to three years in places where we have some understanding of the funding, and then see what happens. After that, we can take a pause and make a judgment. We will need to do because there will be a lot more MSAs, and more variation within the MSA community as more players come in.

Stephen Jones: To pick up on your question, it is an important point for how we design the system. We will see what comes out of the Budget next week.

The lesson I would draw on is this. When we were designing the Manchester earn-back model, in 2011-12, which fed into the gain-share model that most MSAs have, that was originally conceived of as a payment-by-results model, and Greater Manchester was going to get up to £30 million per year for 30 years, subject to five-year review points around whether it had met various agreed targets. Before the first five-year milestone was met, it became evident that the quality of the metrics that were set could not disentangle whether activity was due to the decision-making of Greater Manchester or to lots of other external factors. Quite quickly, that up to £30 million subject to five-year reviews translated into—as Lord Houchen gave evidence to this committee—we have £30 million, or whatever the Tees Valley pro rata equivalent is, for 30 years.

There is a need for pragmatism. Can you truly design in value judgments over the decisions that are made—positive and negative decisions—vis-à-vis what is happening in the wider economy, or do you accept the fact that there is some form of blurring and compromise for other benefits that talked about?

Councillor Vince Maple: A real-life example of the question you pose is where local government funding has gone over the last seven or eight years and exceptional financial support. MHCLG—the clue is in the name—started, it would be fair to say, on a cross-party basis. The first to access it were those who made bad political decisions. I think most people in local government would agree with that. We are now in a position where the majority of those accessing exceptional financial support are just dealing with the day-to-day, and there is not enough resource to deal with that.

Flipping that to where you get to with an MSA, you might say here are some expectations but, if there are challenges and difficult decisions, and the local and regional government and the community say that it was not a very well made decision, it should not be the residents of the place who suffer.

That is the potential challenge. It is about having those MHCLG-style safeguards and recognising that as an example. The tool was originally made for people making bad political decisions, and that was the case for the first councils that had to access it. Now, that is a tiny amount of the proportion of people accessing it.

Q133 Lord Razzall: I would like to bring us on to different levels of responsibility and powers. We have central government, the mayor and strategic authorities—which now seem to be rolled together into the acronym “MSA”—and we have different levels of local government. The Government have clearly decided that major devolution will go to the MSAs. What do you think of that, particularly in the context of the current mess in local government reorganisation, or its postponement, which I know is going to come up in a subsequent question? What level of responsibility should be given to local authorities, particularly in the context of the mess that we are now faced with? The three of you are clearly pro devolution to MSAs, but can you think of any disadvantages of powers being given to MSAs rather than retained in central government?

Andrew Carter: The original guiding principle for the MSAs—they were called combined authorities back in the day, but we move on—was that we should have some form of political geography that is coterminous with the economic geography of a place. That was the original principle behind Greater Manchester. If you look at its economy, it is broadly cognisant within the 10 boroughs; it is slightly larger for higher-skilled travel to work on the south side, but there is a degree of coterminosity. You want an institution that can make economy-related investments and decisions that are broadly comparable to that geography.

When you take that principle, which is still a solid one, and think about the economy-related issues of strategic transport and planning, skills, and the interaction between all those things across the geography in which everyday life is lived, then, yes, they make perfect sense in that regard.

Lord Razzall: What about local authorities though?

Andrew Carter: That is a different question.

Lord Razzall: Particularly where we do not have MSAs.

Andrew Carter: We have tried in various ways through different periods of policy to deal with that problem. We had RDAs back in the day, which were an attempt to deal with the economic problem relative to local authorities that were, invariably with few exceptions, too small to do economic planning sensibly and appropriately. Then, in a similar vein, we tried the local enterprise partnerships, and they lasted as long as they did.

There has always been an attempt to grapple with the fact that, from an economic planning perspective—I am making an important distinction there—many local authorities are too small to do that sensibly, because 50% of people live in one local authority and work in another. You cannot plan transport systems at the borough level. You have to do it at a strategic level. If you have that in mind then the MSA idea, and the geography, to most degrees makes perfect sense.

Stephen Jones: With the Government’s rewiring the state delivery plan, which they intend to publish later this year, there is a huge opportunity to move to a way of describing a system that works up and down the vertical channel. There are, broadly speaking, four tiers of government: central government, the strategic authority tier, the local authority tier, and the community neighbourhood tier. No matter which policy area you talk about, all four tiers have a legitimate say and role, not just in delivery and implementation but in decisions, policy, and strategy. You need to design a system where they talk to each other. That is where there is a need for some principles.

The conversation I am currently having with civil servants is that we should not try to produce a NEETs table with one row for what, for example, the strategic authority tier does. It is about principles and how that framework will work. How do you build subsidiarity into decision-making? How do you build in accountability and alignment of incentives? For any given place in the UK, if the community wants one thing for the economy and the local authority wants another, the mayor wants something else and the national Government want something else, then that place will get stuck and will not go anywhere. How do you recognise that all four tiers have equal status, importance and stake in that decision?

We can design that system. It is not the one we currently have, but that is the challenge. If we are to rewire the “state”, we need to build into that thinking what this means for the delivery of health and care services, transport and so on. The balance between those four tiers will look slightly different. Some places will lead and some places will follow, and vice versa.

Lord Razzall: It will always follow the money. If money is given to the MSA, clearly that reduces the capabilities of the other authorities.

Stephen Jones: Indeed, but, equally, MSAs are not dictatorial organisations. We might have dropped from their name the term combined authority, but their governance is still made up ultimately of a mayor and the leaders of the constituent authority councils. Decisions need to be passed through a majority. There is still a sense of the structure and system that we would look to design.

Equally, we in Core Cities are very conscious of the fact that, within the constituent authorities that our eight English cities sit within, our city councils are hugely important economically and for where we have the capability, relationships, design, structures and assets. The money is one part, but other dimensions to do with legitimacy, relationships, structures and assets are also part of the system that we can contribute to.

Lord Razzall: It is easy to call them MSAs and combined authorities, but the mayor of Teesside was very clear that the money should be given to him and not to a strategic authority. There are two different levels of power there, in addition to the others.

Councillor Vince Maple: To use a wonderful cliché, the power should sit where it needs to sit. On local regional government structures, we have mentioned the pause, but the Government are clear that they want to move to everybody being a unitary authority. I support that as a principle. Key Cities is certainly not opposed to that.

It is worth recognising today that, in some areas, both with and without any form of combined authority, it is the districts and boroughs—places such as Exeter and Norwich, which are not upper-tier authorities—that we need to hear from. They need to be part of the conversation. I say that as a leader in an upper-tier authority. In my region, having places such as Canterbury and Dover would, for obvious reasons, make sense, but we are where we are with that.

We talked earlier about fiscal devolution. It has to empower people and communities. The Minister of State has been pretty clear on that: devolution is not just down to councils and mayors; it is down to back-bench councillors and communities. Pride in Places is a good example of a programme that does exactly that.

It is also about recognising that one size does not fit all. What will work for a coastal community in the south-west will be very different from what will work in an urban area in the north-east. Trying to have a system that helps and supports both those communities in their wider regions is tricky. That is why this conversation has probably taken longer than we all would have wanted—decades, realistically.

Lord Razzall: What about the disadvantages? Can any of you think of any?

Stephen Jones: Do you mean of the mayoral model?

Lord Razzall: The disadvantages of giving power to the MSAs rather than retaining it with central government.

Stephen Jones: The interaction with business is interesting, particularly those businesses that operate across the whole of the UK or globally.
