Chair: We will come to that, don’t worry.

Mr Falconer: On your broad question, at the outset, and on whether the Government think we need to move closer to our friends in Europe, yes, we do. We made progress on Friday. We discussed yesterday in the House the elements in relation to tuition fees, economic security and the so-called Made in Europe agenda. We also set out a commitment to negotiating on the internal electricity market. There is a set of issues—

Chair: I understand that, but that wasn’t what I asked. Chair: Okay. Let me ask—you may not be able to answer this—about the wider relationship, outside the specifics that we are talking about negotiating. The Prime Minister has said that he wants to set out some form of conversation that we are going to have with the European Union and with the British public. How does all this work?

Mr Falconer: The Prime Minister has been clear: he will set that out around the timing of the 10-year plan next month, so it is not long to wait until he says a little more. You saw on Friday that there is obviously a national element—that is, the conversation that we have as the UK about our future relationship with the European Union—and you saw a willingness from our friends in the European Union to discuss what those options might look like.

I think President von der Leyen described it as a brainstorm between the UK and the EU, so the two things are obviously related. As this discussion has taken place over the last few weeks, there has been a lot of speculation about whether the European Union would be open to discussing options with the UK, and it was obvious on Friday that it is.

Chair: It is very difficult, though, because so many different people from the European Union and the UK say so many different things. Mr Falconer: You are right that one is able to avail oneself of lots of different quotes from different parts of both the European and British systems at any particular point. I have sought to be quite careful over the last few months while we have been negotiating, because there have been quite a lot of noises off as we go forward. I am sure there will be quite a lot of noises off about these wider relationship questions as well, which is why I think it is important to go back to what the leaders themselves have said, including on Friday, about the way ahead. Yes, I have been made aware of unfavourable quotations from the European side about how Britain has conducted itself, whether during Brexit, after Brexit or even more recently; I think we have all just got to do our best to listen to the actual authoritative statements of the institutions involved.

Chair: Let us move on to the UK-EU summit.

Jessica Toale: Thank you for being here with us, Minister. Last week, you obviously struck a number of deals on food and drink, carbon markets and the youth experience scheme, but we seem to have ended up in a bit of a three-way tango with the EU: the EU is insisting that a separate deal on tuition fees needs to be struck to ratify or complete these treaties, and the Government are insisting that progress is made on Made in Europe before we look at tuition fees. Could you talk a bit about why the Government is still insisting that these treaties will come into force regardless of the tuition fees deal? How confident are you that we will get somewhere with tuition fees?

Mr Falconer: There were four agreements struck on Friday, three of which I expect now to be legally binding treaties. Of course, the House will see those in the normal way. Those three treaties are the youth experience scheme, the SPS agreement—the food and drink agreement—and the emissions trading scheme agreement. The fourth agreement that we reached was a joint statement between the UK and the EU. It included a number of provisions, including on tuition fees.

President von der Leyen was absolutely clear in the press conference, and the EU ambassador to the UK was clear this morning, that those four agreements are on separate pieces of paper; they are not linked. Of course, we agreed to all four things at the same time. We are as good as our word, and we will progress on all of them.

The fourth, the joint statement, includes a commitment to reach an agreement within six months on tuition fees to make it easier for EU students to study in the UK and UK students to study in the EU. It also includes a range of other things. In relation to the Made in Europe agenda, the last two paragraphs talk about a partnership for economic resilience and meetings every four months to ensure that we are doing nothing to harm each other, particularly in electric vehicles, steel and emerging technologies, and that we are working together on common challenges of overcapacity.

We are committed to the joint statement in full—all 72 paragraphs of it. We will deliver it, just as we will—obviously, subject to the will of Parliament—go through and ratify those treaties, and bring into force the necessary arrangements to make them happen.

Jessica Toale: So in your view, there is no barrier to the three treaties that we have signed going full steam ahead?

Mr Falconer: There are dates set out in the joint statement for when they will all come into force. Obviously, I will not prejudge Parliament—we have the CRAG process and we will need to pass legislation, and there are processes on the EU side too—but both sides have reached agreement, and we are committed to bringing this into force on the timings set out in the joint statement.

Jessica Toale: For the benefit of the Committee, could you tell us what the dates are for the implementation of those three treaties?

Mr Falconer: It is January 2028 for the emissions trading scheme, March 2028 for the SPS scheme and March 2030—early 2030—for the youth mobility scheme. That last one is on a later date because, in relation to youth mobility, there are both Commission and member state competencies involved. This will be the first such scheme that has been done in this way, and it is important that we give people enough time to prepare and put in place the necessary arrangements. We will enable the youth mobility scheme to happen quicker if we can, but I think the language in the agreement is that the latest point is that date in 2030.

Aphra Brandreth: I want to ask you a couple of questions about the SPS agreement and dynamic alignment. As you will know, the Genetic Technology (Precision Breeding) Act 2023 put England ahead of the EU, letting our scientists develop crops that are more resilient to disease and drought. Given the weather that we have experienced this year and food security pressures, that has become even more important. The NFU and the EFRA Committee have both pressed for that advantage to be protected.

Once the deal’s transition period comes to an end, British farmers and scientists will be bound by the EU’s far more restrictive rules. Will any precision-bred crops that are currently legal in England become unlawful here after that date?

Mr Falconer: This is such an important area, and you are right that the UK has really led the way. We have engaged extensively with industry throughout the negotiations to make sure we get the right arrangements in place so that things are not taken off the market as a result of this deal. We have a transition timetable and a commitment from the European Union that they are moving towards arrangements much more similar to ours. We are speaking to the National Farmers’ Union about these questions. There is a win-win here. I think you described the arrangements as more permissive in the UK. If we end up with arrangements that are more similar between the UK and the EU, that not only allows us to keep products that have been innovative and helpful on our own market, but gives our industry an opportunity to export into the European Union.

Aphra Brandreth: Can I just clarify? Are you saying that no products that are currently legal in the UK would have to be taken off the market? Can you guarantee that?

Mr Falconer: For the precise timings, I will check my notes before I come back to you. This is how it will work: there will be a transition arrangement until—

Aphra Brandreth: Until 2028.

Mr Falconer: Yes, and then business certainty until October 2029. If you are cleared under the UK framework, nothing is happening until October 2029. After October 2029, there would then be European-wide arrangements, similar to ours currently, which you could then apply for.

Aphra Brandreth: Have you been guaranteed that, or are you hoping that they will have reached that stage by 2029?

Mr Falconer: I am confident that we will reach that stage by October 2029.

Aphra Brandreth: Thinking about the deal’s commitment in terms of Britain following EU food and farming rules automatically—dynamic alignment—you have been keen to push the line that the UK will have influence in making key decisions. I am not sure that I really see that in reality. Could you give us a concrete example of how Britain could stop or change an EU regulation if it were deemed to be disadvantageous for our farmers?

Mr Falconer: I am sorry—you said that something was not clear in reality, but I did not hear the question.

Aphra Brandreth: What is the reality of the decision-shaping ability we will actually have? Is there a concrete example of how we could stop something if we deemed it not to be in the best interests of our food and farming producers?

Mr Falconer: That is a very important question, and I want to get it right, because it is a particularly important question for the farming and wider food industry.

Let me be clear on what we have agreed. The text will be out shortly—I hope before the Bill is introduced on Thursday—so we will be able to have a detailed conversation about the arrangements as they are in the text. The Commission will consult us at every stage as new rules are developed, at the same time and on the same texts as the member states. Our experts will be in the room for relevant meetings on implementing the rules, and where a new rule does not work for the UK, joint committees can agree technical adaptations. If we think something goes beyond the scope of the agreement, there is a process to resolve it, with independent arbitration as a last resort.

Aphra Brandreth: How would you involve Parliament in the process of decision shaping? If Parliament disagreed with a new rule, what would happen if we wanted to refuse to follow it?

Mr Falconer: We will be able to have this debate with all of the text—both of the Bill and the treaties—shortly, so I do not want to—

Aphra Brandreth: It is not really a debate; it is more a question. Presumably, you have thought through how Parliament would be involved.

Mr Falconer: We have, and I am conscious that we will have a discussion about what Parliament thinks of the arrangements we are reaching.

We are committing here to dynamic alignment with decision shaping, so we will be going forward on the basis that we will align with rules made by the European Union but in which our experts have some say. When those rules come through, many of them will be subject to statutory instruments, which could be voted down in this place in the usual way. That would of course have a problem for the overall alignment, which is why we have some of the mechanisms I just described—the joint committees. If there are things we think do not work for the UK, we have a process to do it.

There is a parliamentary role, but we would obviously prefer to fix any problems that would emerge from the UK as rules are being developed, either through the consultation process or the joint committees. It would obviously not be ideal to deal with technical issues of this kind through parliamentary measures; it is much better to deal with these things so that you do not cause difficulties.

Aphra Brandreth: To ensure proper scrutiny, the Government might now want to think again about the Committee’s recommendation to have an EU Affairs Committee. Is that something you will commit to?

Mr Falconer: The questions of how the House chooses to scrutinise the Government are not solely for the Government, but we will obviously engage with the House as we go through this process on what the right scrutiny arrangements might be.

Chair: Hang on. If you said that you were in favour of it, that would be really helpful.

Mr Falconer: I can see that. We will have that discussion through the passage of the Bill.

Chair: It was a recommendation from our report. At the time—though I do not want to mislead you—the Government were not terribly impressed. However, given the ongoing conversation that the Government want to have, we would say that now is the time to have that committee. There are only 11 of us, and we have the whole world and Europe to deal with. We do our best but we think that we should be backed up by a House of Commons European Affairs Committee. I am advised that in order for that to happen, the Government have to introduce a motion that we, as Parliament, decide on. We are dependent on you taking the initiative on that. If you would take that away, be assured that we will raise it again.

Mr Falconer: I am assured.

Dr Ahmed: As the Minister responsible for not only European but intergovernmental relations and as a fellow Scot, you are possibly uniquely qualified to give us an adumbration of the impact of the deal not only to the UK as a whole but to the constituent nations of the United Kingdom. Would you feel it appropriate for there to be a separate track in which we can quantify the effect on the constituent nations—from my perspective, on Scotland in particular? How do you think that conversation would intersect with calls from some quarters to simultaneously start a conversation about Scottish independence?

Mr Falconer: As I said on Sunday on the media rounds, the deal is going to be a really welcome step for Scotland. You will have seen some of the commentary, particularly, from the Scottish salmon industry. Salmon is an important export hugely damaged by Brexit. The deal brings back to Scotland many of the benefits that were lost by the nature of the Brexit agreements that the Conservatives reached. I do not favour a revisiting of the independence referendum questions for Scotland. I think the deal is an important step forward for the UK as a whole.

The relationship between the UK and the European Union is quite different from the question of what we are as a country and where our borders are. That is a question that we should not visit very often. I am called Hamish; I represent an English constituency. Like so many people in this country my identity is a combination: I have a Scottish dad and an English mother. Revisiting those questions on a regular basis is hugely divisive and erosive. As a country, we can talk in a much more easy and free way about our what our international arrangements can look like than about what our actual country should be.

Dr Ahmed: From your answer, and from the Prime Minister’s answer previously, it is clear that this is only the start of a deeper relationship. How would you envisage the conversation about a deeper relationship going if, at the same time, we have a deepening conversation around the constitutional arrangements inside the UK. Would that be a helpful, an unhelpful or a neutral phenomenon, from your perspective, as you navigate our relationship with the EU?

Mr Falconer: Unhelpful in the way that I just set out. I think it is important that we try, as the UK, to secure benefits, improve our relationship and move forwards. I do not think that debating where our borders should be is a helpful thing to do during that process. It was very important to me as we negotiated the deals that we thought about the particular needs of Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland and England. Whether in relation to Scottish salmon, Welsh shellfish, Welsh heavy industry or the way in which steel moves between Great Britain and Northern Ireland, it has been important throughout that we consider the needs of each of the constituent nations and parts of the UK. That is the approach that we will continue to take.

Alex Ballinger: I think lots of businesses in the UK will be pleased with the outcomes from this summit. The manufacturing sector is of course very concerned about the Made in Europe deal and the effect it could have on the sector’s exports in the future. Will you give us an update on the progress on that deal, any timetables that we know about, and whether we are considering any economic security measures against Chinese EVs, or things such as that?

Mr Falconer: That is a very important agenda and it has been a regular topic of discussion. The elements of the joint statement that I referenced included a commitment to an economic security dialogue with the European Union. One point we have made over the last few months to our friends in Europe is that many of our challenges are common challenges, such as overcapacity problems and risks to our industrial base. Those are issues best tackled together. For example, our car industry is completely integrated in a wider European industrial base. That industrial base is not strengthened by becoming smaller and more expensive, which is the impact that some of the Made in Europe proposals would have meant.

There are competing proposals around Made in Europe; there have been 5,000 amendments to the Industrial Accelerator Act, which is the first piece of legislation being brought forward under that rubric. It is obviously a matter for the European Union how they progress. When I took on this role, there was speculation that we would be in a position around this time of year where the Industrial Accelerator Act might have passed and, therefore, the consequences of that legislation would be felt in the UK and elsewhere by the end of the year.

As I understand it, they are not on that timeline. The Industrial Accelerator Act debate is certainly not concluded yet and may take some time before it is. That is obviously a matter for them, but it means that there is not yet a specific legislative proposal for us to interact with. We, of course, discuss this with our friends in Europe, both in the Commission and the European Parliament and in the member states. I am really glad of the commitments we have in the joint statement to a shared approach and to minimising harm to each other. Industry here will take real confidence from that.

Alex Ballinger: You mentioned steel as part of the joint statement, and the intention to limit any negative impacts from both sides. If you speak to manufacturers in the Black Country and elsewhere, they will say that the current safeguarding arrangements around steel are already causing them damage from both sides of the channel. They were quite disappointed that that was not resolved at this summit. Do we have a timeline to expect any progress to be made on steel?

Mr Falconer: Both I and the Secretary of State for Business are very focused on steel. There is a welcome commitment in the joint statement to further progress, but we are obviously following up directly with our counterparts in the EU to try to resolve some of these issues, both in relation to the current safeguarding measures and any future plans for both our side and the European Union. The steel industry is incredibly important to this Government. We all saw the risks it came under, particularly in Scunthorpe and Wales. We will remain very focused on those issues. I would like to assure the Committee that that is top of the agenda for me, the Secretary of State for Business and the Prime Minister.

Chair: Can I ask you about sequencing? The impression was that Made in Europe and any potential deal on tuition fees would happen at about the same time. As with all negotiations, there is always a bit of give and take. If we agree tuition fees within six months, but the Europeans still have not got themselves organised as to what Made in Europe means—making it difficult for us to negotiate where we slot into their understanding of what is good for them—do we not lose a bargaining chip?

Mr Falconer: We have set out some timescales in the joint statement, on which we want to make progress on a range of things. We are discussing a whole range of different things with the European Union, often on slightly different timescales. I described the changing nature of the Industrial Accelerator Act’s timescale. I talked about four months for the economic security dialogue. It is six months for the tuition fees.

We are also, of course, talking to the European Union about the rules of origin questions in relation to electric vehicles, where the deadline is coming up, which originates in the TCA at the end of the year. There are quite a lot of moving parts in our relationship, which reflects what a deep and broad economic relationship it is, in other areas, too. I think, Chair, you are trying to invite me to comment on how each of those arrangements might interact as points of bargaining leverage. For obvious reasons, I am not very keen to.

Chair: No, no. It is much simpler. We may have leverage when it comes to tuition fees, and they have leverage when it comes to Made in Europe. Presumably, it would happen at the same time, and that was the impression that was given, but if in six months’ time we are going to end up with being stuck for the tuition fees, and they still have not sorted out Made in Europe, might we get into the same position that we did with SAFE?

Mr Falconer: I do not want to be drawn too much into how we will conduct the talks going forward. I am confident that we will reach agreement on all those things. Why am I confident? When this process started, there was not much expectation that we would be able to deal with three big treaties within a year, and yet we have worked through a lot of difficult things. I think we will be able to work through those difficult things, too, but I do not want to get too drawn into how we might conduct the talks.

Richard Foord: Last week, the UK and the EU agreed to deepen exchanges on defence-industrial co-operation. What does that mean?

Mr Falconer: We made a variety of commitments on security and defence, including taking part in civilian missions, taking forward work on the security and defence partnership and, in relation to defence industrial, some commitments on capability coalitions. I know you will know that we have been heavily engaged with friends in Europe—often on a bilateral basis—on some of the important defence-industrial questions that we face, whether that is in relation to building frigates together or next-generation fighter jets—

Richard Foord: Those are, as you say, bilateral—

Mr Falconer: Those are bilateral.

Mr Falconer: I will not rehearse a sequence that I know you are familiar with. We did secure access to the Ukraine support loan, which was welcome—that provides opportunities for the British defence industry to support the Ukrainians and shows our solidarity, alongside the EU, with the Ukrainians—but we were not able to conclude an agreement on SAFE, because we did not think that the terms we were being offered were sensible. I have been asked by colleagues a fair bit whether we would be interested in a SAFE 2, should one emerge. We would be very open to a discussion if the European Union wanted to have one with us in that area, but at the moment there is no SAFE 2 to talk about. SAFE 1 is basically allocated. We are still doing a lot of important defence work with European Union partners. We would be happy to have the discussion about SAFE 2, but I suspect that it is queued up behind the negotiations about the multi-annual financial framework that the EU is having at the moment.

Chair: We have a last question from Fleur. We are conscious that you need to be in the House of Lords.

Mr Falconer: I do, yes.

Fleur Anderson: I just wanted to ask whether online safety for young people will be included in the EU-UK digital partnership. Will that be part of it?

Mr Falconer: I think it will be. We are certainly having that conversation with partners in Europe. It is obviously a transnational issue that we need to talk to our friends and allies about. Examination of witnesses

Witnesses: Ed Miliband MP, Lindy Cameron and Nick Dyer.

Chair: This is a hearing of the Foreign Affairs Committee in public on the work of the Foreign Secretary. We are very glad to have in front of us the right hon. Ed Miliband, the Foreign Secretary. Could his two wingpeople introduce themselves, starting with Lindy Cameron?

Lindy Cameron: Thank you, Chair. I am Lindy Cameron, the new permanent under-secretary at the FCDO.

Nick Dyer: I am Nick Dyer, the second permanent under-secretary at the FCDO.

Ed Miliband: I feel very lucky to have them.

Chair: We have had the pleasure of having Nick Dyer in front of us a couple of times now, which has been really helpful.

I will start with some general questions, if that’s all right. There has been a lot of talk recently about international friends. Could you say who Britain’s true friends are?

Ed Miliband: I will come to your question, but let me start, Chair, by thanking you for the work of this Committee. Since becoming Foreign Secretary, I have had the chance to read some of your evidence sessions, including on Russia, which I know we will get to. The work of this Committee is very important, and I know it has been very important on some of the issues we will cover, whether it is Israel-Palestine or misinformation—so first, thank you for your work.

Secondly, let me say how I have approached my role as Foreign Secretary, and answer your question in that context. When I got the job on 20 July, I said that I am very influenced by my parents, who came here as Jewish refugees and who saw Britain not just as a place of sanctuary but as a place that was fighting for the values of freedom, democracy and the rule of law. I see those values as more under threat than at any time since my parents came here, during and after the second world war. A central part of my role, I believe, is to fight for the values that we care about and hold dear.

It is right to do that, because we want to relieve suffering and injustice and to have more democracy. It is also right for our interests. There is this old tension between values and interests. Sometimes there is indeed a tension, but I think that by fighting for our values—the Prime Minister said this in his speech at the Guildhall—we can pursue our interests, because we have an interest in a more stable world that is better for our security and our prosperity. Also, crucially—I have seen this already in the work we have done—it increases our influence, because if people see us consistently pursuing our values, it gives us greater influence in the world. What you have seen since the new Government came in is what I would call three big resets: on Israel-Palestine, on the Commonwealth and on Europe. All of them, I think, are about fighting for the values that we care about as a country.

Chair: In that context, then, who are the friends who are going to help us to fight for those values and interests?

Ed Miliband: I start with our friends in Europe, because it has been very clear to me in the work I have done so far that, whether it is on fighting for Ukraine—fighting with the people of Ukraine—or on the Middle East, climate, debt, development and the many other issues we face, our European friends have common values and interests with us. I think our friendship with the United States is also important. It is an enduring alliance.

Chair: Are they helping us to fight for our values and interests at the moment?

Ed Miliband: We have significant disagreements with President Trump—of course we do—but there are areas of common interest. The way I think about it is that we have such close ties, not just of history but in intelligence, defence, security and trade, that it is part of both Governments’ job—particularly that of Foreign Secretaries, or the Secretary of State in the case of Marco Rubio—to seek to make this relationship work and to seek out common ground, but also to be honest where we disagree. We disagree on climate. The US has announced some measures on the International Criminal Court, and we have said publicly that we strongly disagree with them. We start from our own national interest and should be unapologetic about that. Our national interest also involves an enduring alliance with the United States.

Chair: On the other side of it, who threatens us, how are they doing that and how are we defending ourselves?

Ed Miliband: In our national security strategy in 2025, we said that Russia represented our most immediate threat. I continue to think that what we said in the national security strategy is correct. It is also right to call out what Iran has been doing on British soil—RAF Fairford is only the latest example—and what it has done against Jewish communities. This is a pattern of behaviour from Iran around the world. China is more complicated. I was speaking to the Chinese Foreign Minister this morning; we have co-operation on some issues such as climate and investment, but there is also challenge with China, including on issues of espionage and other things.

Chair: As a country, we have a history of the most terrible lack of post-conflict planning. You referred a couple of times to the conflict in Ukraine.
