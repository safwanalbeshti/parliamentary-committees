Q144 Ben Maguire: Thanks, Chair, and thanks to the panel for coming in to see us today. First, I want to get some context and background. How has the scale and extent of rural, and particularly organised, crime changed in recent years? Have you seen any trends or patterns?

Robyn Munt: I will just give a very broad overview of how farmers feel about this. Dr Tudor: I would probably draw attention to the fact that it is incredibly difficult to get an accurate picture of what is actually happening for a number of reasons. If we take the concept of rural crime, we do not have reliable statistics on rural crime for a number of reasons. The first reason is the problems associated with policing statistics. Rural crimes are not generally separated from other crimes, so it is very difficult to ascertain how much rural crime even takes place in one police force area, and added to that is the fact that national figures are not currently collated.

For that reason, when we talk about how much rural crime is going on, we usually see people make reference to the NFU Mutual claims data—that is really where the debate is at the moment. There is some really good work going on under the National Rural Crime Unit to try to clean up that picture and get a more detailed picture of what is actually going on.

On the second point that you raised in relation to organised crime, it is very difficult to understand what percentage of rural crime would be considered organised crime for a number of reasons. Again, it relates to how we define it and then how we impose that definition in order to come to some kind of statistical measurement—but that does not happen, so we do not have clear statistics.

The research that I have carried out into organised crime in rural areas indicates that a significant proportion of criminality that takes place in rural areas would be considered organised criminality, according to a number of different measures. Whether we take the UN definition or the NPCC definition, all of those definitions can be imposed on to the kind of criminality that takes place within rural settings. But if, for example, you looked at traditional measurements of how many organised crime groups we have operating within rural areas, that data would not give you insight into that, because the groups that are engaged in rural organised criminality have not been defined as organised crime groups.

Dr Smith: I just want to come back to what Kate mentioned about the NFU Mutual data, which is really the tip of the iceberg in terms of what we know about rural crime. That is insurance claims, so it is focused very much on acquisitive theft and damage—theft of property. But we know from various research sources that rural serious and organised crime extends far beyond that particular element. There is modern slavery and human trafficking; cyber-dependent and cyber-enabled crime, which is very much a concern in rural communities; drug trafficking; wildlife crime; food fraud and adulteration; agrochemical counterfeiting—the list goes on.

While NFU Mutual gives us something—£41.5 million from its last report earlier this year—that is the tip of the iceberg. I did some rough calculations with a lot of assumptions included, but that brought my figures to approximately £1 billion in terms of just potential insurance claims. Again, that does not go into other criminality that would be considered organised within the rural space.

Robyn Munt: Just to reiterate, NFU Mutual is our partner insurance organisation. That is the data from only one insurance company. It does not talk to the full cost of the impact of all these things. There is also an issue around reporting, and that fundamentally is down to the relationship between farmers and their local police forces. Q146 Ben Maguire: Can I pick up on a point that Dr Tudor made earlier about the statistics not being recorded by police? What would best practice in that space be? We have heard about the evidence we have in terms of NFU Mutual’s statistics, but how can we tackle that?

Q148 Ben Maguire: What examples of best practice in tackling serious and organised rural crime can you point to that you have seen, perhaps in other parts of the country?

Dr Tudor: There is some really good practice, but it is not on an even playing field. If we take TVP—Thames Valley police—for example, they have a really exceptional rural crime taskforce. They have huge numbers of personnel on that taskforce and they have taken the serious and organised element of their rural crime problem very seriously. That is probably a bit of luck, in the sense that they have a chief officer and a PCC heavily invested in this reality, but they have also a very unfortunate history that has made them prioritise rural crime and the more serious element of that—in the light of PC Andrew Harper.

That is an exceptional example for all those reasons. What they do is incredibly impressive. They are really, really good at collating intelligence and creating operational outcomes on the back of that intelligence, using quite sophisticated SOC tactics within the rural arena. That is very rare nationally, because of all the problems that we see around funding.

Rural crime teams, if they exist, do not have the resources, knowledge or capabilities to take on jobs like that. In a situation where they do not have those capabilities, they could, traditionally, escalate that to ROCUs—regional organised crime units—to draw on their specialist capabilities in order to help them out with that. What I have found, looking nationally, is that a lot of these jobs are far too big for rural crime teams to take on, because they are very complex and very serious. They often involve crossover with high-level drug supply. They can have a national element; they can have an international element.

If you have one or two people on a rural crime team, they simply do not have the investigative capacity to take on a job like that. But when they are thinking about escalating it to a regional organised crime unit, that becomes problematic because they might not reach the threshold of threat, harm and risk to justify that intervention, because they are competing with things like child sexual exploitation, drugs trafficking, firearms, terrorism and so on. What I have found is that quite often the organised criminals who are operating in rural areas are able to act with impunity, because they slip through the cracks of policing. They are too large for local forces and too small for ROCUs, so—

Chair: We have some questions specifically on ROCUs later.

Robyn Munt: There is a piece about recognising the full breadth of the impact. Dr Tudor: Familiarity is obviously a major thing. We have had the guarantee that we would have a named officer for every community, which is fine, but they also need to be competent. Dr Smith: I think we can add to that. We have a cycle. If farmers do not see a satisfactory—in their consideration—response to a report of a crime, they are less likely to report future crimes. That means that police do not know that these things are going on. They do not have the information, they cannot create the intelligence mapping and they therefore cannot request the resources to be able to tackle those issues. That means that police are not visible. Farmers and rural communities do not see the police, and the cycle continues. It is a self-defeating issue if there is not a visible presence.

We know that police presence itself does not deter crime, but it is not necessarily about that; it is about building community confidence. I am not saying, “Let’s go back to having a village bobby,” because we know that that is just not feasible, but it is about being able to know who to contact and how to contact them if there is an issue. As Robyn pointed out, farmers, in particular, cannot separate home and work, which puts an added pressure on the farming community to protect their livelihoods and their homes adequately, and to try to deter being targeted in the first place.

Chair: We will move on to drugs and county lines with Bell Ribeiro-Addy.

Q154 Bell Ribeiro-Addy: How are county lines operating in rural areas, and what impact do you think drug crime is having there generally?

Dr Tudor: We have seen a real transformation of county lines in recent years. Traditionally, they were conceived of as drug lines coming out of major hubs such as Birmingham, London and so on, running to more rural areas, the idea being that they could operate with impunity and not be noticed so much. They could also expand their territory and market share. However, in recent years we have seen a real contraction of county lines, so they are no longer as frequently running across those large geographical areas; they have significantly shortened. We do still see them running into rural areas, but we tend to see them within a force area. County lines do remain an issue within rural areas, but in other ways than you might traditionally think.

I did some analysis of wildlife offenders within a couple of force areas, and when we looked at the nominals involved in those offences, such as badger baiting, hare coursing, poaching and so on, a lot of those nominals were county lines-affiliated OCG members from other force areas. When we are thinking about rural offending, it is not just the direct presence of a county line within a particular rural area; it can also be the nominals involved in those drugs networks coming to rural areas to engage in those other types of crime.

We have been alluding to the kinds of acquisitive offences that we traditionally relate to organised crime activity, but actually when we are thinking about things like poaching, hare coursing, badger baiting and so on, they are the offences that rural communities really worry about, because they tend to be associated with much higher levels of violence, intimidation and aggression, over a sustained period.

Violence during a particular act of theft is very rare, because the offenders do not want to engage in that—they want to get in and get out. However, in relation to those other types of crime, the people involved in county lines who are involved in poaching, hare coursing and other wildlife offences engage in much more sustained aggression towards rural communities and business owners.

You have two elements there to think about. County lines are relevant to any community. It is no longer just rural communities, seaside communities, local towns and so on. It is a huge problem, but it has changed quantitatively and qualitatively in recent years. You also have that other link between traditional rural offences and county lines OCGs.

Q155 Bell Ribeiro-Addy: How do policing strategies to tackle county lines differ in rural areas?

Dr Tudor: It is amazing. Actually, I think the county lines model is worthy of consideration in relation to rural crime. I work with the National County Lines Coordination Centre, which does some absolutely brilliant work. It has regional county lines co-ordinators for each ROCU, who act as a point of contact for the forces within those regional areas. To replicate that with rural crime co-ordinators would be a massive step forward, both in the dissemination of best practice that we talked about earlier and in intelligence collation and creating those jobs for some kind of regional-level force operational outcomes as well. There is a lot of best practice that sits in the world of county lines that could be replicated within this arena.

Robyn Munt: It is important to recognise that, from a farming perspective, when we talk about wildlife crime, the impact starts to get a bit lost in translation. As Dr Tudor said, the intimidation and violence that our members experience as a result of these crimes is a big part of it. That is what makes them feel very unsafe in rural settings. We tend to find that once a group has found a soft patch, as it were, for this kind of crime, they keep coming back. We have members who have unfortunately faced repeated instances of this, and it just gets worse. Our members have suffered some awful instances of assault. If you put that in an urban setting, there would be a much different response to it. It is important to recognise that impact piece.

Our members also feel that if there is a push down on these crimes, they move somewhere else. How the intelligence is shared between forces means that it sometimes feels like you are starting from scratch again in a different space—it is that falling through the cracks piece. From an impact point of view, as well as intimidation, there is disruption and damage. With hare coursing, we rightly focus on the welfare of the animals involved, but there is also a lot of damage to fields, crops and food production, as well as issues with being able to access fields, because you have individuals there. It is not just happening at night time; this happens in broad daylight.

Dr Smith: We need to consider the information that is being provided to Government about the role of county lines and the impact it has on our rural communities. The 2020 report from His Majesty’s inspectorate of constabulary only mentioned rural once, and that was in relation to rural being an importing area—the drugs coming in. A 2025 evaluation report of the county lines programme did not mention rural at all. We need to consider that rural is no longer just an importing area. It should be seen as an exporting area in some places. There are plenty of locations in rural communities where drugs factories or drugs farms can be set up under the radar—abandoned barns or buildings. That is changing what drug production and trafficking looks like from a rural perspective. That needs to be recognised.

Q156 Bell Ribeiro-Addy: What about the effectiveness of initiatives like the national intensification week? Have you found them productive?

Dr Tudor: County lines policing is a contentious issue, because it is dominated by a strong Home Office-driven vision of what constitutes success, which is based around the number of lines that are closed. If we think about the harms associated with county lines, we are thinking about serious forms of child criminal exploitation, so we should really be looking to measure the outcomes around safeguarding as well. Yes, we can say that the taskforces that have been set up have been a massive success in terms of line closures, but that has not done anything to chip away at the broader networks of drug supply. It certainly has not done anything to protect the children who are exploited within them.

Q157 Bell Ribeiro-Addy: You touched on the issues of people using fields, abandoned places and so on, but obviously some places are legally allowed to grow cannabis under special licence from the Home Office. How do you think this impacts the issue of tackling county lines overall?

Dr Smith: That is an interesting point, and it is something that Harper Adams investigated. We wanted to do some research using cannabis, and we decided not to apply for the licence to do that, because of the potential threat of criminalisation and criminal activity that it could bring. From a criminal perspective, it is difficult to separate those licensed locations. They are still locations that they could target. If it is not something that is appropriately protected, they are just going to infiltrate it. Having those legal, licensed locations is great, as long as there is a reason for them. We decided against that approach and changed tack in our research. In terms of criminal thought processes, they will find anywhere and they just do not care, essentially.

Q158 Robbie Moore: Thank you for being in front of us, everyone. I am keen to focus on rural organised crime units. I know they have been touched on, but I would like to delve a bit deeper. Dr Tudor: Can you just clarify—

Robbie Moore: How effective do you feel that rural organised crime units—

Chair: Regional organised crime units.

Robbie Moore: Sorry. How effective are regional organised crime units at attacking organised crime in rural areas?

Dr Tudor: Regional organised crime units do not generally take on jobs. They offer specialist capabilities to forces that need support because a job would be too big for them. When they are thinking about their capacity to take on jobs, they have to take on the jobs that have the highest threat, harm and risk. It could be related to the volume of their offending, or to the networks that they are involved in, potentially involving trans-national drug supply and so on. They are also involved in multiple forms of poly-criminality involving really serious forms of serious organised crime. Just because they have not been mapped, as Robyn says, that does not mean that they are qualitatively different from the groups that have been.
