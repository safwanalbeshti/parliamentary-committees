Chair: Welcome to the final session of our inquiry into the impact of serious and organised crime on local neighbourhoods. We have found this to be an interesting inquiry. I think it is fair to say that when we started it, we had not expected to see how broad the work would be and the different areas that this affects. Sarah Jones: I am Sarah Jones; I am the Policing and Crime Minister.

Rod McLean: I am Rod McLean. I am the head of the serious and organised crime system unit, and I also have a second job as head of the drugs and firearms licensing unit in the Home Office.

David Grahame: I am David Grahame; I am the crime director at the Home Office, looking at ASB up to knife crime.

Dr Prinsley: Good afternoon and thank you for coming. I am Peter Prinsley, the MP for Bury St Edmunds and Stowmarket. My question is about intelligence on serious and organised crime. What does the Home Office see as the key gaps in the understanding of serious and organised crime threats? What plans are there to have a better national understanding of this problem?

Sarah Jones: As you have explored in your sessions, serious and organised crime is extremely wide-ranging and constantly changing. Part of the nature of serious and organised crime is the way in which criminals manage to stay one step ahead in terms of technology. If you read the National Crime Agency’s national strategic assessment, the risk is increasing primarily because of changing technology. We have some very good and well-established intelligence-gathering systems, but we also have the constantly changing nature of crime where criminals are taking advantage of the situation, and needing to stay one step ahead.

To give an example, through the national strategic assessment and tasking strategy, the National Crime Agency will gather information from across a range of different sources—not just from their own intelligence but from policing or surveys, such as waste water surveys. We will gather intelligence from a range of places and then have a process of tasking what needs to happen based on threat, harm and risk, which I think you know about.

There are emerging areas where we do not know as much as we would like. One example is high street dodgy vape shops and the fact that criminals have exploited the opportunity of struggling high streets to use those spaces to do all kinds of crime. We are in the foothills of tackling that, I would say, and our evidence and intelligence around what is joining up some of the OCGs that are involved are at the early stages.

Dr Prinsley: We are all aware of the problem of these vape shops, because there are more vape shops than there are people vaping, as far as I can see. I also think that there are more hairdressers than there are people who need haircuts, but that is perhaps another topic.

We visited TOEX—the tackling organised exploitation programme—in Wymondham in Norfolk recently on a Home Affairs Committee visit. It struck me that what was really brilliant about that is the way in which it was using technology to interpret data that was coming from all over the place. My question is about the importance of national-level data and intelligence to understanding these threats, and what help we would need with the technological solutions to this intelligence gathering. In other words, are we investing the right amount of money in the right stuff to help us do this?

Sarah Jones: The race of technology is a very real one, with AI emerging and being used by criminals, and now being used by police and other agencies to try to keep on top of the criminals. There is a huge amount of work going on in technology.

At the most basic level, in policing we have a lot of old systems that do not necessarily speak to each other in the way that they should, so we are investing quite a lot of money in those systems to make sure that the datasets can be analysed with AI. But you have to fix the datasets first so that you can bring all this information together. There are huge new emerging technologies across policing, whether it is the use of drones and ANPR systems to make sure that we are tackling crime, or bringing together all our databases and systems across policing and at the NCA level.

To give an example of emerging technology that we need, look at the drugs market and the increasing number of drugs coming into the country that have been broken down from their original form into something completely different. As the chief of the NCA says, it is no longer drugs being hidden in boxes of bananas; the drugs are the boxes of bananas. They are literally being turned into new products, so we have to have the technology to find them.

There are also drugs coming in underwater, with GPS tracking so that the drug dealers know where they have dropped them. We have a lot of work to do on technology, but there are structures in the Home Office where we are investing significant amounts of money, whether in PoliceAI, data centres or new tech.

Rod McLean: It might be worth adding, for your benefit, that the NCA is also investing a lot of money to address what it calls its tech debt challenges. It had a record funding settlement this year, giving it a budget of close to £1.1 billion—a 19% increase on 2024-25—and a lot of that is to invest in improvements to its technology.

Dr Prinsley: Coming out of the health service, I can tell you that I understand what a tech debt is. My other question is about how easy it is to get the data and intelligence that are presumably being gathered regionally and nationally to the frontline—to the police officers on the street who are dealing with the vape shop. How do they get what they need to know when they are dealing with the criminals in front of them?

Sarah Jones: To give an example, there is the money that we are investing in the vape shop world—the £30 million work on which my colleague the Security Minister is leading. At the most basic level, you would have a vape shop that may be involved in illegal employment. It may be selling illicit goods; it may be money laundering—it could be doing a range of criminal activity. You need all the agencies to work together. In that example, you physically bring people and organisations together to work on that criminality. It has to go beyond policing to trading standards, HMRC, Companies House—all of those. The information flows have to be there.

When we have a programme like that, it is relatively easy to make sure that the information is flowing in the right way, because we have a problem that we are there to solve, and the police are better at solving problems than anybody else I have ever come across: give them a problem, and they will solve it. The challenge for us is how we do that across all crime types. That is one of the most powerful reasons why we believe we need to fundamentally reform the policing system, which has not been reformed for a long time. We need to restructure it so that those information flows—that golden thread of information and intelligence—can feed up and down the system much better than they do at the moment.

Dr Prinsley: If we were to do that, do you think the public would soon notice a difference on the high streets?

Sarah Jones: With the work on the vape shops that we are investing in now, I think we have had 6,000 raids since we started, and the first, Op Machinize, was much smaller. We have really ramped up, so I am hoping that people will start to see a difference. I have certainly had a couple of shops closed on my high street—I do not know what colleagues have had—but I want us to see a difference. They are a good example of where criminals have jumped on an opportunity, and we need to shut off the opportunity altogether. There is more we need to do and we are constantly looking. We are going to do a closure order thing where you can close a shop for up to a year, but what else do we need to do to really home in on this challenge?

David Grahame: To build on that—this has come up in a few of the sessions—having that kind of national capability to bring together intelligence on a particular threat type has been useful across a number of threat types. There is Opal for acquisitive crime, the National Rural Crime Unit bringing together those sorts of patterns, the county lines work to bring that together and assess it, and now the work on high street dodgy shops. Getting that pattern at the national level and seeing beyond what is happening in local areas, but then being able to push that information back out to particular local areas to take action seems to be a model that is pretty effective across multiple crime types and threat types.

Dr Prinsley: My sense is that some of the crime levels are falling, but the population is somehow not noticing. The worry about crime remains at quite a high level in spite of the statistics about actual crime. Your chance of getting mugged, for instance, is much less than it used to be, which is interesting.

Sarah Jones: But the crime types that the public are most likely to observe have been going in the wrong direction. Shop theft, antisocial behaviour, the vape shops—the volume-level crimes that we see in our high streets—are the kinds of things that make people feel they are unsafe in their communities. That is where the collapse of neighbourhood policing over many years has led to a real gap. That is the other reason we want to reform policing. We have to protect and enhance good, neighbourhood, local policing and not allow it to be constantly eroded.

Another example is the county lines programme that the previous Government started funding and we have continued, which is very effective. Again, there is an exploitation strand in that. There will be an exploitation strand in the work on vape shops. It is a key part of quite a lot of the programmes of work, because it is in every part of our serious and organised crime.

Chair: My final question, before we move on, is around rural crime. One of the things that was worrying when we had our session on organised crime and its impact on rural crime was the sense that maybe it was too low level for the ROCUs but local policing did not know enough about it. We will come to questions on Clear, Hold, Build and so on, but rural crime does not feel like a natural area that would fit a Clear, Hold, Build model. Are you specifically looking at rural crime in the Home Office?

Sarah Jones: Yes, we spend quite a lot of time thinking about rural crime. I watched that session with great interest. Because rural crime is not defined—the datasets, as you know, are by crime type rather than the type of area that they fall within—what we saw with the waste crime agenda, for example, which we have now gripped, was something that grew up over a period of time. It was not seen as a national priority quickly enough, but it now is—the Prime Minister is very clear that we have to take action, and we are clearing up sites and we are intervening. The Environment Agency is leading on that.

As you know, we also had a huge issue around equipment theft, GPS trackers and all that kind of thing. There has been a push there. Obviously, the rural crime unit and the NPCC’s strategy on rural and wildlife crime really help with that. There is a hierarchy of risk and harm. What is the international risk level where the NCA has to intervene? What is the right level for a ROCU? What is the right level for rural crime? Part of the answer is about, as you said, training officers to understand what rural crime is. There has been a huge operation on hare coursing, with a massive reduction in hare coursing as a result. That was a national operation that joined up the forces.

Where the police put the focus, they have really good results, but how do we maintain that? Neighbourhood policing in every single area is part of the answer. Having rural crime teams in forces definitely helps. I have been to visit Thames Valley police—you heard about that in your evidence—who are brilliant on fighting organised crime within their boundaries and have a relationship with farmers who trust that something will be done so actually report the crime. All of these things together are really important.

Chair: The equipment theft is perhaps more concerning than the hare coursing and things like that in terms of the evidence we heard, but I take your point.

Matt Bishop: You have mentioned neighbourhood policing a few times, Minister. The latest police workforce figures show that there has been an increase in neighbourhood police, which is good news, but they also show that the workforce has gone down within the police overall. Was that the Government’s intention? By what means has that happened? Was the intention to increase neighbourhood policing by taking reductions elsewhere? If so, where will those reductions come from, moving forward?

Sarah Jones: There are a couple of things running along together. First, we were lobbied very heavily by chief constables around the way that funding was allocated to them and how they had to maintain officer numbers at a certain level in order to get funding. That meant there were perverse outcomes, where they were maintaining officer levels, but they were then cutting police staff and putting those officers into back-office jobs that should be done by police staff, often for less cost. These kinds of perverse incentives were not working in the system, and the police chiefs and the PCCs said to us, “Please can you take that away, so we can look at our workforce as a whole and decide where resource needs to go.” We have done that, which means that police chiefs are making decisions about whether they want police staff, PCSOs or officers.

We have made a choice that, if we want to increase resources into neighbourhood policing, that has to be the priority. We have provided a lot of funding to do that, and there are new officers in those roles. People will also be deployed. Some chiefs might recruit a staff member and put the officer who was doing the back-office job back into neighbourhood policing, for example. That is happening. Some of them are new and some of them are redeployed.

We need to understand what officers are doing. Historically, one of the struggles we have had with the Treasury in terms of spending review rounds is: how can you show us that the officers are being more productive than they were? There is a huge amount of work to try to evidence improvements in the productivity of policing so that we can then justify and make the case for more funding going forward.

To take one example, the AI changes that we are introducing will, by the end of this Parliament, free up the equivalent of 3,000 officers because people are filling in so many forms and spending such a long time redacting information to go the CPS—or whatever it might be; you know, you’ve done it. Whenever I go on a ride-along for shift out on response, something will happen—they will arrest somebody; something will need to be done—and then they spend hours filling in all the various forms afterwards. If we can strip out all of that and use new technology, such as using drones as first responders—all those things—you can get more bang for your buck and enable officers to do more productive things. It is a mix of all those things.

Matt Bishop: That all sounds really positive—it would do to me—but should we anticipate a further reduction in overall policing numbers in the future, or will there be no more reductions?

Sarah Jones: We have another spending review coming up, and we will have to make choices. We have not made any decisions about how we do that, so we will have those conversations with the Treasury. We want to change the entire system so that we protect local policing and drive improvements in standards across policing as a whole. There are, I think, only two forces that are outstanding at the moment; police forces are struggling in many different ways.

We believe that we have the answers to a lot of the problems through police reform, and through how we navigate inspections—we are changing the whole inspection regime—and how the centre sets priorities. With all those things together, we can have a much stronger sense from the centre of, “This is what we need policing to be doing”, and more freedom for the police to decide locally how they deploy their resources.

Matt Bishop: Moving on slightly, the former HMCI told us that investigative skills are a key part of neighbourhood policing—I think we all agree with and understand that—but the new neighbourhood policing pathway does not seem to highlight investigative skills specifically. Are there any updates on whether the College of Policing has been commissioned, or asked, to promote an investigative mindset in neighbourhood officers specifically?

Sarah Jones: I saw that evidence to the Committee. I think he came back and said, “What are you doing in policing if you do not have an investigative mindset?” I think that is right. The six bits of neighbourhood policing training all have investigation at their heart. Whether it is tackling ASB, problem solving, tackling serious and organised crime, building relationships across communities or managing offenders, there is a need to solve problems and investigate.

The college is writing authorised professional practice guidance on neighbourhood policing. We have not had such guidance before. That is an opportunity to set the gold standard for what is expected of policing. We have done that for other areas but have never had it in neighbourhood policing. When I saw that, I made a mental note to talk to the college about what is in the APP, how it is developing and what we are being sure to cover. Clearly, that needs to be at the heart of everything that police officers do.

Matt Bishop: I do not know if you can answer this, but I will ask a question that I think I also asked the former HMCI. Yes, you can teach some of these skills in the classroom, but for a lot of them you need frontline experience on the streets, actually doing the job. Do you agree with that sentiment?

Sarah Jones: Yes.

Matt Bishop: My last question is about how you are balancing the priority of neighbourhood policing with the need to maintain capacity in other areas. Are you concerned that your ambitions for safer high streets and other such projects may be undermined if investigative capacity is undermined in any way?

Sarah Jones: Do you mean by the numbers reducing or by abstraction?

Matt Bishop: Both.

Sarah Jones: We are going to ensure that the numbers carry on going up, but abstraction is an issue. There has just been a new agreement across all the forces on abstraction rates, and what should be expected and what should not—that is very important. Through the reform programme, we set up our local police commands, with a commander, so that you have protected local policing for neighbourhood policing response and volume crime. That resource needs to be even more protected than it is now.

Obviously, that is a challenge. I am a London MP and, in the Met, there are lots of abstractions. The Met is making choices about how it deals with that, in terms of the risk-based approach that it is taking to some events in central London, so that they need fewer police and we can have more in our neighbourhood. There are choices that we can make, but we need to make sure that we have got a response team and neighbourhood policing.

Matt Bishop: It should not be an either/or. As you say, it should be both.

Sarah Jones: It cannot be an either/or.

Matt Bishop: So you are saying that we need to ensure that those investigative skills and experience are there to make sure that we hit the safer streets ambitions and such things?

Sarah Jones: Yes, the police need to be investigating and solving crime. That is fundamentally what they are there to do.

Robbie Moore: Following the same theme of neighbourhood policing, the incoming Government made the promise, as part of their manifesto commitment, that there would be an additional 13,000 new neighbourhood police officers. Breaking down that figure, how many of the 13,000 are new recruits so far?

Sarah Jones: By next year, we will have just shy of 5,000 additional neighbourhood police officers. There is not a breakdown of how many are new because the police chiefs themselves have decided how to do the recruitment. As I was saying, sometimes they are recruiting a staff member and moving an officer on to the neighbourhood. What we are measuring is how many police there are in neighbourhood policing. At the moment, there are about 20,900. We need that number to go up.

Sarah Jones: We have not got that figure.

Sarah Jones: We are targeting the number of neighbourhood police. That can be officers or PCSOs. We are ensuring that number goes up and that is what we are monitoring all the time. We are on track in terms of reaching our target for next year.

Robbie Moore: So these are not new police officers coming in, newly recruited to the force?

Sarah Jones: A lot of them are newly recruited and others will have been redeployed.

Robbie Moore: But you cannot give me the data. The Committee took evidence on this. Sarah Jones: Not for the 13,000, because we have not recruited them yet.

Robbie Moore: What about the figure as of today?

Sarah Jones: As we are going now, we know that there are a lot of new recruits. However, because you might be recruiting a staff member and then moving an officer, we do not have an answer as to how many there are. It will be a mix. When we set the 13,000 target, we always said that some of them would be new and some would be redeployment, and that we were also still hoping to improve the number of specials.

Robbie Moore: If we are moving a police officer from a job to do neighbourhood policing, is the Home Office looking at the impact that is having on the previous role that the officer was doing? The analogy put to me by my own police force and others that I have spoken to is that the reallocation to neighbourhood policing is now having a detrimental impact on the roles that were previously being undertaken, simply because that officer is now not doing it. The redeployment being done to chase Government funding streams is having an impact on community engagement, traffic, PCSOs and intelligence gathering. What impact assessment is the Home Office undertaking on the loss of a police officer’s role and the impact that it is having on policing?

Sarah Jones: We are, for the first time, monitoring a huge amount of data on all our police forces. We have set up a new performance framework. We are monitoring how the police are doing across the board alongside HMICFRS, which also does investigations and reports into each individual force. We have a huge amount of data that we monitor all the time. I would certainly not expect other services to be detrimentally impacted, because I know it to be the case that there are a lot of officers who are doing jobs that staff could be doing, and we need them out on our frontline.

As the policing Minister, I certainly would not expect to see—and would be very surprised if I did see—any impact in terms of other services being detrimentally affected. The police chief has to manage his or her workforce—sadly, often his—and it is for them to make those decisions, but we are looking at all the levels of how they are doing on every single crime measure, and we will monitor whether there is any—

Robbie Moore: Correct, but the chief constable is making that decision based on how the Home Office is allocating the funding. Additional ringfenced funding is now being allocated for recruiting neighbourhood police officers.

Sarah Jones: It is a much smaller amount than was ringfenced previously during the officer uplift. That was a much bigger proportion of funding. They have a lot of flexibility in their budget, and, of course, we have increased the overall budget for policing by £2 billion since we came in, so everybody has more money than they previously had.

Robbie Moore: Before I move on to Clear, Hold, Build, do you acknowledge that the total number of police is decreasing at the moment?

Sarah Jones: It has gone down, yes.

Robbie Moore: And you cannot say how many additional brand-new recruits have come into the system to help with neighbourhood policing.

Sarah Jones: No, but we have 4,000-odd additional neighbourhood police.

Robbie Moore: Do the Government consider Clear, Hold, Build to be a success?

Sarah Jones: Yes. The pilot of 16-odd evaluations showed that, in the main, there was quite a large reduction in acquisitive crime—about 23% or 24%—so yes, it is successful. It is now operating in about 60 different sites across 42 forces, so it is a tool that is being used by all police forces. Where it does not work so well is where people are not collectively invested in it or where resources are difficult. The Build bit is the trickiest: you need buy-in from lots of different organisations to clear an area and get rid of the criminals, maintain that, not let them come back in and then try to build up the community, but it is good evidence.

Robbie Moore: Rightly, in the evidence that the Committee took, it is generally perceived as a success, with a 23.7% reduction in acquisitive crime. Given that it is considered a success, why are the Government not funding it?

Sarah Jones: We never have. It was not funded in its pilot form. The only case was following a horrific murder in Liverpool, where there was a small amount of money and that was given for a Clear, Hold, Build piece of work. All the other money has always come out of the policing budget. We would expect it to come out of local police budgets, but it is an effective tool. I encourage forces to use it, and they are.

Robbie Moore: Correct me if I am wrong, but the Clear, Hold, Build strategy rolled out maybe five years ago as a new-ish strategy to try to get that partnership-led approach between police forces, local authorities, community buy-in and so on. The Hold and Build parts have been identified as being a bit more challenging, but why would the Government not allocate ringfenced funding to the Clear, Hold, Build strategy if it is considered a success?

Sarah Jones: That is a very reasonable question. The Home Office is sometimes in a slightly odd position, where we give away the vast majority of the money that we have directly to local police; they have operational independence to decide what they do with that, and a small number of projects are funded at a national level. In policing, my preference and the Home Secretary’s preference is to push all the money out so that people can make the choices about what is needed in their area, but to set much clearer targets for the overall strategy that the Department wants to see.

That is what we are trying to do, with much more performance management, much more intelligence about what is actually happening in each force and more direction setting, but also letting the forces do what they need to do. It is a completely valid question, but it is not something that we have ever funded through the Home Office. We fund some of the knife crime budget, the county lines budget and the violence reduction units, but that is sort of it in terms of the pots of money that we give.

Robbie Moore: Does the Home Office expect every police force to carry out a Clear, Hold, Build strategy?

Sarah Jones: They are doing.

Robbie Moore: But does the Home Office expect them to do so?

Sarah Jones: I have not instructed them to, but the evaluation is certainly positive, and I would encourage forces to use it. But you have to have all the ingredients to make it work, and you cannot have it as a blanket thing because it is very intensive. You cannot do it everywhere.

Robbie Moore: Is there a clear direction from the Home Office to a police force that they must have at least one Clear, Hold, Build strategy in their force?

Rod McLean: It is very context specific. It requires local authorities and partners to be around the table. As the Minister said, the evaluation indicated that it has a measurable impact on reducing crime. But it works best in medium-sized areas of 1 to 3 sq km.

Certainly, when I was leading on the policy, where I saw it working best was often in council estates. The example would be Barnsley in South Yorkshire. South Yorkshire council very much bought into this idea of community groups being very much involved.

Those are the kinds of ingredients that you need to have a successful Clear, Hold, Build. It is not something that you implement everywhere. It has to be tailored to the context of the individual area. I will just make that point.

Sarah Jones: I saw a really good one in an estate in Cardiff where they had completely cleared out the crime and transformed the estate. Similarly, that was a small estate.

Robbie Moore: In Keighley, we had a Clear, Hold, Build strategy. It was working very well. That was then removed, and we now have a Clear, Hold, Build strategy in the centre of Bradford, which is working well. I cannot understand why the Home Office is not properly funding it if it is recognised as a good strategy. Resource and funding are seen as a challenge, and have certainly been reiterated as a challenge to me by many a police force.

Sarah Jones: As I say, it has never been something that the Home Office funded. It has been something that the police can do within their own resources.

Preet Kaur Gill: I want to talk a bit about the high street organised crime unit. Sarah Jones: I do not know if I have those figures. My colleague might. Preet Kaur Gill: You mentioned cash seized and freezing orders. Can you say what those numbers are?
