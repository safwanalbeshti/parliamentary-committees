Q25 Chair: A very warm welcome to the second oral evidence session for this inquiry into the Child Maintenance Service. Today we are very lucky to be joined by our first panel: Elisabeth Cooper, who is a barrister from St John’s Chambers, Dr Samantha Callan, who is a co-founder and Director of the Family Hubs Network, and Michelle Counley, who is joining us virtually from NACSA, the national child support advisory service. Thank you again. If you have any technical issues, wave and we will make sure that we are aware of those.

I will start the questions. I wonder if you could outline to the Committee the support that the Child Maintenance Service provides to parents to make an initial child management arrangement, what that is, how effective that support is, looking at both the family-based arrangements but also statutory arrangements. Who would like to start?

Dr Callan: I will start. On family-based arrangements, the 2012 scheme was I would not say pushing but really hoping that as many parents as possible would undertake family-based arrangements. In many ways, it keeps you out of the complications of somebody else being involved in your finances, if at all possible and fair. The types of support are things like information about options and guidance on the different ways to arrange child maintenance.

Q26 Chair: Is that in person? Is it online? Is it by phone?

Dr Callan: That is a very good point. It is pretty much all online and you can ring. One of the main points I want to make is this is such a contested, difficult subject, whether you are on a family-based arrangement and you have somehow managed to make that work or you are going for the statutory service, which is currently direct pay and collect and pay. I know that the Government are planning to legislate to put everyone into collect and pay who does not want to make a family-based arrangement. There are all sorts of online tools and an explanation if you are trying to get the advice before perhaps going into the CMS proper. The problem is you have two people; you have two very different customers. The CMS treats both as customers but they both have different ways of grading whether the outcome is acceptable or not.

You can help people create an arrangement and come to an agreement, but it is the sustaining that is the difficult thing. There is no help in the current system to sustain an arrangement and things are subject to change. Emotions are subject to change. Circumstances are subject to change. Yes, there are variations that can be made, and you can submit a change of circumstances all through an online or a phone system, but it is very mechanical and harsh, despite the fact that DWP is trying to develop trauma-informed systems.

Elisabeth Cooper: I would add that there is an online calculator that is usually the first port of call for most service users, which is as a starting point helpful for them to get an idea of what their obligation is. But it is quite simplistic and does not deal with the more complicated cases. If you are a PAYE earner, then it is great, but if you are or you are the receiving parent of maintenance from somebody who has a company and receives dividends, for example, then there is an issue because the online system and the information online does not make it clear that the CMS does not, as a starting point, take dividends into account in the initial calculation. With both receiving parents and paying parents I have situations where you get an absurdly low calculation based on what is inevitably a PAYE income set below the tax threshold. Then the receiving parent does not understand why, when they are clearly far more wealthy than earning £12,500, for example.

What the current system requires you to do is to make a variation application for dividends or other unearned income to be taken into account. That is some months potentially down the line. That is not backdated to the original date of the calculation. That is only dated to the date of the application for variation. It depends when that receiving parent, for example, suddenly obtains the knowledge that they actually need to make that variation.

Q27 Chair: Are there any other anomalies, Elisabeth? You have mentioned dividends. Is that fairly regular? Are there any other non-regular forms of payment that are making this difficult for a realistic calculation of income?

Elisabeth Cooper: Yes. Rental income, for example, depends on the classification of the tax paid on rent as to how it is then input into somebody’s tax return, and then whether or not the CMS takes that into account as rental income or other unearned income. Anything that falls into something that is not salary, effectively—including, for example, payments made by way of directors’ loan accounts or shareholders’ loan accounts that are ostensibly loans but could be characterised as income, depending again on the treatment of that for tax—falls outside of that very straightforward calculation that the calculator enables you to access online.

Q28 Chair: Thank you very much. Michelle, would you like to come in?

Michelle Counley: Yes, thank you. The problem in my view is that the CMS is very much an administrative service. It is not advisory. The support throughout the entire scheme is very limited. There is another gap that was created when the CM options helpline was removed, because at least that was a touchpoint where parents could feel that they had a human contact, with a voice, that could listen to their personal circumstances and address any specific concerns or queries. Everything is now online. It is just a factual sheet that parents have to read. It is very indifferent. It is very matter of fact. It is quite impersonal at a time where parents really need to be able to have that practical support.

There are experiences here that are negative from the receiving parent’s point of view. In our line of work we see both; we help receiving parents and paying parents. We get a good insight to the overview that parents experience completely. Certainly, one of the common problems that we are having at the moment is these applications have just been shut down before they are even processed. They are not following the regulations. They are based on perhaps lack of time, lack of resource, lack of training, but they are being shut down and paying parents are having their applications refused for six or seven months before that application is revisited again.

From a paying parent’s point of view, most paying parents want to support their children. They want to be involved with their children and the upbringing of their children. Right from that very first contact, the language used is very hostile. It is entrenched with threats. There is a no discretion part where if you do not reply within the 14 days of that QR code, you are immediately deemed as non-compliant. There is no discussion about any facts that may have hindered that particular contact. If somebody is searching for advice about how they deal with this application, it is all stick and no carrot.

Addressing the point with the online calculator, it is a very basic starting point. Sometimes there are real clear indications that perhaps there is more to what is being declared as an earned income, and there needs to be a little bit more of an exploration about the unearned income, particularly with dividends. I know a point that has been mentioned is legislation to amend that, but I think that was back in 2022. We are now approaching the end of 2026 and we do not seem to be any further forward.

Again, what it is creating is a system that immediately isolates and antagonises parents. Instead of reducing parental conflict, you are, right from that very beginning stage, inflaming that relationship.

Q29 Rushanara Ali: Good morning and thank you very much. Can you talk me through some of the common challenges that parents experience when they try to navigate between the Child Maintenance Service and the Family Court?

Elisabeth Cooper: There are a number of challenges. The two systems are clearly set up to be independent, but the decisions made in each of those systems impact on the other system.

Just by way of example, I specialise in financial remedy proceedings. I also do private law proceedings. If we start with financial remedy proceedings, the determination of child maintenance is directly relevant to a number of the section 25 Matrimonial Causes Act factors that a court has to take into account when determining the fair outcome in financial remedy proceedings. There is a tension in the fact that maintenance is a highly variable calculation. Clearly, inbuilt into the system is the provision for annual reviews of child maintenance, albeit that that in itself can rely, depending on when the annual review is, on quite outdated information. It could be from a tax return from several years prior, for example.

The Family Court, in financial remedy proceedings, is being asked to make a final decision on capital distribution, where somebody’s income feeds into their mortgage capacity. Mortgage lenders sometimes will take into account child maintenance as an income resource, and therefore, when the court is assessing that party’s housing needs and the capital that they need in order to purchase suitable housing, the maintenance calculation, whether that is notional because it has not yet happened or is existing, is highly relevant to that. As it is so variable moving forward, there are final decisions being made in the Financial Remedies Court that ultimately a year later can change because even if the parties agree that this is the right amount and this is the information and the order that is based upon that right amount, the jurisdiction of the CMS can only be ousted for that one year.

You may have heard of what we call Christmas orders, which are the mechanism by which some practitioners attempt to get around this point that the CMS, if it is approached a year after a maintenance order is made in the Family Court, will then make an application that discharges the Financial Remedies Court maintenance order. That then removes the certainty for the parties that have agreed or had a judge impose, for example, a financial remedy final order.

One of the key things that I think is important is when a Financial Remedies Court requires some level of certainty as to what the position is moving forward. As they are making very important decisions, final decisions, based on that—because capital decisions are final and non-variable—there needs to be, in my view, consideration of whether or not that 12-month period should, subject to appropriate safeguards, be extended further. That is not to say necessarily for five or 10 years and so on, but to give some certainty to parties for the foreseeable future.

Dr Callan: The thing to bear in mind is that the Family Court’s primary concern is, yes, the child’s welfare and safety, and finance becomes interlocked with all that, whereas the Child Maintenance System is interested in money flowing. There are different aims; there is different information. Parents are caught up in this system. Both systems are the state, but the parents think there is no join-up and they are right. There is no join-up.

In my review for the Government on how the CMS responds to domestic abuse, one of the areas that I felt was very important and neglected was the legal administrative abuse, where systems can be used. Paying parents can be victims of this as well as receiving parents. In a 2025 Family Court case, the judge accepted evidence that the deployment of multiple forums—the Family Court, the Financial Remedies Court and the CMS tribunal—all formed part of an alleged continuing pattern of coercive and controlling behaviour and it caused substantial harm.

May I make one more point? It is not meant to be political, but I am concerned at the current clause 17 of the Courts and Tribunals Bill, which would remove the presumption that it is in a child’s best interest—it is a rebuttable presumption—to have contact with both parents. That immediately introduces a whole new possibility that parents will feel at a disadvantage before they even come to the courts and the child maintenance system. It is that knock-on effect of a presumption that is having a good effect in prisons, because there is an acknowledgement that even if a father or a mother is away from a child it is good if that child has contact, unless evidence is to the contrary. I am just inserting this extra layer of concern.

Rushanara Ali: Thank you. Michelle Counley, would you like to come in?

Michelle Counley: The whole point of jurisdiction is complex. It is one of the areas where there is a lot of misunderstanding. As Elisabeth mentioned, CMS can trump the courts after 12 months. That court process has had a much deeper review of the circumstances and perhaps a longer view of the future of how that would look. Certainly, we see a number of applications coming through that are likely to be heading towards a tribunal venue because paying parents with complexities and high net worth individuals see the CMS as a good, safe exit route from an expensive court order. I certainly think an extension of that 12-month period would benefit, with some security for the children.

The information about jurisdiction is wildly misunderstood across all sectors, and it can have significant financial consequences. We have seen a number of cases where CMS had the jurisdiction. Mum had applied to both the CMS and the courts, processed through the Financial Remedies Court and secured a maintenance order within the final court orders, but nobody actually took the effort to notify the CMS. We have a system where a paying parent actually becomes liable under both schemes. Again, there is no discretion within the CMS legislation to apply common sense where the evidence clearly shows, “This court order is what told me to pay and that is what I have been paying, whereas actually the debt has just accrued”. We have seen cases with significant debts that have accrued because that information is misunderstood and it is not available through CMS, through family lawyers, through online information. It is not available. That is having significant financial consequences to parents.

Q30 Rushanara Ali: I will press on. My next question is for Elisabeth. How often do you see challenges arising because lawyers do not have a full understanding of the CMS? Very briefly, if you have suggestions on what could be done to promote more joint working beyond what you have already said, then please say.

Elisabeth Cooper: There are very few solicitors and barristers who are comfortable practising in this arena. I am direct access based as well as receiving instructions through solicitors, and I very commonly get referrals from solicitors for clients who have approached them. These are exceptional city centre-based firms, yet they do not feel comfortable taking on CMS cases.

That continues within the Family Court itself because frequently I encounter judges who clearly understand the jurisdiction to an extent but do not understand the intricacies in relation to annual reviews or how variable the calculation may be depending on the complexity of the paying parent’s situation, and that then causes problems because if you are at a final hearing or at an FDR, where a court is trying to push the parents and mediate effectively an agreement between them, there has to be a level of knowledge from the judiciary to say, “Well, this is the scenario moving forward on the income that this party will receive by way of CMS liability and I take that into account in X, Y and Z ways.” Instead, they are treating the current calculation as if that is the calculation and is cemented moving forward, even when there is evidence to suggest that next year’s calculation may very well be different. It is because the counsel on the other side is not necessarily up to speed with the impact on it and therefore you cannot necessarily have the exchange of submissions that you might want to to put to the judge the knowledge that they would need in order to take into account the number of variables that the CMS will take into account in the future.

I have been wondering about this as to whether or not the Financial Remedies Court and the DWP could work together on a standard approach to be taken in CMS, in financial remedy cases. For example, could there be a Child Maintenance Service information statement, which sets out the current assessment, the effective date, the income upon which that is based, and how that decision has been reached, the shared care assumptions and, importantly, whether there is a pending challenge and what the relevant review dates are? The other issue is that invariably, when calculations are appealed to the lower tier tribunal, those appeals sometimes take years. In the meantime, the financial remedy decision has been made. Sometimes the appeal will enable a decision to be backdated and sometimes it will not do that because in the meantime there will have been a number of other annual reviews with the same issue.

I think that the starting point needs to be potentially an information statement that gives the judiciary a particular format, and then perhaps an idea of consistent treatment of maintenance in mortgage capacity evidence as well—I think that would be helpful, so taking it into account with and without maintenance—scenario testing in disputed cases so that the court again can assess whether proposed housing solutions remain viable, depending on the various CMS positions, and then joint guidance and judicial training. I am not sure whether there is any specific judicial training on CMS cases. We have, for example, the “Financial Remedies Guide” of earlier this year, which tells us what should happen at every stage of the financial remedy proceedings, building on top of the Family Procedure Rules. As yet, there is nothing in there in relation to child maintenance, so I think some joint guidance and judicial training would be of massive assistance.

Q31 Rushanara Ali: Thank you very much. If other panellists have any additional points very briefly, then please say. Michelle, did you have any additional points to this question? If not, I will move on to some further questions.

Michelle Counley: No. I think Elisabeth raised some really good points there.

Q32 Rushanara Ali: Thank you very much. Moving on to mediation, what would you say are the benefits of accessing mediation for parents who are struggling to agree to a child maintenance arrangement? Michelle, do you want to kick off?

Michelle Counley: This is an area where I think real change is needed, because the aim here is to remove child poverty but there is a much broader picture involved. As I said from the outset, the problem is that the starting point is that CMS treats parents in isolation and it cannot distinguish between parents who cannot pay and parents who will not pay. There is no distinguishing between those two categories. Right from the outset the language is hostile and threatening. That will immediately lead to one party is feeling attacked, which if you are getting unfairness, if you are getting that view from one parent, you will immediately affect the compliance.

My point has always been that the system must have an effective communication process that involves both parents from that very first point of contact. It has to be inclusive of both parents. Whether there is a point of disclosure from the beginning part of the application to eradicate concerns such as we addressed earlier, with unearned income, there needs to be a much better inclusive communication system so that both parents feel that they are treated with the same level of respect and involvement within the decisions around their children rather than one parent continually feeling that they are not listened to, their complaints are unanswered and they have random poor decision making with no explanation around it, compounded by threat after threat after threat, ignoring all their protests, ignoring all their complaints—just continual threats. There would certainly be an argument for mediation to be included, but I am not so sure the resources would extend that far. Whereas if we had something with a good communication system, we could at least start to rebuild the trust and repair the damage that has already been created.

Q33 Rushanara Ali: Thank you. Samantha, did you want to come in? The Government programmes, the reducing parental conflict programme or the mediation voucher scheme, do you have a view on those?

Dr Callan: I think the mediation voucher has been very important. For the reducing parental conflict programme, almost all the local authorities have taken the money up and run with that. They are encouraging everybody in the local authority, in the best cases, to have an eye to conflict as being a problem in a family system that you might not present with going through a messy separation; you might present with all the offshoots of that.

The voucher has been an important nudge in the right direction, but at £500 the people around me will know that that might get you one and a half sessions. Still, I don’t think the Government should have to pay for everything. I think you value what you have to pay a good contribution towards. I do not know if you want me to talk about it now, but I think there is a greater role for mediation, though it needs to be as part of a community-based support package.

I will finish with this. We have had almost 40 years of a child support system and we keep discovering the same mistakes. DWP and CMS are constantly trying to improve and up their game, and that is what I found in my review. They are a reform-minded organisation, but they are working with the most frail family systems. They need something in the community where people can go and say, face to face, “Look at my ream of paperwork from the CMS. What do I do?” and get in there before the ream builds up—early intervention.

Q34 Rushanara Ali: Thank you. Could plans to remove the direct pay service reduce opportunities for parents to resolve difficulties through mediation?

Dr Callan: On the whole direct pay problem that the Government are trying to solve, which is that conflict can build up because there is no automatic Government redress if you do not pay, it is good that they are reducing the fees and everyone is moving into collect and pay, to 2% and 2%. The problem is, as Michelle has explained, you are still in this very hard-edged system with so many things.

We have had so many reforms to the payment system of child maintenance. If we only tweak at the edges—this is quite a big reform, I grant you that, but if we are just trying to reform a system rather than to put other support in place, we will never get over the internal contradictions in the CMS, such as the more contact you give to another parent, the less money you get. That is a big internal contradiction that needs proper smoothing in a community-based setting.

Q35 Rushanara Ali: Absolutely. Finally, what are the safeguards that can be put in place to make sure that parents are not pressurised into an FBA or mediation when it is not appropriate, especially in relation to the risk of domestic abuse?

Dr Callan: That is yet another reason for these family hubs that are now all around the country—if you have triage, if you have a family separation clinic, which is something I have been working on, it is spotting these issues, coercive control and so on, psychological abuse and emotional abuse. You cannot tell that on the phone, despite all your training. It is another reason why, if you are in a family-based arrangement, let’s talk to you in a setting that is—

Rushanara Ali: There is so much here that I have run out of time, but please do write in if there is more you want to say. Thank you.

Q36 Patrick Hurley: I have a question about equal-care cases. Whichever of the panel feels like they want to come back, I am fine to open it up. The 2012 regulations say that a parent should only be treated as the non-resident parent if and only if that person provides day-to-day care to a lesser extent than the applicant. If two parents provide precisely equal care, neither parent will be required to pay maintenance, is my understanding. The problem comes when it comes to the interaction with child benefit, and I want to clarify or get some confirmation from the panel as to the interaction and how child benefit is treated in these equal care cases, whether or not it is used as evidence for one parent to be non-resident or one parent to be given primary duties. That is to whoever would like to take that on.

Michelle Counley: If I can address that first, child benefit very much is the language for CMS and, yes, that is very often used as the deciding factor. Although you have the rebuttable assumption for where child benefit is paid to determine the primary carer, that is usually the deciding factor. The CMS will routinely disregard lots of other evidence that might have been provided, including maybe a child arrangement order. Traditionally, or historically, that has forced most applications where equal day-to-day care has become an issue into the tribunal arena. There are long delays on that. That then brings complications with payments and arrears accruing, and so on, and whether payments continue, because there is no responsibility to reimburse.

The problem is that the narrative around the equal-care position is misunderstood. Again, we find lots of parents have been told, “My solicitor told me I don’t have to pay”. That is not exactly true, because there is a difference between what the CMS treats as band equal shared care and day-to-day equal shared care. There needs to be a greater involvement to decide whether parents do equally share the day-to-day care of their children, and CMS routinely does not take that level of investigation. No communications, no explanations, it just rejects the position and forces the aggrieved parent into tribunal. The only point I will make on that is that I am aware that CMS has introduced new checks and changes where the equal shared care is concerned, and I understand that they are now taking a greater view on evidence that is provided rather than just relying on the child benefit. It is relatively new. It would be nice to see that there is a reduction in applications to tribunal when regulation 50 is raised. But it is a case of watch this space, because up until now it is very much decided by who claims child benefit.

Q37 Patrick Hurley: A very quick supplementary. Because this is relatively new, what questions should the CMS be asking to determine day-to-day care of children?

Michelle Counley: It comes down to the boring day-to-day routines of child contact. A “lives with both parents” order does not necessarily provide sufficient information. It is about who is providing school runs, who is doing the after-school pickups and drop offs, who is taking the children to the doctors, who is administering the medicine, who is having the discussions about what school the children will attend and what mental wellbeing is being provided for the children. There is a whole scheme of questions that need to be addressed to drill down into whether children are being cared for equally by both parents, because this is not about number of overnights. This is not, “I have them five days a week and then two days and swap it the next week”. It is about who is providing that day-to-day care for the children. It has to be about that day-to-day routine that everybody does, or parents do, without having to think about it.

Elisabeth Cooper: Can I add to that please? I have experience of a number of cases where parties have an exactly equal number of nights spread over, say, 14 days, shared school holidays, exactly equal. Their understanding is that there should not be a liability for child maintenance. However, as you may know, there is a difference between having an exactly equal number of nights, which entitles you to a discount in what you pay, and equal day-to-day care.

The questions that Michelle raises are exactly the questions that I advise my clients to get evidence of to rebut the presumption that child benefit answers the question. They submit that evidence and provide the exhibits that they need to do that, “I am the first port of call that the school identifies. I am the address that the child is registered with the doctor”, and still repeatedly the CMS comes back and says, “No, I don’t accept that information”. Then I am struggling to advise my clients, “I am not sure what else you can provide here. We have to appeal it to the tribunal and see if a face-to-face human will give us a different answer”. Invariably they do, but of course that then clogs up the tribunal system, which could otherwise be dealing with far more complex cases on income.

Q38 Patrick Hurley: You mentioned earlier the family hubs and the rollout and the integration there. Could you talk me through the benefits and the difficulties of trying to integrate with other agencies such as family hubs so that parents can get that better support that they need?

Dr Callan: That is a great question, thank you. We need that crock of gold at the end of the rainbow, cross-government working. MoJ is keen to have this because, as you may know, the President of the Family Division, Andrew McFarlane, said about 38% of separating parents are using the courts. Judges are saying, “Why am I adjudicating between which service station on the M4 to drop this child off at?” It is tragic, it is a huge drain on the public purse and on the court time, but it is because parents do not have somewhere to go early to prevent conflict from building. It will sometimes build, regardless of any early help. Unlike in Australia, where it is a statutory pre-action family dispute resolution requirement, when parents are in dispute, that they will have been to somewhere that they—sorry, let me start again. You cannot take action in the family courts in Australia unless you have used statutory family dispute resolution. The gateway for that is these family relationship centres. I visited them in Australia. They do good work, they do child-focused mediation, you can watch films about the effect that will likely be incurred on your child if you have war without end, but the main thing is that it is storefront, it is not a government building in the usual sense—the family court can look very austere—it is a place you would like to go.

The Government have made a good start. In 2022 one of the minimum expectations on family hubs, which has been continued under this Government was that they would support separating families, but it is very minimalist. What we need is, as I said—I can write in about this because there is quite a lot of detail—that what would be good in a family hub is you could have something called a pre-mediation information and assessment meeting. You could get something that is not short-circuiting the process of the MIAM, and then maybe mediation or going to a lawyer, but where you just jolly well get some advice. You know that even if you do mediate, you will still have to pay a solicitor to get a consent order. A lot of people have very little idea of the process of separation, and they go into it and suddenly it gets very contested.

I hope I have answered your question. There is a lot more that I could say, but it will take a resolution at the heart of government that this is a very important area for family hubs, because they are meant to be nought to 19. Separating families, almost without exception, are going through something pretty traumatic. In Norway or Australia, as I said, they do not expect you to go through that process without face-to-face help, and that is where CMS could be helped as well.

Q39 John Milne: This is a question for Dr Callan, specifically in relation to your recent report into the CMS. What improvements have you seen in the service in response to domestic abuse cases?

Dr Callan: One very good reform is that you have named and trained specialist caseworkers, so that rather than you having to repeat your story again and again—which is very, very distressing, as everyone can imagine—you can go straight into a team. It might not always be the very same person, but people do not mind, as long as good records have been kept and they say, “This is my name”, or they give their number, and people say, “Oh, yes, we have all your information here”.

One thing I would say for CMS is that I have heard that it is like you are starting all over again every time you pick up the phone. Why can’t we be using AI like so many doctors are now to record these conversations so that people do not have to repeat themselves all the time? They can make outbound calls. They can be much more proactive. One thing that I asked the team recently is, “Are you capping the number of people in this specialist team by the amount of people you have who are trained?” They said, “No, we are trying to ebb and flow the team, adjust it for the number of cases that we think qualify to go into the team”. I thought that was quite encouraging.

Moving on, I watched some of the training that all operatives have and it was very focused on it being a woman who is the victim and it being the receiving parent. We need a lot more balanced training. I have not seen a lot, but I mentioned it to them recently, and they said, “Yes, we will we will go and review that”. Another thing that others will talk about is that the calculation has not been reformed. They made a decision not to reform the calculation for cost of living reasons. However, what their research did show—the Government did some attitudinal research—is a priority to change the calculation. I know that Professor Hirsch will be talking about this in the next session. Their research showed that the paying parent’s willingness to pay increases if they have more contact with the child. If the calculation is viewed as fair and not excessive, and if it is transparent—we all know about the algorithm—there is less conflict with the other parent. I wish that the Government had made a different decision on the basis of their research, but they have not, but I think that there is some good data in there.

Finally, both salaries should be taken into account. A service that is considered inherently unfair is an engine of more conflict. In many European countries, and Australia and New Zealand, the systems take both parents’ income into account. But we are still saying, “No, it’s too complicated”. I made several recommendations, but my final recommendation, which I have not seen enough progress on, but again it is a political decision, is this community-based help. We will not see a major change, especially in identification of domestic abuse, if it is all done on the phone or online. That is why I keep pushing on the family hubs button. Thank you.

John Milne: Thank you. I was going to ask you what recommendations you felt should be acted on that they have not acted on, but you have just answered that.

Dr Callan: Good, I am glad that I have done that.

Q40 Liz Twist: I want to ask about the wider aspect. How well do you think the CMS works with other agencies to support safeguarding, for example, as part of the multi-agency risk assessment conferencing, or MARAC, system? Who would like to start with that one?

Dr Callan: Would Michelle like to start, because I have talked a lot?

Michelle Counley: To be fair, because we are more proactive on the frontline of helping parents, we do not generally get involved in the interactions with multi-agencies, so I would probably defer to the other panels.

Q61 Chair: Looking forward, how can we move from the position where we have had what seemed a questionable engagement with parents, which has resulted in a status quo remaining? What would you do? How would you design a good quality review that wanted to look at the calculation?

Dr Davies: It is a very complicated situation. The system that would work—Australia has always given us a good example—is where you look at the income of both parents, whereas now we just look at one. You share the costs between the children according to their ability to pay, and that ability to pay includes the benefits they get from the state as well.
