Q28 The Chair: Welcome back to this meeting of the Finance Bill Sub-Committee of the Economic Affairs Committee, where we are looking at some of the Government’s proposed tax measures in the Finance Bill. Today we are considering the mandatory payrolling of benefits in kind. We have another expert panel who we are going to question about this. I would like you to introduce yourselves, if you may.

Kevin Hart: Good morning. I am CEO and chair of BASDA, the Business Application Software Developers Association. We represent many diverse software developers in this country. One of our special interest groups focuses solely on all PAYE matters, and mandating payroll benefits is a significant one to us.

Mathew Akrigg: I am the policy and advisory leader at the CIPP, the Chartered Institute of Payroll Professionals. We support pay professionals across the UK. I have also had the privilege of being seconded into HMRC in the past to assist with the mandatory payrolling of benefits project.

The Chair: Very good. We have a dual-use witness here.

Andy Scott: Morning everyone. I am principal tax adviser at the Confederation of British Industry, the CBI. We represent around 850 direct business members, mainly large businesses across the nations and regions of the UK and most sectors. A core part of my role at the CBI is that I am the secretariat of our employment taxes working group.

Q29 The Chair: Thank you all for coming along. I start with a very general question that I hope will provoke discussion. HMRC argues that this proposal will simplify processes for employers as well as for the Revenue. From an employer’s point of view, do you think this will be a net positive benefit in time?

Mathew Akrigg: I will kick off. The thing to note now is that we obviously have a phased approach. That elongates the amount of time that people need to get used to these new measures. It is not necessarily going to be something that in year one provides any tangible benefit, if at all. From HMRC’s perspective, it is seeing the removal of the P11D process as a big burden on businesses. However, the thing to look at in the round is that it is not necessarily removing a process, but changing where that process lies. There is some complication in the fact that this is now a yearly process merged into a real-time process each pay period; at its heart, however, it is still a yearly process. We are still looking at things in terms of the tax year.

There will definitely be a lot of friction in year one while people get used to what their software provides, what data they need to get from third-party suppliers, and how they manage their own internal processes. That obviously now extends into year two, and we do not have a full idea of what that is going to look like yet. There is a misconception in some ways that the mandatory process will be very much like the voluntary process, which is a lighter process. We are going to see a lot of employer feedback in the coming months that they are not as prepared as they think they are.

Kevin Hart: I would echo that. However, for the software to work, it needs to work for the end user. That could be the employer, but many of them have outsourced to payroll bureaus to undertake the payroll for them. Building on Matt’s point, in effect, the processes of pay and benefits are wider than my arms will reach: on one side is an HR and benefits function and on the other is a pay function. This specialism sits over in the HR, not in pay.

Not only will you have to run this every pay period, rather than the end of year—it is always run at the end of year because it takes time to recalculate everything from the employer’s perspective—but you will then have to make sure that there is in effect an education on handover from HR and benefits. However, there cannot be because it is in two phases. Even after phase 2, loans and accommodation will not be in phase 2. There will still be a need for the HR function to be performing this. We are also concerned that the data, some of which needs to come from the benefit provider—be that the car leasing company or the private medical company—needs to be available to be used by the payroll function. There are a lot of moving parts that have not really been fully considered and brought in, despite strong requests from us all to be doing that.

Our general observation is that most employers at the moment are not prepared at all for this. They expect the software to resolve this all for them, which will be incredibly difficult to do because it specialises in pay, not a mandatory tax piece over there. In the short to medium term, it will in fact increase burden and processes rather than simplifying them. In the long term, it is questionable how well HMRC systems deal with all this because—I am sorry to segue to another point—RTI is bursting at the seams. We need new technology to be able to exchange data effectively with HMRC.

There is a misunderstanding that software will solve it all. While it is generally very good, it is only as good as the processes, and we still have outstanding information. In the short to medium term, it will increase overheads and burdens for businesses. They will all do their best; payroll people are fabulous. They are so diligent. They will not pass Go and collect £200 unless they have all the qualifying criteria, but the worry is that they do not have it all and will not for quite some time.

Andy Scott: I very much agree with Mathew and Kevin’s points, so I will not repeat those. However, if the exam question is, “Will this regime make life easier for our members?”, I cannot give you a direct answer. It is both yes and no because it is a mixed picture across the board. We represent businesses of a range of sizes, from very small to very large. Surprisingly, some insight from our small members is that they are not too worried about it; they are reliant on their software providers—I know everything is hanging on those technical specifications. A lot of small businesses rely on external advisers as well because a lot of small business owners have to wear many hats. With compliance obligations, you do not want the impact to be loss of opportunity to grow that business.

On the large side, it depends on how complicated the business’s benefits offer is, the structure of the business, but also their own internal systems. Some members in the population have a very streamlined benefits offer. They have been voluntarily payrolling benefits for some years now, since it was introduced about 10 years ago. Some members have complex reward and benefits offers. They might need to engage and collaborate with a number of external reward providers. Also, large groups can often have subgroups with different financial systems. At the moment, for that population, it is a challenge to see whether all those systems can be interoperable with most of it automated.

It is important to note that the human judgment element is a really crucial part in being compliant because our tax system is very complicated. A lot of the treatment of some benefits is quite subjective. If we are moving towards real-time reporting, our tax rules are currently analogue and need to be upgraded in the digital age by removing subjectivity and moving more to a yes or no condition basis. That human judgment is more efficient for some members in a single year-end process. They will now have to do that 13 times a year and ensure that that monthly roll is compliant. It is therefore a mixed picture across the board.

The Chair: What are the trends over time? Have businesses become more or less willing to pay P11D-type benefits? What is the balance between very big employers and medium and small employers?

Andy Scott: Things will probably become clear in only year three and beyond. It will be a massive hurdle for a lot of larger businesses in the first two years of the phased implementation. A lot of those businesses will not be able to reliably or confidently tell whether they will have savings in the longer term.

Mathew Akrigg: To expand on that, we have to look at things from the individual point of view. Next year, there will be greater visibility of what benefits they are receiving and, more importantly, the cost of those benefits. There will also be the ability for them to assess whether those benefits are of value to them, because it will be on the pay slip. From the employer perspective, it will be about demonstrating that value as well as being prepared to deal with the burden of having those conversations and explaining the value to those individuals.

Just to pick up really quickly on a couple of things that Andy mentioned, where you have outsourced payroll providers or accountancy services in the mix, it adds that extra layer of complication that any payroll policy will. However, because you have that man in the middle where data flows are required to be quick and efficient, it slows things down. It will be an extra complication for those payroll providers.

P11Ds, historically, have been a chargeable product from accountancy services, but that will, in essence, be removed for the large part. There will still be some exemptions but, because that is removed, there will be a hit to their revenue, which has to be absorbed somewhere. Payroll services are likely to be where that revenue is recouped.

Not to pre-empt further questioning, but the cost to businesses may be more than we might anticipate because, even though we are removing that P11D process, the rest of the process is more complex and therefore requires a greater level of involvement and expertise. If everything was a simple world with simple benefits, maybe the policy itself would go very smoothly but unfortunately the world of pay is quite messy sometimes.

Q30 Lord Leigh of Hurley: You have hit the nail on the head with that last comment. I do not have any data to give you; I am sure we can see if we can find some for you. I wonder whether Kevin or Andy have anything.

Kevin Hart: I could probably help there from a software perspective. The software is both in the outsourcer as well as in the end user. Certainly, if you look at the small to medium company spectrum, it would not be unreasonable to suggest that about 60%—at least—will be outsourcing that function, exactly for the reasons Matt has indicated: it is primarily skill sets, focus and capabilities.

As the larger businesses become more complex and build up resources, to your good point, they tend to bring them in-house. Then they get a bit larger, become more complex again and have a hybrid outsource model where, in effect, they have the software to use but all the difficult stuff is still actually contracted out.

Mathew Akrigg: That is a good point that it does not necessarily have to be outsourced or in-house. There is a whole range in the middle, especially for the P11Ds, as I briefly mentioned; they are something a lot of businesses would have outsourced regardless of whether their payroll function was in-house or outsourced.

Kevin Hart: Therein lies our worry: that may well be a separate company or at least a separate division of a company, so how do you make sure that the outsourcer doing all the expenses with the P11D knows that they have to provide the information to those who are running the payroll function, be that in-house or outsourced? That is a significant concern.

The compliance need, if you will, is upon the employer. We have pushed HMRC a lot for months and months, if not years, to engage with the benefit providers but there is not a great will. It was only recently—in the past month—that we managed a meeting and only with the trade bodies, which did not seem to have a great deal of interest or impetus, if I may say. That is quite worrying because while at phase 1 there are only seven new data items, most of those still will have to come from these benefit providers. Also, with the way it is structured, if they have dental and private medical, you will have to combine the two. You will have to do some key calculations and, again, those functions have been sitting in the HR world, not the payroll world.

Lastly, to build on the point from both Matt and Andy, it is a highly pressurised environment doing payroll. You are up to the limit with all the changes, “Oh, sorry, we forgot this overtime; it’s really important or that person’s going to walk” and “Oh, we have the starters and leavers”. It is all lastminute.com and is therefore highly pressurised. We saw a lot of people leaving that whole payroll environment during Covid because of the additional pressure that Covid brought in. It is only now starting to recover from that point of view. As a software body, it is the whole value chain we are concerned about. Therefore, we cannot have unnecessary pressure on those key functions that are basically keeping Britain going.

Andy Scott: Kevin and Matt have covered that in sufficient detail. It is very much reflective of our membership base. To summarise on the CBI members’ behalf, the SME population are more likely to rely on payroll providers and outsource and lean on external advisers but, as you get towards larger businesses, it is a mixed picture.

We need to appreciate that there is no one-size-fits-all practice. Each business is complex and different in its own right. In the larger population there will be some that lean more towards external support, others with a hybrid approach and others with a more internal leaning with a higher headcount in internal project teams or their own in-house payroll team.

Baroness Wheatcroft: I presume that you and your members have made HMRC fully aware of this, yet apparently yesterday it announced that it is sticking to that deadline.

Andy Scott: The HMRC policy team has been open with us privately in terms of unpublished guidance to a select few of our members. Quite a few of our specific member responses on that front, which were privately sent to HMRC, have been very stern. I appreciate that the phased implementation was officially announced in June. I know it can cause some embarrassment to U-turn sometimes but, if there were some easements, that would receive a lot of good will from the business community. Decision-makers would not be badly judged for that.

There could even be an easement, say, for larger businesses with really complex internal structures and systems that say to their customer compliance manager at HMRC, “Look, realistically that technical specification was released too late”—I believe it will be released next month—“It is just not enough time, we are not going to be able to fully comply with this”. There could be some sort of discretionary dispensation through the CCM route. Say you are a small business relying on a certain software provider, there could be similar dispensation if that software provider has worked with HMRC to say, “We’re not going to be ready in time with those technical specifications”. If particular businesses are relying on a particular software provider, there should be an easement there as well.

It is also important to note that, in HMRC’s software strategy published in March, section 4.4 made a commitment to the software industry that there would be predictability and stability to enable the software market to confidently invest with certainty. It is not upholding those principles. I am sure Kevin might want to add to that.

Kevin Hart: Thank you for the segue. I will try not to comment too much on some of the peripherals but, to go back to voluntary payrolling, it was literally that: voluntary. There was very low take-up. It was a lot of work for our members to do it, and quite a low take-up from their customers. However, there is also a misbelief that because voluntary payrolling will still be supported in phase 1 next April, it is happy days, but unfortunately not. There are quite a few changes to the voluntary payrolling structure, particularly to the payments associated with the Class 1A. There is quite a lot of misunderstanding out there because of this piece.

We escalated this at the beginning of this year. Going back to your good point about January 2024, we offered to get round the table with HMRC people regularly through all different forums, saying, “We’ll meet you anywhere, any time, any place; we’ll take a full day out and work through all these pieces”. Regrettably, it has not taken us up on that at all. In some fairness, part of that is that it has not been resourced properly for this. It does not have the capabilities and the breadth and depth of people needed. You can understand that maybe it sat back and thought, “That makes sense. We’ll just pass it across to there” but, unfortunately, it did not engage with us and did not really understand the value chain, as I describe it: all those functions and roles.

What, dare I say, little information has been made available looks at only what we call the happy path. If everything works well, you get this information, it needs to have this data—we still do not have the calculations, by the way—and that then gets passed over. What if it goes wrong? Unfortunately, believe me, there is a high chance that in the early days it will, for the reasons we have discussed earlier.

There is real concern about this. I spoke also with some members yesterday who said, “We’re having to assume a number of things on the basis of what we have been told so far. We won’t know whether it works until we actually start sending things into the test system from HMRC”. Unfortunately, despite so many requests and offers, HMRC has not engaged with us for the test system. We said that, for the early days, it is critical that we help it to get the test system right. That has been delayed because it has changed supplier—why it would do that at this point, I do not know. That main testing system has been delayed by over a month.

You cannot be in this world of assumption, hoping that things will work, and then trying to have enough time. We have this little thing called Christmas and new year getting in the way as well. A lot of larger businesses will have a code freeze for at least a month, if not six weeks, over the festive period, where they will not accept any changes. Therefore, even if you had something, you cannot get it in this side of Christmas.

Kevin Hart: Into the employers. As a software provider, you cannot get your software into a number of those employers, because of the code freeze. If things carry on with the current trajectory, do you see a crunch point as we get towards April next year, or do you think that, because it will still be phase 1, there will not be a significant penalty or impact on businesses if the system is not worked out or as developed as you hope it could be?

If software does not understand what needs to be implemented to the fullest extent, the employer education cannot happen. We are just looking at this regime from one angle, but policymakers need to step back and take a synoptic view of the tax regimes in this particular space.

The elephant in the room is the RTI system. I know from the advance questions that you are well aware of the issues particularly a lot of larger businesses have in reconciling their payroll tax records with HMRC’s systems. Some members have had reconciliation differences to the tune of millions of pounds. That was automatically being referred to debt management; some had bailiffs come to their premises to pursue debts that the businesses themselves genuinely—backed up in their records—believed they did not owe. Guidelines for compliance were also issued recently to help taxpayers minimise and prevent user errors with the RTI regime at the outset. However, ideally, the regime needs systematic reform to stop the automatic reallocation of taxes to different taxes and tax periods and provide a clear audit trail.
