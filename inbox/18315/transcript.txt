Examination of witness

Witness: Lord Mulholland.

Chair: Good morning and welcome to this meeting of the Scottish Affairs Committee where we are inquiring into the dual roles of Scotland’s Lord Advocate. Good morning to our witness, Lord Mulholland. Before I begin questioning, can you briefly say who you are and what your role is?

Lord Mulholland: I will focus on the role of Lord Advocate. I have worked for and with a number of Lords Advocate, and I have been Lord Advocate. I worked with Lord Cameron of Lochbroom, then Peter Fraser, Lord Rodger of Earlsferry, Lord Mackay of Drumadoon, Lord Hardie, Lord Boyd and Dame Elish, and then I became Lord Advocate. I have now been a judge in the Court of Session and the High Court for 11 years. In a past professional life, I was Procurator Fiscal for Edinburgh, and I was Procurator Fiscal for the Lothian and Borders area. I was a Crown Counsel for 11-ish years—senior Crown Counsel included—and I have appeared in courts all over Scotland.

Chair: Thank you; that is very helpful to know. I think I am right in saying that you were also Solicitor General, which is actually very interesting to us in terms of this inquiry.

Lord Mulholland: Yes, I was Solicitor General when Dame Elish was the Lord Advocate. I worked with her for four years when she was the Lord Advocate. I was speaking to her in advance, and she passes on her apologies because she is not well. She is recovering. I do not want to make public what she is recovering from, but I would not be here if I had it, I can tell you. She is very sorry not to be here, and she passes on her apologies. If she can with the work of the Committee in any way, for example, in writing, she would be delighted to do so. Another thing is, I am just at the tail end of sinusitis, so I am a bit deaf. If I ask you to repeat the question, I am not buying time; I will genuinely have not heard.

Chair: That is absolutely fine, thank you. Can you pass on our best wishes to Dame Elish?

Lord Mulholland: I will do. I will be in touch with her tonight or tomorrow.

Chair: We are very disappointed that she could not be here, but we understand why that is the case.

Based on your time as Lord Advocate, Lord Mulholland, what do you think are the main advantages of the devolved Law Officers having that dual role as both the Government’s legal advisers and the senior prosecutor?

Lord Mulholland: There are a number of advantages. First, it is the rule of law, and when you are dealing with politicians, they obviously want to apply the rule of law, but they are not lawyers, so they do not know the intricacies. I will give you examples to try and give colour to this.

If you remember the Glasgow airport bombing, the First Minister rang the relevant Cabinet Secretaries, who were all quickly advised what they could and could not say. It was huge news in Scotland, so they were obviously going to make public statements on it in the media. You needed to tell them what they could and could not say so they did not prejudice the inquiry.

Another example was the Clutha helicopter crash. All these examples were in my time as a Law Officer. Again, you needed to speak to them quickly so they knew what they could and could not say. Of course, as politicians it is incumbent on them to give reassurance and information to the public, so you need to understand their role as well. But there are certain red lines that you cannot cross.

As head of the prosecution service, you know where the problems and gaps are arising. To give an example from further back, I do not know if you remember, but there was a real fear around the 2000s when the police became aware of links between seven Algerian persons living in a flat on Easter Road. It was said that one of them had links to some people who the Met police were very interested in in relation to bombing. The police searched the flat and found a note on top of a wardrobe. I saw the note and it looked to me and the police as if it was a little diagram of where you would drop or lay bombs. One was the Edinburgh Woollen Mill at one end of Princes Street, and the other one was Jenners—it was open at the time—around mid-Princes Street. It was a few days before the Hogmanay celebrations, so understandably the police were very concerned about it.

Fast forward to us trying to link the cases down south and up north, but the prosecution authorities down south applied different timescales than in Scotland. It is 110 days to 140 days to 12 months in Scotland; it is a lot longer—I do not know what it is now, but it certainly was at that time—down south. So the two investigations were clashing and eventually no proceedings were taken. It is a long story as to why, but it seemed to me and the head of the prosecution service in Scotland at the time that you needed universal jurisdiction in the UK for cross-border terrorist crimes because of the timescales.

I talked to the UK Government and in 2008—I think it was—they made a provision in the relevant Counter-Terrorism Act for universal jurisdiction in Scotland. That is another example. That would not have happened had the Lord Advocate not been aware of the issues.

Do you remember the issue about “revenge porn”? I hate that phrase. I call it “indecent images”, but it was called “revenge porn” at the time—you all know what it is. There was no legislation covering that in Scotland at the time. I spoke publicly about it and spoke to the First Minister to alert him to the issue. Of course, there is now legislation in Scotland. We had the issue of offensive behaviour at football still going on. That was discussed with the First Minister.

There are plenty more examples of where prosecutorial knowledge is brought to bear in the Parliament. There are certain things that First Ministers need to be aware of. If you split the roles of Lord Advocate and head of the prosecution service, it would be much more difficult to have that one-to-one with the First Minister.

I will give you an example. I was heavily involved in the inquiry into the Lockerbie bombing. When I became Lord Advocate, in around—forgive me—2011 or 2012, one of the first things I did was to look at the papers for the case against Megrahi in the Lockerbie bombing. I satisfied myself there was more than a sufficiency of evidence that it was a rightful conviction. I applied my judgment to it. I was then interviewed—I think it was by Magnus Linklater of The Times—and was asked about that. I told him I had read the papers and I was satisfied that the conviction was sound. That was a published interview. The then director of the FBI, Robert Mueller III, got in touch because he had read what I said. He asked to come over to Edinburgh to meet me, and did so.

In discussion it was obvious that we needed to go after the others involved in the Lockerbie bombing. It is still the biggest terrorist atrocity to have happened in the United Kingdom—270 people killed. The notion that Megrahi did it on his own was just risible. He was part of what were then the security services of Libya.

Luckily for us, it was just after Gaddafi had been killed, so there were opportunities with the new Libyan Government. I went to Tripoli with Robert Mueller to Tripoli two or three months after the fall of Gaddafi; Libya was just coming out of a war. My wife was not too happy about it, let me tell you. I did not want it to be made public.

If it had been made public, it would have put my life in jeopardy, as well as those of Robert Mueller and the support network that went over with us, but the First Minister has to know what you are doing because if something happened, it would be worldwide news. You have to be able to have a conversation with the First Minister on Privy Council terms so that he knows what you are doing. I am not discussing the evidence with him, but he needs to know. That is another example of the crossover between the head of prosecution and the Lord Advocate as a Government Minister and the principal legal adviser to the Government.

Chair: Would it be fair to summarise what you have said by saying that you think there are practical advantages in terms of both prosecution and policy development, as with the issue of the universal approach?

Lord Mulholland: Absolutely. I could sit here for another hour and give you examples, but I am sure you have other questions. I thought I would illustrate it with some real, live prosecutorial conversations about new legislation and ongoing inquiries. I am not discussing the evidence; I am just letting them know where I will be in the next four days.

Chair: That is very helpful. Thank you.

Lillian Jones: Morning, Lord Mulholland. Based on your experience as Lord Advocate, what are the main risks or disadvantages that come with the Law Officers having a dual role?

Lord Mulholland: I suppose the risk is that people think you have malign intentions or political intentions. If you look at the history of the Lord Advocate over the centuries, the Lords Advocate were politicians. I am being a bit of a historian, but I will I illustrate it this way. It goes back to the 16th or 17th century. The Lord Advocate was also head of the police forces when they were developed in the early 1800s. They were also in charge of the military in Scotland. On the eve of the battle of Prestonpans in 1745, the Lord Advocate and Solicitor General rode out with the dragoons to meet Sir John Cope. It was a futile journey, I would suggest.

During the French revolution, there were a lot of political prosecutions for sedition. The Government were concerned that the desire to change the system, as was happening in France, would be imported into the UK and Scotland, so there were a lot of those prosecutions. After Prestonpans, the Lord Provost of the City of Edinburgh was prosecuted, no doubt on the Lord Advocate's instructions, for failure to close the gates of the City of Edinburgh to the Jacobite rebels. He was acquitted, but he was prosecuted for breach of duty. Those are all political prosecutions.

Dame Elish’s second stint as Lord Advocate began when the SNP Government was elected. She had served a Labour First Minister, Jack McConnell, and was asked by Alex Salmond to stay on, and one of the first things he announced was that the Lord Advocate would no longer routinely attend Cabinet, which I think in retrospect was a wise thing to do at the time.

I can also tell you, because she asked me to inform you of this, that when the SNP Government came into power and Dame Elish was asked to stay on, she did it on the condition that her budget—the COPFS budget for the prosecution service in Scotland—was not part of the justice budget. Up to then it was not, but there was a discussion and she knew they were considering making COPFS part of a justice budget. She made that a red line, and it was not made part of a justice budget. It was all part of the independence of prosecutors and of the Lord Advocate in Scotland exercising his prosecutorial function.

To give colour to this, the Scotland Act 1998, which is a magnificent piece of legislation in my humble opinion, has the independence of the Lord Advocate right throughout it—section 48, section 52, section 28—so that the exercise of the retained functions of the Lord Advocate, such as investigations of deaths and prosecutions, are guaranteed to be independent.

To go back directly to your question, it said there is a perception that, when you are exercising the head of prosecutorial function, that can cross over into the political sphere. You cannot stop people thinking what they want to think, but I have to say that I was a Law Officer for nine years and, in all my time, I never had one approach by an MSP, MP, Government Minister or First Minister wanting to discuss the evidence in live cases—not once. I had very good links with all parties in the Scottish Parliament. My door was always open if they wanted to discuss something, but they knew that a live prosecution was off limits.

On the perception point, you will know yourself that, currently, a UK Minister is considering Rosebank. Jackdaw has been announced and a decision taken, and Rosebank is live. The relevant Government Minister who is going to take this decision—the Energy Secretary—acts in a quasi-judicial role, but she is also a politician of the governing party. People can have their own views on it, but as far as I can see she is going to exercise that in accordance with her quasi-judicial role; she’s not going to exercise it as a politician.

It is the same with major planning decisions, which are taken by Government Ministers in Scotland and down here. They exercise that quasi-judicial function in accordance with the law, but without political interference. My point is, if it applies to politicians exercising quasi-judicial functions, why would it not apply to a Lord Advocate who has two hats, or a lot more hats? We are talking about the Government legal adviser as opposed to the prosecution.

You have to remember that I was Lord Advocate during the referendum, and I had to give the Government legal advice. It was 24/7 in the lead-up to the referendum. I got through that, I would like to think, with no suggestions that I was in any way biased. I was absolutely determined that, whatever the decision, whatever your views on the referendum, the one thing that I could influence was that the result would be respected by everyone in Scotland. In other words, it would have a legal base and, whatever the result, it was legally rooted. I hope you think that I achieved that.

My point is that the dual role can be done. It has been done for a long time, as far as I’m concerned. I think it goes back to perceptions. If you have that belief, what can be done about it? The reality is different.

Dave Doogan: You made the point that you cannot stop people thinking what they are going to think, which I fully accept. You are somebody who has been in and around this function professionally as a Lord Advocate. The concern that the Committee is looking at, and that is held in some elements of society, is that there is a conflict of interest. There might be a breach of professional rules whereby, because of the Lord Advocate’s position in Cabinet, politicians will secure information in Cabinet that they otherwise should not have.

We have heard from you today, and from previous witnesses, that that is just unrealistic. It doesn’t serve the reputation and professional candour of the postholders, whoever they were and whoever they will be in the future, to think that there would be some slack-jawed leakage of information from a Lord Advocate to a Government Minister in a Scottish Government of any stripe. We are not talking just about right now; it is in perpetuity, as long as this role exists. People are entitled to their perceptions, but is there a frustration at the perception people have that there is leakage across the information barrier, because that would never happen?

Lord Mulholland: There is a frustration, I suppose, that some people do not understand that and some people do not accept it. The reality is that there was no leakage of information in relation to criminal cases during my time as Lord Advocate and Solicitor General. You would tell the First Minister or the Cabinet what they needed to know. You have to apply your judgment as to what you think they need to know.

Going back to Alex Salmond’s decision, Lords Advocate do not routinely attend Cabinet. A standard FOI question during my time as Lord Advocate was, “How many times has the Lord Advocate attended Cabinet?” and I think that will have continued. I suppose the interest in that comes from people saying, “You say you are non-political, but you are.”

I do not have the exact figures—it is a long time since I was Lord Advocate—but from memory I attended about one in four. That was the Government Cabinet. There were two Cabinets—a Government Cabinet and a political Cabinet. I am not suggesting that that is the case now; I am just talking about my time as Lord Advocate. In the political Cabinet, as the name suggests, there was a discussion of politics. They were not held routinely, but there were, from time to time, political Cabinets. I never attended any, and the Solicitor General did not attend any, because they were political, as the name suggests.

But, on the attendance of the Government Cabinet, I would look at the agenda for Cabinet. If there was something I had given legal advice on, I would be there to answer any questions about that legal advice. If there was something legal on the Cabinet agenda, I would attend to advise the Cabinet on what they could and could not say. I also attended if there was a discussion about an inquiry to be held. I will not name it—I don’t think it would be right—but there was one inquiry I looked at where it said the costs of the inquiry would be circa £30 million. I thought, “What? Are you kidding?” I then attended Cabinet to tell them what I thought the real costs were. I was actually quite conservative about the costs. You will know yourself about the costs of inquiries, but I just thought the cost was completely unrealistic. That was one occasion. Deaths is a retained function of the Lord Advocate, so you can see why there would be circumstances where you would attend Cabinet.

Another good example, just to bring colour to things, goes back to the Glasgow airport bombing. The bombers tried to run a Jeep through the main doors on Fair Saturday, when the place was absolutely packed with holidaymakers, ready to fly out for their hard-earned holidays. They did not get through the main doors, and they were arrested. One was too badly injured and died soon after. The other was brought to justice. They had moved up to Scotland after trying to set off a nail bomb in a car in London.

That happened on the Saturday. On the Sunday, Law Officers, including myself, were in the Crown Office to discuss it. We had senior CT people from the Met, and they wanted the Scottish bomber brought down to London, for this to be tried alongside the London charge, because it made the case stronger. We would not, at that time, release the Scottish bomber—or bombers, as we thought then—because we wanted to see the evidence, to know that the Scottish prosecution would be looked after and to ensure that the public interest went forward.

It became a bit fraught, because we would not agree to that right away. In the book of one of the two officers who attended, he suggested that Alex Salmond was behind it, trying to create division between Scotland and England. Let me tell you: Alex Salmond had nothing at all to do with it. It was not even discussed with him, and we would not discuss it with him. What had then happened was that we looked at the evidence and saw the connections between the London charge and the Glasgow charge.

We then had the discussion with the then Attorney General, Patricia Scotland, and we agreed that the Scottish charge would be taken down south and they would be tried together. At Elish’s insistence, we entered a memorandum of understanding that the Scottish charge would not be dropped without the express consent of the Law Officers in Scotland. Just to ensure that it was not dropped, or that they had not overlooked this memorandum of understanding, we sent someone down to the trial. At one stage, there were discussions about a plea of guilty and dropping the Scottish charge, but when that came back to Scotland, we were straight on the phone to say, “You’re not going to do this. Something has gone wrong.” The conversation happened, it was not dropped and it went to verdict.

What I am saying is that there are circumstances in which you work with UK Ministers. The Attorney General did not have prosecutorial functions, but there were functions that she was exercising in relation to the law and the interaction between England and Scotland. That is another example, and there are plenty of them.

Mr MacDonald: Lord Mulholland, I think we know where you are coming from. For the avoidance of doubt, can we just have a yes or no to this question? On balance, and based on your experience in the role, should the dual role be split?

Lord Mulholland: No.

Mr MacDonald: If it did happen, what would be the unintended consequences?

Lord Mulholland: Well, the unintended consequences would be that you would not have what I have just described—that input into Government policy and new legislation, or input into Ministers to make sure that they do not say things that are going to prejudice an inquiry. You would lose the good governance of inquiries and legislation, and of telling them, “You cannot say that when it is a live prosecution. You cannot talk about it.”

In fact, during my time, I remember a discussion with the Presiding Officer about what could be said in Parliament. If you look at the Scotland Act, it actually prevents a Law Officer from answering questions in Parliament where it could prejudice a live inquiry or is not in the interests of justice. I have to say that I have never exercised that.

There is one other point, which is a side issue in relation to the point you made, Mr Doogan. The only people who have rights of audience in the Scottish Parliament are the Law Officers. Obviously, they cannot vote, but they are required to answer questions. Certainly, in my day, every six weeks there were Law Officers questions for half an hour at the end of Justice questions. Anyone in the Parliament can put down questions, and anyone can ask a written question.

You might disqualify yourself from a decision. For example, if it is an MSP who is charged with, or suspected of having committed, a crime, you would disqualify yourself from taking that decision. That is a long-standing convention that I have exercised in respect of myself on a few occasions—not a lot of occasions, but a few. That also applies to Westminster MPs, although I think I exercised it only once in relation to a Westminster MP.

The point is that you need to know if you are going to answer the question in Parliament, even though you have not taken the decision or been involved in the case. You need to know something about it to be able to do your duty and serve the Parliament. That is perhaps not understood enough. You need to be briefed, even though you have not been involved in the decision at all.

Chair: Can I just clarify something, Lord Mulholland? Are you saying that there might be occasions when the Lord Advocate, having not been involved in a case because it was not considered appropriate for them to be involved, would still answer questions in the Scottish Parliament about that case? It would not be passed to the Solicitor General, for example.

Lord Mulholland: The same convention would apply to the Solicitor General as well. If it is an MSP, the Solicitor General—certainly in my time—would disqualify themselves from dealing with the case or taking a decision in the case. The Solicitor General does, from time to time, answer questions in Parliament. The point is that whichever Law Officer is answering questions will need to be briefed. The brief might be why you cannot answer that question, because it may prejudice proceedings or be contrary to the interests of justice.

Chair: Who would make the decision to prosecute in that case?

Lord Mulholland: It would be independent Crown Counsel. A senior member of Crown Counsel would be given the papers to take a decision on the case. They are not a politician or a Law Officer, and they do not have any rights in Parliament. They would take the decision, and it would then be notified as to what the decision is.

I will give you an example on Government legal advice. I mentioned to Ms Jones that I had given the Government legal advice. I will not go into what that was, because it would breach the convention, but I gave them legal advice on the vires of the Scottish Parliament to legislate for a referendum. Because of the magnitude of that decision, having drafted the legal opinion for the Government, I then thought it prudent to go to independent counsel—obviously on a confidential basis. I gave them the draft opinion and said, “Do you think that is right? Tell me what the pitfalls and problems are. Tell me if you don’t agree with it.” Thankfully, they did agree with it.

Another example is where I had made a public statement about a live case. I did not think it would cause controversy, but some people thought it would. They wrote in and said, “You should disqualify yourself from any future decisions.” I looked at it and thought, “I don’t agree that I should. But I am just going to ask someone else to take any future decision.” So, going back to the Chair’s question, if there was a perception of conflict in an individual case, you would send it to independent Crown Counsel.

Douglas McAllister: Is that not the problem with the dual role? You have given all these examples of where there is a perception of conflict. These other obstacles and barriers are then put in place, which are the various recusals. Does that not demonstrate the weakness with the dual role? It is not that there is actual political interference; it is that the reasonable person test must be considered. There are various occasions where you will take the view, “That might be that person’s opinion. It is, of course, not the case, but just to safeguard it I had better step away.”

Lord Mulholland: The only recusal that I am aware of was an MSP or MP—there may be others that I have overlooked. To be honest, I do not know how that came to be. It was certainly an unwritten convention when I became Lord Advocate, so I am not going to challenge it; I am going to go with it. That is what I did.

I do not know why that is the case; if you hold that view, you hold that view, and maybe nothing can shift you from it. I come back to the reality of it: I have never had any political interference in any decision that I have had and have never discussed live cases with any politician.

Douglas McAllister: I would not suggest for a second that you would. However, you have given examples where you have had to say that it goes to senior Crown Counsel to avoid that perception. That happens because of the dual role.

Lord Mulholland: I get that point, but I think that would be in the perception of the decision maker—the Lord Advocate—at the time. I think that there is a cost-benefit analysis in relation to those cases. There was an MSP who was charged, prosecuted and convicted for domestic abuse. When it was decision-making time, that was sent to independent Crown Counsel. When that decision was then announced, because live proceedings were taken, I was asked questions about bias. At the time, I did not even know who had taken the decision. The point is that people of those views have those views, but the reality is that I have never known it. I hope that that answers your question.

Douglas McAllister: It does, but that is the point: we will never be able to fix people’s views. That is why judges and sheriffs will recuse themselves day in, day out: not because they do not have the ability to put something to the back of their mind and make a proper decision, but because of the reasonable person test.

Lord Mulholland: Yes and no—there are motions for recusal in cases all the time. I prosecuted the Transco gas explosion case, and there was a motion for the trial judge, Lord Carloway, who then became Lord President, to recuse himself. He refused, so just because there is a motion for recusal, that does not mean that the judge or the decision maker should recuse themselves. I gave the example of that case, where there was a letter. I did not think that I should have recused myself, but I took the decision that it would not have affected it and that someone else could take a decision.

Chair: I am struck by the issue of complexity that you mentioned. Following up on Mr McAllister’s question, the Lord Advocate might not make the decision about the prosecution of an MSP, say, and the Solicitor General will not make that call, either—it will go further down the chain, as it were. You may, however, be asked to answer a question about it in Parliament. Does that not give rise to a public perception that something in the nature of this is a wee bit strange and that perhaps these roles need to be split for that reason?

Lord Mulholland: You may have a point to a certain extent, but the Lord Advocate does not take many case-related decisions. He will take ultimate decisions in murder cases, if we cannot accept the plea to culpable homicide, or in rape cases, if the charges are recommended to be dropped. On the Lord Advocate or Solicitor General answering a question in Parliament about a case where they have not taken a decision and it has been taken by independent Crown Counsel, it is kind of standard practice for the Law Officer, in answering a question, to make that clear: “Can I just advise you? A decision was not taken by a Law Officer because the convention applies; it was taken by independent Crown Counsel.” Since I stepped down as Lord Advocate, I have certainly seen my successors answer questions in that way. Of course, you would be briefed on the answers by permanent staff, and it would be standard fare to include that in the suggested answer.

Douglas Lumsden: I will stay on the subject of the perception of political interference, safeguards and processes. Are formalised safeguards in place to ensure that there can never be political interference in a case?

Lord Mulholland: There are statutory provisions that guard against political interference. Of course, the structure of the Crown Office and the private office is set up to apply the legislation. As Lord Advocate, you will have a private secretary and a legal secretary, who is very experienced and knowledgeable. You will have quite a large team, in Crown Office terms, briefing Lords Advocate as to what they can and cannot say. You will get briefed in advance of, say, questions if something high profile comes in for decision. If you are an experienced Lord Advocate, you will know the position anyway, but if you are inexperienced, they will tell you what you can and cannot do. You get advice on that basis.

There are very high-profile decisions that have to be taken by Crown Counsel acting on behalf of the Lord Advocate and by the Lord Advocate and Solicitor General themselves. You know that they are going to be high profile, that not everyone is going to agree with them and that they may be subject to questions in Parliament. You are well aware of that and of what you have to do. The structures are in place to protect the independence of the decision-making process itself.

The Crown Office has great corporate memory; it goes back for centuries. On the people who work in it, if you look at the staff turnover of the Crown Office, it compares favourably with other organisations, because it’s a great organisation. It’s very interesting work. It’s a great organisation to work for. People are imbued with the Crown culture, so all the systems, practices and procedures are put in place to ensure independence of decision making for criminal cases and deaths.

Douglas Lumsden: But sometimes it goes wrong. Would it not be better if the dual role was split, so that there was some additional oversight when things aren’t quite going as they should?

Lord Mulholland: What would the oversight be? This is underpinned in legislation—the Scotland Act. It is underpinned in practices and procedures. Would it improve people’s perceptions? I don’t think so, because the structures and procedures are in place to ensure independence. I think any Lord Advocate giving evidence before this Committee would confirm what I am saying. Decisions are not taken while wearing a political hat.

I am not going to talk about the Peter Murrell case, but I would imagine that when that case came in, hearts sank because they knew that a big decision was down the road. But that decision—I say this without knowing about it. I was not involved. I did not actually want to read too much about it. I well appreciated that the Lord Advocate of the day, Dorothy Bain, would—it would be difficult to manage all these things, but I have no doubt that that decision was taken without political interference.

Douglas Lumsden: When you were Lord Advocate, did you ever experience any political interference or attempted political interference at all?

Lord Mulholland: Did someone attempt it? No. No, I didn’t experience that. One or two MSPs were interested in certain cases. I will give you an example: a number of MSPs were particularly interested in the RBS second share issue—you will remember the rights issue when a lot of people lost a lot of money when the bank effectively went bust. There was an investigation headed by the Crown Office and a Law Officer, and work went into whether there was any criminality.

I briefed certain MSPs who were interested in that. I didn’t brief them on the evidence. I briefed them on the process. I briefed them on a timeline as to when the decision was likely to be taken. But not one of them ever asked to discuss the evidence, what the understanding of the allegations was, what was suspected, or any of that. It was a decision taken by me, rather than them asking. I just told them that it’s under investigation and the dual benefit of that is that, if it’s under investigation, the rules of sub judice apply, so they would need to be careful about what they said in public. That is an example.

Douglas Lumsden: I am curious about the relationship between Lord Advocate and First Minister. Is everything on a formal footing? You mentioned that you had to call the First Minister before you made a trip to Libya. Was everything formalised, or would there be a phone call chatting about things that were going on?

Lord Mulholland: Well, that was a phone call or a face-to-face conversation, because I did not want anything in writing before I went, in case it leaked out or got FOI-ed. You apply your judgment. I served two First Ministers: I served Alex Salmond for four years, and I served Nicola Sturgeon for one year. I stepped down in June 2016.

It is a mixture. If it is formal, it will be in a minute across to the First Minister’s office. You do chat from time to time about things, not about live cases. The First Minister might say, “I want your advice about something,” and you would ask, “What’s it about?” You might chat it over, then you get a formal request coming in on the civil side, and then you issue your opinion.

I will tell you a funny story, but it is a true story. I do not know whether he was serious or not, but Alex Salmond once asked me to look into a pardon or quashing the conviction for William Wallace. Of course, he was hanged, drawn and quartered in London in 1298. I remember the conversation because it was quite humorous, and my answer to him was, “Well, I’m afraid you’re speaking to the wrong Lord Advocate, because I think he was tried and convicted in England, so you’ll need to make that request to the Criminal Cases Review Commission down south.” There are conversations, but if it is a legal matter, you would expect it in writing, and your answer would be in writing.

Douglas Lumsden: I hope that was not a serious request from Alex Salmond, because I guess that could be seen as political interference.

Lord Mulholland: I do not think it was, but it was funny, and when I came back with the story, everyone was laughing.

Douglas Lumsden: Do you think the safeguards could be strengthened further, or do you think they are adequate?

Lord Mulholland: There could be strengthening of some of the safeguards. The convention—it is unwritten—that Law Officers do not take decisions in criminal cases involving Members of Parliament or Members of the Scottish Parliament could be put in legislation, because I suppose a later Lord Advocate might not agree with that. You would need to look at the conventions. I take it you have read the McMillan report, which is pretty comprehensive, although certain things are missed out in it, unless I have overlooked them; I read it fairly recently.

I will give you an example, unless it is in the report and I have overlooked it. One of the functions of the Lord Advocate in Scotland is that he is responsible for the licensing of the printing of the Bible, and it does happen from time to time. When I was briefed on the role of Lord Advocate, I was asked why, and the answer—I fell off my seat laughing—was that it is to prevent religious war in Scotland. In 1836 there were disputes about the correct version of the Bible, and some people got very upset about a different version—the non-King James version.

There are always quirky things that a Lord Advocate does. For example, the Lord Advocate is the guardian of the public interest. In the Law Hospital case, when medical staff wanted to switch off a ventilator keeping someone alive, the Lord Advocate had to go in the public interest to the Court of Session to get authority for that to be done. He would represent the public interest in that case. He would also instruct the police on the reporting of cases, and he would issue prosecutorial guidelines to procurators fiscal and Crown Counsel on prosecution policy.

For example, when I was Lord Advocate, I issued instructions on knife crime, as did Elish and Colin Boyd. My instructions were that anyone caught with a knife in public in Scotland had to be prosecuted on indictment, unless there were very exceptional circumstances, which would cover 13-year-old kids or something of that nature. In general terms, that halved the homicide rate in two or three years in Scotland. It went from an average of about 120 a year down to just over 50. The Lord Advocate has a lot of powers, which are of assistance and improve lives and save lives.

Douglas Lumsden: Would an improved safeguard be the strengthening of the formal arrangement between the Lord Advocate and the First Minister? We spoke about how sometimes there would be calls and chats that were not minuted and did not have civil servants there. Would that strengthen the whole perception that there is a potential conflict, or would that just be over the top and then mean that Government could not really work?

Lord Mulholland: In my personal opinion, it would be over the top. I do not give opinions here as the opinion of the judiciary. I am sure that you are all aware of that, but I wanted to make a public statement to that effect. That would not benefit good governance. It is up to the First Minister to have a chat with the Lord Advocate. No live cases are ever chatted about.

Do you remember the Nat Fraser case? It went to the Supreme Court on a devolution issue. There was a statutory instrument, or an instrument, that was drawn up by the Supreme Court about taking the ability to quash convictions. Up to then, if a devolution issue succeeded in the Supreme Court, the Supreme Court would remit it back to the Court of Appeal to then quash the conviction, if that was what was to happen. That respected the power of the Appeal Court in Scotland to quash convictions. But the Supreme Court drew up an instrument, a rule—I cannot remember precisely what it was—giving the power to the Supreme Court to quash convictions.

I cannot tell you the concern and anger that there was in Scotland about that. I do not know if you remember, but the First Minister, Alex Salmond, went on “Newsnight” and used the phrase, “We are not having Hope’s law in Scotland”. I am paraphrasing. I am not sure if I have got the quote right. Let me tell you, I went to the First Minister’s office first thing the next morning and spoke to him. I am paraphrasing, but I read him the riot act and said that he is not going to say that again—he is not going to criticise judges.

To go back to your point about these kinds of conversations, and if you legislated to prevent that, I would suggest that me going over to say do not be saying those kinds of things—do not be criticising judges—was good governance. There are ways to deal with this, and it was dealt with. That is just an example of the types of conversations that you have sometimes. That was related to a criminal case.

Douglas Lumsden: Does that not support breaking up the dual role? As the role of the Government’s principal legal adviser, those conversations can take place, but with the role of being the public prosecutor, then that would have to be formalised.

Lord Mulholland: When I told him not to say those things, I was going over as the prosecutor because it related to a criminal case. I would not have had that access to the First Minister if the roles were split. I had to go over very quickly because the media in Scotland wanted to follow up on, “We are not having Hope’s law”.

It calmed the whole thing down. The Supreme Court and the Appeal Court were going to work it out between them, and I was not having any more public comment. That was not as the Government’s legal adviser, that was his head of the prosecution. It is the best example that I can think of.

Lillian Jones: When you were Lord Advocate, Andrew Coulson was unsuccessfully prosecuted for perjury—

Lord Mulholland: Who was that, sorry?

Lillian Jones: Andrew Coulson. What actions or precautions did you take as Lord Advocate to ensure that that criminal case was progressed free from political interference or prejudicial comment?

Lord Mulholland: It was Crown Counsel that took the decision, and I think it was Crown Counsel, as the decision maker, that prosecuted the case. As Lord Advocate, you have to support your prosecutors—your High Court prosecutors and Crown Counsel. There was no reason for me to interfere—I would not want to interfere. I am aware of the politics. Crown Counsel took the decision and was prepared to prosecute it. I think he was acquitted, wasn’t he? From memory, it was a trial judge, Lord Burns, who upheld the “no case to answer” submission on the basis of the law on perjury. Other than that, I just knew that the prosecution was ongoing; I was not involved in the decision making.

Lillian Jones: Documents published in February show that you wrote to the Presiding Officer, the Commons Speaker and the Prime Minister asking that they take steps to prevent or discourage prejudicial comment in the political arena. Why were those interventions necessary?

Lord Mulholland: Because there was a fear that they would. Of course, it was not the Crown Counsel that would write to the Prime Minister, the First Minister or the Presiding Officer. It had to be a Law Officer—the Lord Advocate. I had not taken a decision, but the message was sent out and it worked. There was no public comment and justice took its course.

Douglas Lumsden: When you wrote to the Commons Speaker, Presiding Officer and Prime Minister, were you just alerting them that there was a live case, and not putting anything about the case in that message?

Lord Mulholland: You have the messages. I think there was a bundle—38, I think—that Dorothy Bain published. I think about 10 of them, or maybe a few more, were from me. You have obviously seen those messages. There is nothing about the case in them.

Douglas Lumsden: I guess that is the difference between that message and the message that was sent from Dorothy Bain to the First Minister, which actually had more details of the case in it, from my understanding.

Lord Mulholland: You mean the Murrell case?

Douglas Lumsden: Yes.

Lord Mulholland: I have deliberately not found out about the Murrell case—it is none of my business, really. I think Dorothy Bain said that she had not taken a decision in that case. It was taken by independent Crown Counsel. I may be wrong about that—you will know better than me; I have certainly not researched it. You would really need to ask her what the considerations and issues were, and why it was sent in those terms.

Douglas Lumsden: We are trying to ask questions because you alerted people that it was a live case. When Dorothy Bain alerted the First Minister to that live case, she added extra details of the scale of the embezzlement. I guess that is a question that we will ask her. I will move on. Did you ever recuse yourself from one of your functions while serving as Lord Advocate or Solicitor General?

Lord Mulholland: From one of my functions?

Douglas Lumsden: Yes.

Lord Mulholland: No, I can’t think of any—unless you know and are not telling me!

Douglas Lumsden: No, I am not trying to catch you out. We are just trying to see if the recusal process in place is robust enough—that the Lord Advocate and Solicitor General know when to recuse themselves and when they can take part in cases.

Lord Mulholland: We are trained lawyers—experienced lawyers—so we know what the rules of recusal are. We know the tests that courts supply. I am not saying it is the court legal test. Before I was Lord Advocate, I had, what, 38 years of experience in dealing with these matters with successive Lords Advocate, so I have seen the way they operate. You are imbued with the culture.

Douglas Lumsden: I am trying to think whether there should be a process that is written down that everyone can see, just for the sake of transparency, so that everyone can tell why that person has recused themselves or why that person has decided they can take part in it.

Lord Mulholland: You may make a good point. It would be helpful for the public if this recusal convention, and when you would recuse yourself, was set down on paper. Guidance has been published on certain matters before so that the wider public and courts are educated. There is a good example of drink driving, when, back in the early ’90s, there was an issue with the CAMIC, which was the device that was used at the time, and the accuracy was within 3% or 4% of the reading. The Crown agent at the time published in the Law Society of Scotland magazine that they would not be prosecuting above 40% or 35%—I cannot remember precise details. That is something that the Crown is then held to.

I certainly support these conventions being written down, including the recusal conventions, and maybe a bit of work being given to all aspects of recusal. For example, is it the chief executive of a political party? I cannot remember what Coulson was—was he the press secretary or something of that nature? Would that be taken into account? Would recusal apply? Or does it only apply to MPs, MSPs and that type of thing? Does it apply to former MPs or former MSPs? I think a bit of work should be given to it, but that is just a personal opinion; you would need to ask the current Lord Advocate what they thought about that, and about what the pitfalls are. I am just speaking off the cuff.

Chair: Lord Mulholland, you have quite rightly been at pains to make it clear to the Committee that you would never have briefed Scottish Ministers about the progress or the content of a live criminal case. Would you have disclosed to Scottish Ministers any of the specifics of a criminal case that was going through? That might be the timeline or the amount of money involved in an embezzlement case, for example. Would those issues have ever been disclosed?

Lord Mulholland: I can think of one instance when they were. You remember the World’s End prosecution. I was the Lord Advocate who— in a conversation about the legislation with the then Justice Secretary Kenny MacAskill, who is a former MP—suggested bringing forward the double jeopardy law. That then went to the Scottish Law Commission, which recommended bringing forward legislation, which was brought before the Scottish Parliament. The legislation was passed and was made to retrospective cases—I remember that that was one of the issues. Work was then ongoing—I think at my insistence—to get the new evidence to meet the test, and that was done successfully, with a forensic scientist who was based in Chorley—she was Scottish. We had trawled the world to see what the forensic science was.

To short-circuit the point, when we decided that the evidence met the test, I formally advised that an application would be made to the court to set aside the acquittal. I did not go into the evidence, explain why that decision was taken or explain what the new evidence was but, because it had been Government policy—because it was a seismic Act of Parliament—I thought it incumbent on me just to let them know that an application would be made. That is the only example I can think of.

Chair: Presumably, that was information that became public. But if the information was not public at that point, would that still be something that you would have passed on?

Lord Mulholland: It was not made public at the time that I sent the minute, but it would shortly become public, because the application was about to be lodged with the court. Timing would be for the Lord Advocate to decide—I would not comment on that—but, as you can discern from what I have just said, it was shortly before the application was going to become public.

Chair: If it were one of the cases that you mentioned, perhaps involving an MSP, where you were not involved in the prosecution directly, would you have passed on information about it, in writing or by word of mouth, to Ministers?

Lord Mulholland: I do not think I would, to be honest, because it is a prosecution. I cannot swear to it, but I have no memory of a minute on the MSP case that I mentioned being sent across to Bute House to advise that there was to be a live prosecution. I cannot swear to that, but I cannot imagine me doing that. I did answer questions in Parliament about it.

Chair: I think we have covered that. In August this year, the new system of Crown Office practice statements was introduced. Do you think that merely codifies a long-standing arrangement, or is it a distinctly new policy since you were Lord Advocate, for example?

Lord Mulholland: It is certainly a new policy, although the Crown does publish things from time to time. I remember that, on offensive behaviour at football, the way prosecutions would be considered was made public. There are other statements. I remember that, in the 1990s, a prosecutorial statement was made about how there would be no prosecutions for homosexuality. The law was that it was illegal at the time, although Parliament was considering legislation. The view was taken that that should be made public and applied.

The Crown has made public prosecution considerations for 13-year-olds, 12-year-olds and 14-year-olds charged with rape, and the circumstances in which the case would be given to the reporter to deal with. There are prosecutorial guidelines—that is pretty topical these days—on the age of consent and when you would prosecute a 16-year-old, say, for having underage sexual intercourse with a 15-year-old. Those are matters that the public should be aware of.

Even the courts themselves—as you will know, although it is not as far advanced as down south—make sentencing guidelines public, such as for causing death by dangerous or careless driving. There are guidelines on sentencing young persons and children, and on sentencing in rape cases. Again, that is to advise the public of what the considerations are. I am a believer in letting the public know, especially when appropriate, about things that matter to them.

Chair: Indeed. You mentioned a little earlier, in response to a question from Mr Doogan, that you did not attend the political Cabinet. Would you have gotten papers for the political Cabinet?

Lord Mulholland: No. I did not even know what was being discussed—I did not want to know, and I was out the door as quickly as I could. Not that I was asked or expected to go; I did not want a “there’s a political Cabinet going on just now, and we haven’t seen the Lord Advocate coming out the back door” kind of idea.

Lillian Jones: You were the second devolved Law Officer to be appointed on an explicitly non-political basis. What, in your experience, are the benefits and drawbacks of this approach to appointments?

Lord Mulholland: The only person who knows my political views is the ballot box. I have never made them known; no one knows what political party—if any—I support. No one knows how I voted; I have never made any statements about politics or what I think about things, and I did not as Lord Advocate. Any public statements I made were in relation to matters of the Lord Advocate’s work.

I will go back to what I said about political Lords Advocate. That can result in difficulties going back through the centuries; there have been political prosecutions, but I am not aware of any in modern times. Not being a member of a political party as Lord Advocate is a good thing, in my humble opinion. I have never spoken about what I think about things or my views on anything other than the work of the Lord Advocate; I do not think it would be right to do so. I have to say that I was never asked. When I met Alex Salmond when I was appointed Solicitor General, he did not ask what my political views were or how I voted or anything; Nicola Sturgeon did not ask either.

Lillian Jones: If the dual role were to be split, would the Scottish Government benefit from having party political senior legal advisers as is the norm in the UK and Welsh Governments?

Lord Mulholland: To be honest, I do not think they would, because if you had political legal advisers, then the legal advice that you give could be skewed. You could be trying to please, and I have never thought that that was a good thing. That is why the convention on legal advice exists: it allows legal advisers to give the Government of the day legal advice without the fear that it will be made public. If you were going to make it public, it would be written in a different way, I can tell you. You give unvarnished legal advice to the Government of the day as to whether something is legal, and, if it is not, what the considerations are and how it could be made legal. If you were a member of a political party—a political Attorney General, for example, if such a role were given—you would find yourself in difficulty in certain cases about legal advice.

I am not suggesting any impropriety, but the changing advice given by the Attorney General on the Iraq war was subject to a lot of criticism. Is there a danger that that is perceived to be a political Attorney General giving the advice that the Government wanted? I have never given the advice that the Government wanted. I never asked, “What do you want me to say?” or “What advice do you want me to give?”

I would go back to the referendum: you could not get a more political decision, or a more political connotation, than whether the Scottish Parliament has the power to hold a referendum without a section 30 transfer of power. If you had a political Attorney General or Lord Advocate at that time—I don’t know. I will leave it at that; I think you get my drift.

Lillian Jones: Finally, there is currently no legal bar on the devolved Law Officers serving as MSPs. If the dual role is retained and they continue to serve as prosecutors, should that law be changed?

Lord Mulholland: Can I tell you how I saw it as Lord Advocate? I did not see it as serving exclusively the Scottish Parliament. I never saw it that way because there are retained functions and reserved matters. You get terrorism, firearms, drugs—all those are major issues in Scotland, as they are down here. I regularly met with the then Attorney General for England and Wales, Dominic Grieve. Jim Wallace, the Advocate General in Scotland, was a friend. I met him on various occasions. I gave evidence to this Parliament on a number of occasions—health and safety comes to mind.

As I see it, my duty as Lord Advocate was a dual one: a duty to this Parliament and a duty to the Scottish Parliament. I cannot speak for successive Lords Advocate, but that is the way I saw it. That is why it is important that this Committee and this Parliament look at this role. The role of the Lord Advocate on reserved matters, retained functions and so on is important to this Parliament, as it is to the Scottish Parliament. I hope that answers your question.

Douglas McAllister: If the public are to have confidence in the dual role of the office, should it not be clear and transparent how the role holder is appointed? For instance, you were chosen by the Scottish Government. How does that arise? Were you approached or did you express an interest in it?

Lord Mulholland: It is probably better if I talk about the appointment as Solicitor General. I had four years as Solicitor General, so they knew me and knew how I worked. I had been a High Court prosecutor. I had prosecuted the most high-profile and serious cases. I prosecuted Peter Tobin, Transco and so on, so I had a raft of experience. I was also the Area Procurator Fiscal for Lothian and Borders, so I was running the second biggest area in Scotland and running the capital, effectively. I had done a lot of work in cold cases and specialisms, which is perhaps not understood as much by the public. Before I became a Law Officer in the Transco experience, prosecutors were generalists, not specialists in any area. I embedded that.

Going back to your question, I think that was known in legal circles. I got a completely unexpected phone call one day from, I think, the Cabinet Secretary for Justice, who asked if I was interested in becoming Solicitor General. I thought about it and said, “I’ll get back to you.” I was given 10 minutes. I spoke to my wife, as you do, and she said, “That means I won’t see you as much.” I said, “But it’s a chance of a lifetime.” I then phoned back and said I would be interested. I then went to the Caledonian hotel in Edinburgh, where I met Alex Salmond. We had a chat of about half an hour. It was all about, “Tell me about your background”—that kind of stuff. Then he said, “I’ll get back to you.” I got a phone call later that day saying, “We are happy to appoint you Solicitor General.”

On the Lord Advocate role, there had been a landslide SNP election victory, I had been doing my job, and Elish had indicated that she was going to step down. I got a phone call a few days into the new Government to ask whether I would be Lord Advocate. That is how. I can tell you that, with successive Lords Advocate, there has been a process involving interviews and there have been a number of candidates, but that did not apply to me.

Douglas McAllister: Quite. That was going to be my next question: do you not think the public would have more confidence if it was a system of competition, like any other job vacancy, rather than phone calls and meetings in hotels?

Lord Mulholland: You may have a point—I do not know. I suppose it would be down to the Government of the day. I mean, are Cabinet Secretaries interviewed? I do not know. I can just tell you, candidly, that, although I was interviewed, I do not think there were any other candidates.

Douglas McAllister: Cabinet Secretaries do not have that dual role, though. That is the point.

Lord Mulholland: Yes. You might have a point about that, but I was very experienced at the time.

Douglas McAllister: You were indeed. Do you think that that experience is essential? Do you think the appointment should be made among career prosecutors such as yourself?

Lord Mulholland: Not necessarily. Elish was a prosecutor when she was appointed, and she was a successful Lord Advocate. I would like to think I was a successful Lord Advocate. I was a prosecutor, predominantly, in my career. Others have not been. James Wolffe was Crown Counsel, I think, for a couple of years—maybe three. Dorothy Bain was an advocate, and she was my principal advocate depute. She had been Crown Counsel before that, so she had a lot of experience. Experience helps in doing that job. It is a hell of a job.

Douglas McAllister: From memory, I think a leading Conservative politician maybe cast doubt on your ability, because you had not been a member of the faculty. Do you think the contrary is important? Do you think it is important to the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service, rank-and-file members and procurator fiscal deputes that the chief prosecutor should be somebody like you and Elish, who were career prosecutors?

Lord Mulholland: I don’t necessarily think so. I was Crown Counsel for over 10 years. You are not a member of the Procurator Fiscal Service if you are Crown Counsel. I became an advocate in, I think, 2007, so I have been an advocate for almost 20 years. I became a QC, as it then was, in 2005. I had been a prosecutor. I had worked in a commercial firm in Glasgow: Bird Semple and Crawford Herron. I was offered a fabulous job in Hong Kong doing patents and trademarks. I would have been a lot richer than I am now if I had taken up that job, but money has never been my motivation. I enjoy court work. That is why I did it. If you are going to do court work, there is no better place to do it than as a frontline prosecutor.

I worked as Crown Counsel all over Scotland—Greenock, Glasgow and Edinburgh. I have done a lot of work on cold cases, which were important to me. I set up the cold case unit in 2011 when I became Lord Advocate, because from my time as a frontline prosecutor, I knew the importance of that. I once asked how many unsolved homicides there are in Scotland. What would you think—20 or 30? It was over 500. I was horrified, and I thought that something needed to be done about that, so I set up the cold case unit.

I remember being asked, “What would success be like?” I said, “One.” If you look at the numbers now, they are into double figures. It has been a big success. People have been helped. Chhokar was a cold case, as were the Emma Caldwell case, the World’s End case, the Tobin case and other cases. I am certainly aware of more in the pipeline. That learning and knowledge was brought by me into being a Law Officer. To answer your question directly—does the person need to be a prosecutor?—no, they do not. It requires hard work, intellect, principles and a determination to make things better.

Douglas McAllister: Do you think the Lord Advocate should step down after five years? That seems to be the established convention recently. Do you think it is important that that is maintained?

Lord Mulholland: Lords Advocate have served longer. There are Lords Advocate in history who have served more than 20 years at different times.

Douglas McAllister: But recently?

Lord Mulholland: I will tell you why I stepped down: I was washed out. I had nine years as a Law Officer. That was during the referendum, and there was all this stuff that I had to deal with: the Clutha, terrorism, Glasgow. When I came to 2016, I was washed out. My health was beginning to suffer. I had put on a bit of weight. It is not a healthy job—you fire down a biscuit at 7 o’clock at night to keep you going. I thought, “I’m going to go into an early grave if I don’t deal with this.”

Douglas McAllister: Would it be healthier if you didn’t have the dual role?

Lord Mulholland: Touché! Well, I don’t agree with that, but it is not my decision; it is your decision. I am just trying to give you the benefit of my experience. That is why I stepped down.

Douglas McAllister: I appreciate that. Thank you, Lord Mulholland.

Chair: Lord Mulholland, would the system have been more difficult if the person who is Lord Advocate did not have a formal deputy with full legal powers to act on their behalf and almost be another pair of hands?

Lord Mulholland: The Solicitor General is there to assist the Lord Advocate, so you have that. When I was out of the country, the Solicitor General would effectively act as the Lord Advocate. You are out of the country from time to time. Tripoli is an example, but there are others. You have to meet your counterparts overseas on extradition, for example, to find out what is going on. For example, the serious and organised crime legislation in Scotland, which is used frequently these days, came from a visit I made to Vancouver to speak to Canadian prosecutors. That was brought back from our discussions. From time to time, you see criticism of Law Officers’ air miles, but I never, ever saw it that way, although you do bring it back.

Should we take the work away? I wouldn’t have thought so. As Lord Advocate, you sometimes take 50 decisions a day on very important matters.

Chair: My point really was—

Lord Mulholland: Was that too much?

Chair: No. My point really was that if you didn’t have a Solicitor General, your role would be that much harder.

Lord Mulholland: Oh yes, I agree with that. You need a Solicitor General. The other thing about a Solicitor General is that if they want to step up to become a Lord Advocate, it is a good way to train the next Lord Advocate. In history, there have been a number of Solicitors General. In the 19th century, I think, there were four at one stage, when the Crown Office was a lot smaller. I certainly think that you need another Law Officer at least.

We are very grateful to you for giving up your time. We understand that you have a busy schedule, so thank you very much.
