Q2 The Chair: Thank you both again very much for making the time to be with us today. The purpose of today’s session is to understand the impact of wind and sun intermittency and how potential gaps in energy delivery from renewables could be filled. Let me start by asking the first question: what is Dunkelflaute? When you address this, could you do so from a meteorological perspective and an energy sector perspective? I would be interested to know whether it is quantifiable. How is it measured and how is it defined? Lastly, how useful do you think the term Dunkelflaute is for modelling and preparing for the risk of energy shortfalls from renewables? Professor Bell, I will come to you first.

Professor Keith Bell: Yes, certainly. I will have to leave it to Dr Wallace. Hopefully Emily will get here soon to address the meteorological phenomena behind it, for example, how long it might last and how you can forecast it. From an energy system perspective, there is no set definition.

The Chair: Briefly, from your knowledge, what is Dunkelflaute?

Professor Keith Bell: When we want to make use of energy from renewable sources to meet demand—wind and solar in particular—we are subject to the variability of wind and solar. When it is not so windy or not so sunny, we have to meet the energy demand from somewhere else, at least the demand for electricity. We can see periods of time when the total available power from renewables is low. That suggests a bigger need for meeting the electricity demand from somewhere else. We could quantify this in terms of residual demand. At any moment in time, what is the demand for electricity and what is available from wind and solar? That residual demand, if you like, is the gap. If that persists over some period of time, that is clearly a bigger amount of energy that we need to find from somewhere else. That is what we can quantify and we can use modelling to do that.

We can look over different periods of time. For example, what is that gap over the worst-case day or week, two weeks or four weeks? Of course that will be subject to weather variability as well. We might be able to model weather patterns over a multi-decade period, over 30 or 40 years, depending on what the data is that we have available. Then we can look at something like the 95th or 99th percentile of that gap over those different periods of time. From there, we have to understand what the risk is of not meeting that gap. What would the consequences be? In the first instance, you would hope that demand would reduce. In that period of time, the cost of meeting that electricity demand from other sources will become increasingly high. You would expect that that would be an incentive to try to reduce your use. For example, a few winters ago, at the beginning of the Ukraine war when gas prices went sky high, we saw some industrial users of gas just stopped using it, just stopped production. Therefore, there is that element, which is not ideal, but it would be a feature of how economics works in terms of supply and demand.

Flexing to move when you use energy is possible. For a day, that is totally possible across all sectors, but for a period like five days or a week or more for the domestic sector, especially depending on electricity for heat or for transport, you cannot rely on that demand flexibility, so that gap still remains to meet that demand from somewhere else. I should also say that as a last resort you might have to rely on rationing access to electricity, such as rota electricity disconnections. The last time we did that in the UK was in the early 1970s when fossil fuel supplies were disrupted, so it is a thing that can happen.

The Chair: Excellent, thank you very much indeed. Dr Emily Wallace is with us now from the Met Office. Dr Wallace, please introduce yourself—but first I will put the first question to you, then you can go straight into the question. What is Dunkelflaute from a meteorological point of view from your perspective, and from an energy sector perspective? We would like to try to understand how it is measured and defined, and whether it is quantifiable. Lastly, how useful a term is it for modelling and preparing for the risk of energy shortfalls from renewables?

Dr Emily Wallace: Thank you, and apologies for my late arrival. Unfortunately, Paddington was not playing ball this morning.

The Chair: Thank you very much for taking the time to be with us.

Dr Emily Wallace: It is my pleasure. I am a fellow in weather and climate extremes at the Met Office and I am seconded to the National Energy System Operator as a meteorological adviser there. Finally, I am a visiting professor at the University of Strathclyde. Today I am speaking on behalf of the Met Office, an executive agency of the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade. While we are best known for our UK weather forecasting service, we are also a world-renowned climate research organisation. It is from that point of view that I am talking this morning.

Dunkelflaute—Keith may well have already covered it—literally means doldrums. That is referring to a lack of wind and a lack of sunshine. The meteorological cause of this is anti-cyclones or high pressure sitting over the UK and Europe. When we think about the weather, it is useful to think in large spatial terms because that is how our weather manifests. What we are talking about in a Dunkelflaute situation is a high-pressure system sitting over the UK. Often that will be sitting over northern Europe at the same time. These are large-scale phenomena, and they often stay in place for long periods of time. High pressure means we have descending air, and that is what traps the moisture at the earth’s surface, which why we tend to get drizzle or low cloud associated with these high-pressure systems.

The Met Office refers to that as anti-cyclonic gloom. There was one last year that was long enough and dull enough for us all that the Met Office wrote a blog about when it would end. A typical feel for a Dunkelflaute event is that we are all feeling a bit tedious—and of course it has implications for the energy sector as well. The other feature that you can get with a Dunkelflaute event is cold conditions. In that case it would have more impact on the energy system. I have talked about wind, I have talked about lack of sunshine and I have talked about temperature. All of those have implications for energy systems. Obviously, the wind and the sunshine—or the lack of—will reduce the amount of generation from wind and PV on a large scale and on what we call embedded generation on our houses.

The Chair: Sorry, what was that term you used?

Dr Emily Wallace: Embedded generation, embedded PV panels on people’s roofs, which means that they are suppressing the demand. If they are operating, they are suppressing the demand from those households, as opposed to allowing the whole demand to be seen by the transmission system. The temperature relates quite strongly already to demand for electricity and demand for gas. In that sense, they are very important for our energy system. I suspect Keith may have covered this already. There is no single definition for Dunkelflaute. It is usually focused on the winter months only when we think about Dunkelflaute, which is because we have shorter sunshine hours and tend to see lower sunshine strength radiation in the winter months. For that reason, it is often thought about as a wintertime condition and that descending air trapping cloud is a wintertime phenomenon. We do not tend to see that in the summer months so much.

Meteorologically, it can be defined in a number of different ways. We can define it, as the name suggests, using wind and sunshine amounts, perhaps choosing some particular locations in the UK where we know that there is generation and looking for periods of time where both sunshine and wind are low, the 20th percentile of what is normal at that location. We can define it with just wind. On our current energy system, Dunkelflaute events are primarily controlled by the amount of wind. That is the larger factor within the Dunkelflaute, so just defining on wind is an option. The other alternative is thinking about the cold Dunkelflaute, which is arguably the more important type of Dunkelflaute because that will be increasing the demand at the same time as the wind and solar generation is depressed, so that omits sunshine from the equation.

The final meteorological way of thinking about the problem would be through pressure patterns, and looking for that high pressure. There are a number of different ways of doing that, some using AI approaches to match pressure to predefined pictures. The other would be via something like the North Atlantic Oscillation, which is a measure of the tendency for winds to come from the west or for that prevalence of westerly winds to be reduced at two ends of the spectrum. In terms of defining on energy, some of the common ways of defining Dunkelflaute would be just on generation, either observed generation from wind and solar or modelled generation taken from a meteorological condition. There are a number of translations between what the wind is doing, what the sunshine is doing and what the temperature is doing towards generation.

The other approach is through demand net renewables. This is taking again either observed or modelled demand, what we expect the demand to be, and subtracting the amount of renewable generation. High-demand net renewables mean that we have a lot of demand to be met with not so much in the way of renewables to meet that demand. It probably means that we are using gas or interconnectors at the moment. If we have low-demand net renewables or indeed negative, that means that we will not be using so much gas. That is the opposite of a Dunkelflaute.

The final way—Keith and Jon may be able to step in to describe some of the mechanics of this more than I can—would be to use something called capacity expansion models. These models are energy system models. One of the uses of them is to chart out potential pathways to meet particular conditions—for example, net zero 2050. A capacity expansion model takes, or can take, year-by-year weather conditions and build out the technologies that are required to meet demand for those weather conditions day by day. A way of defining Dunkelflaute or these conditions is something called system-defining occurrences within that modelling process, where there is a big step change in the technology that is required to meet a particular weather condition, perhaps because in the time series something as bad has not been met before. There may be some additional details that fellow panel members can add in there.

In terms of definition, the best-practice approach is take a cumulative view of Dunkelflaute, because one of the things that can be particularly taxing is if it is going on day after day. A very severe Dunkelflaute may be less important, depending on the energy system, than one that is perhaps less deep but goes on for a number of days. As I say, intensity is important. There is no specific definition for Dunkelflaute, but we do have models and data. There is a lot of rich information on what we can expect to experience today and what we can expect to experience in decades to come coming out of climate simulations.

It is that rich information combined with understanding of energy systems or energy system modelling that we can use to understand whether or not our system is resilient or what it takes for our systems to be resilient. The important part is that collaboration between the rich information coming out of climate modelling and how we apply that to energy systems. It is advocating for that data-driven evidence approach, because what matters is that it is system dependent, it is situation dependent and it is application dependent—so that rich approach is needed.

How useful a term is it? It is useful for communicating compound risk. I described that picture of the large scale and the long term, the combination that means that you might get cold at the same time. It is a term that helps us to communicate that compound aspect, so it supports having a conversation. However, it lacks the meteorological and engineering precision that we need to say whether we are resilient to a Dunkelflaute. It is not a question you can answer—it comes back to the genuine collaboration between the rich information we have about the climate and what it could do and how we build our energy system.

Q3 The Chair: Thank you very much. Jon Saltmarsh, over to you. Could you keep your remarks brief? We have several more questions.

Jon Saltmarsh: I will be very brief because my two colleagues have covered this in immense detail. I absolutely agree that it is a useful shorthand, but if you try to focus down on what we mean in terms of whether it is two weeks, one week or 24 hours, that does not help. The second point to think about is that it is not just about the supply of energy. You need to think about demand as well. Within the Catapult’s modelling, we talk about gaps rather than a Dunkelflaute, because it is not just a Dunkelflaute. You mentioned the cold weather and cold weather drives up demand. It is the gap between supply and demand that is an important bit.

It is also broader than the single event. Generally, it is defining the amount of storage that you want as far as your energy system is concerned. However, there are two ways to deplete storage, one of which is to have a long period with slightly below average wind or renewables or generation, whatever you are particularly measuring. The other is to have an intense period. In a lot of the scenarios, we see that it is not the intense period of a two-week Dunkelflaute that is the problem but the 100 days of 5% below average wind. That is important from a planning basis. You very much need to state what you mean by a Dunkelflaute and how you are measuring it. Clearly the amount of storage that you require varies dramatically, depending on your mix of renewables and firm generation. Most of our modelling would recommend a balance between renewables and nuclear. Generally, the more nuclear you put in, the higher the costs are of the overall generation, but the less storage.

The Chair: We can come on to specific technologies later.

Jon Saltmarsh: That covers all the extra points that I wanted to make.

The Chair: We will come on to that and talk about trends. Professor Bell, do you have anything to add to that?

Professor Keith Bell: Yes. I would favour looking at things from an energy system perspective, because that is what we are trying to deliver—reliable supplies of energy. What Emily called demand net renewables I call residual demand. We need to be able to quantify that and we can do that using the models. We can look at that and the risk over different periods of time, given the variability of the weather over long periods. We might define it in a 24-hour sense or one week, four weeks, or indeed—as Jon suggested—even 100 days and look at a particular infrastructure mix of where we are getting the energy from, what the demand is and the different options of that, how much we want to build and what storage capacity and so on. We can run through the models to assess the one-day, one-week, four-week or 100-day risks that we are exposed to. However, in the end we still need to make a judgment about what is an acceptable level of risk, because we cannot eliminate it completely.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: Mr Jon Saltmarsh, your organisation did a joint report not so long ago on the frequency, duration and severity conditions. Jon Saltmarsh: We look backwards rather than trying to predict forwards. Probably the best data for this comes from a Royal Society report that Sir Chris Llewellyn Smith did a couple of years ago on large-scale electric storage, where he looked back over 37 years, from 1980 to 2016. He analysed the gaps in lots of different ways, identifying 22-hour gaps, which was one of the peaks of short-duration gaps, up to how many of those months we saw 20% below normal. What it identified was three years, 1997, 2007 and 2013, where there was a whole month where wind and solar output was 65% below average, and that was one of its forcing functions. We have done similar: we ended up identifying a two-week period as probably the worst situation that we saw in the eight years that we were looking at. The model that we had for generation and the mix of generation resulted in a 15 terawatt-hour gap that needed to be plugged with some form of long-duration storage, if that answers the question.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: That is very helpful. Does it help us predict these situations going forward?

Jon Saltmarsh: I would turn to Emily on whether past history is a forecast of the future. I do not know how climate change is likely to impact that.

The Chair: We will be coming on to that, so we can park that.

Baroness McIntosh of Pickering: Professor Bell, do you have anything to add?

Professor Keith Bell: Yes, a couple of quick things. We can model what would happen if weather conditions from the past were reproduced. To the extent that future weather patterns are similar to past weather patterns, which we know they are not because climate change is happening, we could say that a particular condition could arise. I expect that it is more difficult to say exactly when it will arise. In some of our modelling, we used 34 years of past weather, put it through a representation of a 2030 electricity system and found similar results to what Jon mentioned in terms of the scale of the gap of 10 to 15 terawatt hours.

The other thing I will quickly mention is you talked about Norway and Denmark. In a sense, Norway especially has faced these questions for a very long time, because it makes use of a lot of hydropower. It has a lot of electricity demand and most of that is met from hydropower. It has a plan for how much water it will get, what the weather will deliver in snow and rain, and plans its reservoir sizes on that basis to manage the possibility of not having enough. This issue has been a live question for it for a very long time, so when you mention collaboration, that is a useful place to look. However, I understand from talking to colleagues that there is still a lot of debate about quite how you model and assess the risk associated with this.

Jon, I think you mentioned five years at the moment, but you also said it was stalled, the development of hydrogen storage. Can you say a little bit about why it has stalled? Professor Bell was talking about molecular storage being a possible way forward for the longer periods when we needed to fill the gap. What is the problem with hydrogen storage?

Jon Saltmarsh: Technically, there is no problem. We have had hydrogen salt caverns in the past in the UK. Most of them have been converted to methane now. You can convert one back again; you can produce new salt caverns. Technically, that is doable. The reason that it appears to be stalled is because of the cost of doing it. We have been very good at funding innovation. You can do an awful lot of innovation for £10 million. When you come to, “We want an end-to-end plant that will electrolyse power, produce hydrogen, store it in the ground and then put a gas turbine on the end of it at a scale that is useful for grid generation” then you are talking £500 million upwards, £1 billion that needs to come from somewhere. The simple answer is that I do not think the Government have had the money to do that and that is predominantly why it has stalled. It is very difficult to do that without putting money into it, because at the moment it is cheaper to do that with a simple unabated gas turbine. It broadly comes down to money.

Q20 Baroness Whitaker: Thank you. We return to intermittency. Baroness Whitaker: I think we are talking about anything that is relevant to addressing Dunkelflaute intermittency.

Jon Saltmarsh: I am not sure how to answer this one. It is driven as much as anything at the moment by when the gas plants are going to go offline. As we have discussed on the previous point, we are not moving fast enough with replacements for those gas turbines. Baroness Whitaker: There are other things. NESO said in July 2025 that this will require investment in new low-carbon generation, but I would also like to turn your attention to the market mechanisms at the moment for getting all these new technologies on to the market. Jon Saltmarsh: This has always been the way. You need the first of a kind funding et cetera to come from somewhere other than the private sector. Baroness Whitaker: There are other reasons for addressing intermittency apart from Dunkelflaute. It is not our concern, but could Dunkelflaute be an incidental gain from the general approach to dealing with intermittency?

Jon Saltmarsh: Absolutely, but as we discussed earlier, you have these two solutions, one for the short-term problems, within day, and one for the longer ones. The resampling of weather years is making its way into calculations around the capacity market and also resource adequacy out to 2040.
