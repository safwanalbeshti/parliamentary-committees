Examination of witness

Witness: Professor Christopher Smith.

Chair: Welcome to today’s meeting of the Science and Technology Committee. Today we are very pleased to welcome Professor Christopher Smith. We are holding a pre-appointment hearing, as he is the Government’s preferred candidate for the role of executive chair of Research England. Research England is responsible for £2 billion-worth of funding, mainly directed at English universities.

Professor Smith has held senior leadership roles in higher education. He is currently the executive chair of the Arts and Humanities Research Council, which is one of the research councils under UKRI, and he is also the UKRI’s international champion. Research England is part of UKRI and supports research and knowledge exchange in higher education institutions.

Our role today is to scrutinise the Government’s decision and to understand the candidate’s intentions for Research England and whether he is qualified to be successful in them. Professor Smith, welcome and thank you for joining us. Let me start by asking you the normal opening question: what attracted you to apply for the role of Research England executive chair?

Professor Smith: I thank you, Chair, and the Committee—it is a pleasure to be here this morning.

Research England represents a tremendous opportunity to continue the work that I have done for six years in UKRI. When I applied to become the executive chair of AHRC in 2020, I very much saw that as an application to join UKRI. I have been hugely attracted by the prospect of UKRI bringing science together in a unified way—both research and innovation—for all the United Kingdom. I have seen my role as executive chair of AHRC—my term has now, properly, come to an end—as part of that.

To move across into Research England is an enormous opportunity. It allows me to look right across the science landscape, which fascinates me. It also allows me to participate in what I think is an extraordinarily important debate, on the way that we sustain the university sector for the long-term future.

Chair: Thank you. I think we will come to that key issue later in our questioning. You have spent a significant amount of time in both the university sector and the research funding sector. You have a number of other commitments, which you have said will “facilitate greater connection across UKRI”. Can you take us through your other major commitments and how they will generate benefits in practice?

Professor Smith: In the past couple of years, we have been working to bring together a set of units that have over time fallen into my remit, which includes the international team, talent and skills, and our research culture and environment team. To that, we will be adding the management of the infrastructure team—not the budget, but the management for our major infrastructure investments.

Chair: Can you give me an example of an infrastructure investment? This is not STFC.

Professor Smith: An example would be CoSTAR—convergent screen technologies and performance in realtime—which AHRC stewards for UKRI. Another would be extensions to the John Innes Centre. These are particular grants that are applied for from the infrastructure fund. They are decided by the infrastructure advisory working group. That budget will remain where it is, but we are bringing the team closer to this group, which we are calling the cross-council capability.

Chair: Is that for the United Kingdom or only for England?

Professor Smith: It is for the United Kingdom—all those operate for the United Kingdom. What this does is bring together a group of people who are stewarding resources that are quite close in some ways to elements of what Research England does through parts of its funding. For instance, within HEIF, there is some infrastructure funding as part of the Research England grant. Research England also contributes to the talent funding through part of the dual funding settlement. You will have seen that the research excellence framework exercise includes an element on research culture. There is a close interconnection between our expectations of open access through REF and our policy work on open science more generally.

On internationalism, what is interesting is that when we fund international work, we fund the domestic side of an international collaboration. We fund UK universities to collaborate, and overseas universities are funded through their own system. There is a tremendous opportunity here to look at the interlocks and interlinkages between these different parts of budgets and have a really strengthened conversation—that conversation has begun already; this is not from scratch—across the staff who are stewarding these critical parts of our underpinning infrastructure, which then allow particular projects to proceed. I think there is a very exciting opportunity here. I genuinely believe that it is a key moment in UKRI’s evolution, and I am looking forward to taking that forward.

Chair: I hear the arguments that you are making, but just to clarify, at Research England you will be responsible for funding in England, and then in your other role, in relation to infrastructure funding, you will have responsibility for the whole United Kingdom. Will you not have a bias towards English funding, given that that will contribute to your perceived success or otherwise?

Professor Smith: To be precise, the decisions on infrastructure funding will be made by a different committee, which I will not chair. I will be responsible for the staff, rather than the decision making—I hope that I would contribute to the strategic thinking—so I do not think I will have a bias in that way.

I have already been operating across most of that agenda. I think that the way that decisions are made is always place-sensitive, and I strongly believe that part of my role in Research England is to foster a very strong relationship, as I know David Sweeney and Jessica Corner have, with the Scottish Funding Council, Medr and the Northern Ireland authorities. As you know, Chair, I have something of a foot in the Scottish camp, since I am seconded from a Scottish university. I believe that we can come to a balanced position across those domains.

Actually, in quite a lot of our strategic thinking about talent and infrastructure, the decisions that UKRI makes have broad significance, obviously, across the UK, but they then need to be taken account of in the way that the English system works. That English system then has some impact, at least ideologically, on the other devolved authorities.

Chair: This is my final question for the moment. You said that you do not intend to seek reappointment at the end of the four-year term. Why?

Professor Smith: First, I will have done a decade in UKRI—that is a good stint. I see this as the last substantive working role that I shall have. The termination will come after the closure of the current REF, but we will have put in place the thinking for future REFs. That looks to me, at the moment, like a good moment to pass over. There are appropriate terms of office for the jobs that we have.

Kit Malthouse: Obviously, your work is filled with lots of acronyms, so before I start my questions, can you just explain to us how the REF works? First of all, for the public, what does it stand for, and what is it designed to do? Then I will ask you some questions about it.

Professor Smith: The research excellence framework is a framework for assessing the excellence of research outcomes—that is to say, the thing that research produces, such as a publication, or the impact that it has, within an analysis of the research culture that produced it. It is based predominantly on an analysis of institutions, but that inevitably requires us to look at the individual products of those institutions. The end result is a map that is graded into categories 1 to 4, with 4 being the top—world-class excellence—for outcomes, impact and research culture. That allows us to see disciplines—my own is classics, but it could be chemistry or whatever—and institutions.

The final outcome of that then influences the proportion of quality-related—QR—funding that we give to institutions. Bluntly, the higher you score in REF, the more money you will receive. That money is then the underpinning to support grants, which UKRI are offered at 80% of full economic cost. That means universities are required to find some other part of that full economic cost. That is done through partly the dual-funding system, where the grant is accompanied by QR. It follows that you should find a correlation between excellence as you look back and the potential to do excellent science in grant applications going forward.

Kit Malthouse: It sounds like quite a complicated system. Do you see it as a strength of the operation of the organisation, or could it be improved?

Professor Smith: A strength of Research England?

Kit Malthouse: Yes.

Professor Smith: First of all, it is a very rigorous exercise and always has been. I have been involved in previous research assessment exercises—and now the REF—throughout my academic career, which, as you can probably tell, has been quite a long one. We have learned how to do this extremely well, and the rest of the world tends to follow what we do. I think the complexity is underpinning rigour rather than an unnecessary set of layers. As you can imagine, it is a major exercise.

Could it be done more simply? One of the things that contributes to the way it is structured is that it has to be negotiated, pretty much by statute, between ourselves and the other funding councils—the Scottish Funding Council, Medr and Northern Ireland. It is delivered by Research England partly in collaboration with them. We have a number of stakeholders. We have consulted substantially with universities who also have their own views on what should be in and out.

Kit Malthouse: There has been some controversy about it over the last couple of years.

Professor Smith: I believe we have arrived now at a very solid accepted REF for 2029. We had a pause instigated by the previous Science Minister, Lord Vallance, to consider some issues, and there was debate. We arrived at, I think, a consolidated view. Research England have produced substantial information about panel compositions and panel criteria for excellence, particularly about the research culture element or the SPRE advice, and that seems to have landed very well in the sector. I think we have got a substantially approved and strong REF exercise that we can run through.

Kit Malthouse: The REF accounts for what—getting on for £500 million in total? And you are saying that is now in a stable state having been through a period of, shall we say, less-than-optimal handling.

Professor Smith: It is always a controversial exercise, but I think we have arrived at a good place.

Kit Malthouse: As you contemplate this job, what other areas of weakness do you think require attention? If that is now sorted, what else needs sorting?

Professor Smith: I would characterise it more in terms of evolution, as you would expect me to. What happens with the outcome of REF is quite a complex formula for distribution of money. Over time, Jessica Corner, the current holder of the position, has started to try to unpick some of that complexity and move towards simplification. We had a transparency exercise that involved the universities, to understand what they use the money for. We introduced some changes to the higher education innovation funding—HEIF—which I think have been welcomed and are useful. We are looking at a further review, which needs to align to the end of REF so that the moment the results are announced we move to a new formulaic system. My intention, and the expectation of the organisation, is that that process will have concluded so that we can move seamlessly into the next formula. I would expect that formula to be simpler.

Kit Malthouse: Simpler, but also to have some winners and losers?

Professor Smith: It already does. The calculation includes quite a sharp gradient, so if you want more winners and losers, or an appropriate gradient for research excellence, that already exists.

Kit Malthouse: But that has a particular impact on smaller institutions, doesn’t it?

Professor Smith: There are two kinds of impact, I think. One is on less research-intensive organisations, and universities do differ in their research intensity according to their mission, role and history. Clearly, the less research intensive you are, the less likely you are to receive very strong marks across the board in the REF, and therefore there will be a consequence in the formula. It is worth saying that on every occasion that we have run this exercise, we have found centres of excellence right across the university system. The difference is the breadth of the research intensity.

Specifically, though, I think you are thinking about small specialist institutions that are single-subject providers. That is going to be the subject of a review that has been announced, which I look forward to engaging with, because there is a very interesting situation where these organisations, when bundled together with multi-faculty universities, look very different. They are often single subject. Their income streams are different because they have different numbers of undergraduates, but they are often highly innovative. They are a miscellaneous group, I should say. The Royal Academy of Music, the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art and the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine are small specialist institutions with very different missions, but often they are doing some very exciting things. We want to protect that capacity for innovation and research at the highest end. We need to see what the right mechanisms are to do that.

Kit Malthouse: Would you say that one of the intrinsic weaknesses in the structure is that assessing excellence is like assessing beauty? If you were awarding grant funding on the basis of beauty, that would be an odd thing to assess on a points-based system, would it not? Maybe you cannot comment on that.

Professor Smith: I am not going to comment on my beauty.

Kit Malthouse: For example, is it possible for a research programme that reaches a dead end to be seen as excellent if it rules something out? If research into Alzheimer’s finds a dead end, could that get money?

Professor Smith: Yes. One strength—I think this is really important—of the way that we have been thoughtful about the research assessment exercises we have done, and of our participation in broad thinking internationally about research assessment, is that we are better now than in the past at recognising both confirmatory and negative results as an important science contribution. In other words, reaffirming that something is in fact true—reproducibility, if you like—or saying, “Actually, this is not going to work,” have to have value, and we can recognise them.

On the beauty issue, if one thinks about it, we spend an enormous amount of time in UKRI trying to assess the potential of an application. The ways in which we can buffer against it being, as it were, about the surface rather than the depth are good construction of calls and multiple peer assessors, so that you have a number of opinions contributing. I do not think any system can be perfect, but what the REF has shown regularly is that there is a high correlation between UKRI-funded outputs and their being independently judged as excellent by a panel that is not taking their funding source into account when thinking about the outcome. Do you see what I mean?

Kit Malthouse: Yes, I do, although there is a philosophical argument to be had about where discovery comes from. It does not come from consensus all the time. Some of the greatest discoveries have been made by people who were seen by the scientific establishment as mad.

Professor Smith: The challenge of divergent peer review is something that we have been looking at a lot. You will have seen that there have been quite a lot of statements by UKRI recently about how to assess. You are absolutely right about divergent peer review—in other words where part of the committee says, “This is marvellous,” and part of the committee says, “This is terrible”. The natural habit of averaging will take that down.

Kit Malthouse: If you look back in history—sorry to stray off point, Chair—to the discovery that stomach ulcers were caused by bacteria, everybody else said that was total nonsense but there were two scientists who said, “We think there is something here.” Your committee would probably have said, “They are mad and we are not giving them any money” because there is a consensus. It is a philosophical point; I will just park that with you because I am running out of time.

Chair: Okay, but it was a good point.

Kit Malthouse: May I ask about funding and cost pressures, and what your appreciation of that is at the moment?

Professor Smith: Just so I answer the question precisely, do you mean in the university ecosystem? Is that your focus?

Kit Malthouse: I mean facing Research England. Some of your funding is effectively being depreciated by inflation and that is going to cause you all sorts of problems. You are not getting massive increases over the next few years. If anything, I think some parts of your money are being held flat, are they not?

Professor Smith: Across UKRI, some parts of the budget are held flat—quite a lot of the budget is being held flat—with specific areas of greater focus that follow the UK Government’s industrial strategy. In relation to Research England, the key thing is that our obligation is to manage a balance of funding so that the amount of money that is spent on grants is matched by a proportion that is spent through Research England. We have more or less sustained that balance. Although obviously everybody could spend more given the chance, Research England is continuing to support a balanced funding settlement.

There is a reduction, if you look specifically at individual pilot projects that Research England did of particular funds. They have been absorbed more into the business as usual of the organisation. I do not see that as a cut; I see that as an integration of Research England into the broader budget. It is important that we keep that balance of funding because one of the key things is that allows universities to continue to fund research that is not project funded through their own funding. That allows some of your unfunded activity to continue.

Kit Malthouse: Maya will ask you a bit about universities, but my understanding is that QR funding over the last few years has fallen in real terms by something like 16.5% and the gap has effectively only been plugged by foreign students. Maya has further questions on university funding.

Chair: Before we come to Maya, do you recognise or not, Professor Smith, that Research England is facing funding pressures?

Professor Smith: I will try to answer the question directly by saying that I am not convinced that Research England is facing a specific funding pressure. I believe that UKRI has sustained a reasonable balance of funding across its various responsibilities. Within the fixed science budget we have, Research England faces the pressure that the science budget has rather than a particular one for itself.

That is important because what I do not want us to do—it is certainly not going to be part of my intent—is start from the position that we are somehow massively impoverished and therefore need money to swing across from other budgets. It is the nature of the way that our budget is funded that it is on a balance calculated against other parts of the budget. Therefore, if the budget comes under pressure at one end, it will come under pressure at ours, but proportionately, not disproportionately.

Kit Malthouse: I will ask you the question a different way. Could you do more with more, and would it be a good investment?

Professor Smith: Yes and yes, but I would not say that what we should be doing is taking that disproportionately from other sides because that would create other problems. A rising science budget will raise the Research England budget, and that will be good.

Chair: That was a masterly political answer, I must say.

Professor Smith: Thank you, Chair.

Chair: You talked about a fixed science budget. We do have a rising science budget. Certainly, it is one of the Government’s claims to be investing more in science. Do you characterise it as a fixed or rising science budget?

Professor Smith: It is a rising science budget, but the UKRI allocation is fixed through the comprehensive spending review—that is what I meant.

Chair: Thank you for the clarification. I was not asking you to reevaluate the distribution of funds within UKRI; I was asking whether Research England faces funding pressures.

Professor Smith: As I say, it is a very different beast in a sense from the rest of UKRI because the budget is, to an extent, calculated as a proportion of what is happening elsewhere. That is how dual funding works. Therefore, what we offer to the system in some ways is perhaps a better way to do it. What is the impact of the amount that we have on the university system? Is it sufficient for them to achieve what they need to do?

For instance, I might make an argument around AHRC based on something along the lines of the success rate of applicants and say that because the success rate is low but the quality is very high, there is a significant pressure on my budget, or that there is a particular commitment that I have which is difficult to sustain because of changes in allocations. Now that is a particular kind of pressure. The Research England pressure is a different one. It is calculated on the balance of funding. Then, you would argue—you may wish to come to this now—whether that balance of funding offered is sufficient for universities to do their business. I am trying to get away from the notion that the pressure is on us internally in the system because I do not think it is, so much as it is on the quantum of funding that is available—I am not trying to dodge the question, Chair.

Chair: I think to a certain extent you are, but I also would say that in today’s environment, if any public body does not think it is facing funding pressures, it is being overfunded. There should be pressures on all bodies to make the best use of public funding. There certainly should be pressures on Research England to ensure that every pound is well spent.

Professor Smith: I misunderstood your challenge. To address that, the challenge to make the Research England pound go as far as it can, and to be as efficient and effective as it can, is profound. Part of the reason for looking at the formula is to ensure that its simplicity makes it easier to deliver and use, but also to be certain that it is answering the levers that we expect across the whole of the science budget, which would include advancing knowledge, improving lives and driving growth as the UKRI mission. “Can we show that that budget is effectively delivering?” is in some sense what REF does, because REF analyses the combined effect of grant funding, not just from UKRI, but across the system and the dual funding balance. Therefore, if we have not seen an extremely strong performance by universities in REF, then somehow or other we are not producing the excellence that our funding should do. There is an in-built test there. Internally in the organisation, the strategic institutional research funding—SIRF—which we are undertaking will be an extremely high-pressure moment for Research England to be able to demonstrate. I apologise; I misunderstood the direction of your challenge.

Chair: Thank you very much. We are now going to look at the implications for universities in more detail.

Maya Ellis: Good morning, Professor Smith. I am Maya Ellis, MP for up in Ribble Valley, which is in Lancashire. We have the brilliant Lancaster University, a research university, and the University of Lancashire. I am very aware of some of the pressures that universities are facing. I have a couple of questions that I will ask all at once so that you can give me your picture.

First, it would be good to get your headline as to how you would characterise the state of the university system at the moment. What are the two or three things that you see as the key pressures? Secondly, what do you see as Research England’s role in supporting the university sector? Critically, what do you hope to bring with your leadership that perhaps you have not seen previously, and what, in future, would you like to do differently to improve and get a grip on the university sector?

Professor Smith: I will start, if I may, by saying something about how brilliantly universities are doing. I do not think that we say this often enough. Right now, students will be going into classrooms to be taught by brilliant people, postgraduates will be thinking things that have not been thought before and students will be entering laboratories and libraries and will be supported by extraordinary technical staff right across the piece—one could go on. I think that it is important that we recognise that we have a genuinely world-class university system that is excitingly diverse and that is a really good thing for the UK.

When we move to the challenges, I think that the baseline needs to be that this is a performing sector. It is not a non-performing or failing sector; it is a brilliant sector. The diagnosis of some of the challenges to universities is now pretty well known. There are a number of vectors of pressure, both internally in the UK and internationally that are impacting on our universities in England and also in Scotland and Wales. However, as you know, I have a lot of connections across Europe, and every European country is finding different sorts of challenges in its university sector.

The key set of conversations that we need to have are about how we maintain and fund a diverse university sector that is excellent in research but also able to do the other things that universities across the sector and ecosystem do. I am going to resist the question of, “What am I going to do that my predecessors did not do?” I always find that one never steps into the shoes that one’s predecessor stepped into. Jessica Corner has already begun many of the conversations that we now need to continue in depth: the review of the distribution of funding; the conversations with the new leadership at the Office for Students and the maintenance of conversations with the new leader at the Scottish Funding Council—who I am looking forward to engaging with. Those conversations right across the United Kingdom are a critical part of how we think about an interlocking university system. There are obviously other partners as well, including Universities UK. What I would want to do first is strengthen all the time the conversation around what the next steps are going to be. We have a post-16 education White Paper and the very interesting challenges of the Milburn review. All of those have impacts on undergraduate funding and teaching income streams that will impact on the capacity of universities to do research.

Coming to the dual funding part, cost under-recovery of research means that universities have made a massive investment in our research ecosystem. They have paid for a lot of the research themselves. If that is reduced because of pressures of teaching income, then we will need to assess what that does and what we need to think about as we move our system forward into a world where science is going to look very different. That difference in science needs to be in constant communication with the pedagogy that universities have, because they are producing the skills base that will deliver the science that we will need in five or six years’ time.

I want to emphasise that the role of Research England, and my role in Research England, is to sit within a really rich but purposeful conversation about not only challenges but solutions. Those are some of the reasons why the reviews that have been set off as so key.

Maya Ellis: I have two brief questions on demonstrating your engagement and understanding of the leadership of universities. Can you tell me the key thing that you have heard from vice-chancellors that you think they want you to understand? Also, what is one hard truth that you think they will have to hear and accept over the next few years?

Professor Smith: My sense is that vice-chancellors want us to hear about the extraordinary things that they do, and to be ever more determined to celebrate that success. Universities make an outsized contribution, and we do not always talk about it enough. The return is that one of the reasons why we maybe don’t talk about it enough is that that achievement is often quite granular and can be quite localised. The conversations in the White Paper about specialisation and collaboration mean we are going to have to think about how we raise individual achievement into a more structural advance for the country. That is not to say that universities do not try, but that is the conversation we are all having: how do we drive growth? How do we make that work regionally, and then nationally and internationally?

Maya Ellis: Thank you. That is really clear.

Chair: You said that science is going to look very different in the future. Can you say briefly how science will look different?

Professor Smith: First of all, it seems that no meeting is allowed to end without some mention of generative AI. I would like to think about my experience of seeing the impact of automation on science in other countries.

Chair: You said it as a statement—“Science is going to look very different in the future”—so I assumed you had a vision in your head of how it would look different.

Professor Smith: One of the consequences of those impacts, and of the way that funding works, is that we will be looking really hard at how teams are constructed and at where science takes place. It is already the case that 70% of science happens in companies, outside universities. We are seeing the rise of research-focused organisations, which are quite small, sharply targeted investments to solve particular bottlenecks in the science system. I think that is quite possibly going to be a much larger feature of our future. Interestingly, the relationship between that and Research England, which funds universities, is somewhat distant. It is those sorts of questions.

Chair: Right. That is what I thought you were referring to—the impact of AI and changing economic structures on science funding. That is very interesting.

Martin Wrigley: Moving on, we see everything changing, including research, at an ever-increasing rate. You are describing a system that sounds very stable, static, fair and, judged from an academic point of view, dispassionate about the science and innovation it is achieving. Is that still fit for purpose?

Professor Smith: That is a super interesting question that I have been thinking about a great deal. The Chair will probably be infuriated by me saying this, but I am going to say yes and no. The yes part is that structures of science—even the ones I have been describing as changing— often change through evolution, not radical shift. For instance, if we wanted to set up a suite of research-focused organisations, they would require PhDs, they would require staff, they would require integration into existing laboratories and so on. There is some value in ensuring that we have stability through part of the system in order for parts of it to change fast. I do not think that is an uncommon situation across much public funding—in order to allow for innovation and fast change, the ground on which you stand must be reasonably firm.

Then the question is, are we good enough at managing that fast pace from a stable base? I can genuinely say that every funding council I know across Europe is asking itself that question: how can we move public money—which is therefore subject to all sorts of rules of accountability—quickly into challenge-driven science? There are mechanisms. We can see that the pace of decision in UKRI is moving more quickly, and I am delighted to say that the Arts and Humanities Research Council has one of the fastest turnarounds on grants in UKRI. We see Innovate UK moving extremely quickly in some of its funding, and I think there are ways in which we can support universities themselves to be more agile in the way they engage with industry. That is, indeed, one of the purposes of HEIF.

But the challenge you make is a very fair challenge to all funding systems where the combination of choice, assurance and the capacity of human beings to move into the work they are choosing to do puts delay into systems where science is moving very fast across a very large landscape. That, of course, includes our international competitors as well. So yes, I think that stable assessment to allow for a strong base is vital. Do we go fast enough? No. I think we shall find this a really significant challenge over the coming decade.

Martin Wrigley: Thank you; that is interesting. I recall from my experiences in working in technology during the dotcom bubble that the skill was in moving at full speed and pivoting at the same time to change direction entirely. Do you think this bucket-based financing approach is helping us, or is it detracting from what we are doing?

Professor Smith: Let me give a very specific example of where it is helping in the area that I have responsibility for, which is industrial strategy for creative industries. We have, as a result of the allocation, the largest settlement we have ever had for R&D in creative industries. Creative industries are currently running at something like five times the economic growth of the rest of the sector. We are able to invest pretty quickly in high-potential companies through Innovate funding, but we have also put in place CoSTAR infrastructure that is UK-wide, which is moving companies into access to the highest-end technology at very low cost and low friction. We are democratising access to technology that they would not have had the chance to access before, and we are seeing micro and small and medium-sized enterprises benefiting really fast in that structure.

Right now in Salford, the BEYOND conference, which is the national conference for creative industries, is celebrating a whole range of phenomenal achievements that are happening through both UKRI funding and the sector itself. That will include the announcement this afternoon of five new clusters for creative industries, which will be up and running in months’ time, but are building on existing activity. I think the industrial strategy focus has really helped us in creative industries. We struggled to have that sharpness of attention before, and I am thrilled to see what my successor will be able to do with that, because it is very, very exciting.

Martin Wrigley: Okay, but if it is gaining in one area, that takes away some of the stability and fairness that you are trying to describe in the previous space.

Professor Smith: Not necessarily. Again, I will be domestic about this, just because I know the figures very well. The AHRC applicant-led mode budget has not changed. It is stable and slightly rising across the comprehensive spending review. It is not calculated council by council, but roughly speaking the consequence of that is that the other side of the balance will have been sustained on the Research England side, because the grant level is the same. So there is quite a reasonable balance for my own areas, and I think those basic facts would be true of other research councils.

Chair: Is it not the case that curiosity-driven research has effectively received flat cash funding, which is a real-terms cut? The quality-driven research funding, which is what Research England receives, has benefited from real-terms funding increases. I do not understand what you are saying about curiosity-driven research not being impacted.

Professor Smith: Let me be really quite specific. I will speak for my own council, but this is not incomparable across the piece inside the research councils. Effectively, our budget had three components, the first of which was skills, which has not changed. Under the last Government we made cuts to our skills budget to balance our budget, which I will continue to defend. That was our choice, and it redistributes cash across. Secondly, we had funding for strategic projects, and thirdly, we had funding for the applicant-led mode, which is exactly what it was before. The strategic funding has translated into the industrial strategy. That is the big difference.

The challenge we have is that our assumption that something that happens in, say, the research-led part of creative industries does not include curiosity is wrong, just as when we ran strategic calls, they were often extremely open to inventive and new ways of approaching problems—

Chair: Are you saying that curiosity-driven research is not simply curiosity-driven research but also industry-driven research?

I will give a good example: I think more or less every project should have a designer in it. Design is a phenomenally powerful tool to drive your idea into application and implementation. That is one of the reasons why we have proudly championed design in AHRC for UKRI.

In fairness, the way that the Government framed the industrial strategy was that there is an overarching desire to drive growth that has certain choices within certain sectors, but it was never the Government’s intent, as I understand it, that we should funnel that ambition into highly particular interventions; it was that we should simply recognise the areas that have the greatest opportunity.

Life sciences, AI and high-value manufacturing are things of multiple significance. For instance, life sciences will depend on high-value manufacturing for drug production or surgical instruments. That is what we should be driving. I saw recently that Innovate UK made some grants precisely on medical instrumentation. Does that answer your question?
