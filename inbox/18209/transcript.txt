Witnesses: Stephen Kiely, Kai O’Doherty and Professor Ilias Trispiotis.

Q1 Chair: Good afternoon, everyone. Today, we are holding our first oral evidence session on the Government’s draft Conversion Practices Bill. The Joint Committee has been appointed to consider and report on the draft Bill, which sets out provisions to prevent and criminalise abusive and harmful conversion practices. I say now, on behalf of the Committee, many thanks to all our witnesses today for attending.

The Committee is dealing with highly sensitive matters, of which many people watching or listening may have direct personal experience. We understand that some people may find today’s evidence session distressing and difficult to listen to. There are strongly and honestly held opinions, and we ask all those listening to do so respectfully.

Lastly, there are several organisations that can offer support; these are listed on our website. I am sure that we all wish to approach these very important matters with due care and respect. To begin, may I ask the witnesses to introduce themselves?

Professor Trispiotis: I am chair in human rights law at the University of Leeds. I also chair the legal forum of the Ban Conversion Practices Coalition.

Kai O’Doherty: I am the director of policy and research at Galop, which is the UK’s anti-abuse charity for LGBT+ people.

Stephen Kiely: I am the head of policy and public affairs at Stonewall, which is a national LGBT organisation that has been campaigning for equality and inclusion for nearly the past 40 years.

Chair: I thank the three of you. Before I hand over to the Committee so that we can start our questions, I have two points to note. Danny Beales sends his apologies for not being here today as he has an unfortunate clash with another committee further down the building; he asked me to put that on the public record. Secondly, I remind everybody about the sub judice rule—that is, not to refer to ongoing, live, extant court cases. With that, I pass over to Lord Isaac.

Q2 Lord Isaac: May I start by declaring my interest as a former chair of Stonewall and the Equality and Human Rights Commission? I should also indicate that I know Stephen Kiely and have met him from time to time in his capacity as the head of policy at Stonewall.

How prevalent are conversion practices in England and Wales? What do they typically entail? Can you tell us where conversion practices generally take place? In your view, are any groups at particular risk? How great a concern are overseas practices? That is quite a few questions, but can I ask you to start with how prevalent conversion practices are in England and Wales?

Stephen Kiely: First, I thank the Committee for taking evidence from us here today. We have been really impressed by the engagement from the Committee so far; it is clear that you want to move at pace, which is really welcome. We have been campaigning on this Bill for eight years, so it is good to see that we are all on the same timeline now.

Before I start, I want to say that, although this is a complex piece of legislation, it has a very simple purpose: to ban and prevent a very specific form of abuse. We do see that abuse happening in real life, though. The Government’s own national LGBT survey found that 5% of respondents had been offered conversion practices and 2% had undergone them. We also have new research now, which will come out next Tuesday—I can send advance copies to everyone—from a poll that we did with YouGov of some 3,500 LGBT adults. It found that 4% of respondents had been offered conversion practices and 1% had undergone them.

We find that trans people, people from ethnic minorities and people from religious backgrounds are around two to three times more likely to face conversion practices. We also have live anecdotal evidence from Galop showing that its services are repeatedly being used by people who present having gone through conversion practices across the UK. So, unfortunately, this is a real and live issue that continues to be present in different communities and different societies.

Kai O’Doherty: From a Galop point of view, we see hundreds of contacts around conversion practices annually. We are an anti-abuse charity, so a lot of these cases are in a domestic abuse context. However, research that we put out in 2022—a representative quantitative analysis—showed that there were disproportionate levels of risk depending on parts of the community. For example, four in 10 trans respondents had been offered or undergone conversion practices, and the level was similarly high for non-binary people. Asexual and aromantic people were also at higher risk, with one in three having been offered or undergoing conversion practices, and young people aged between 18 and 24 were also disproportionately likely to be impacted. It is also true that people living in rural areas were more likely to be impacted.

In terms of where the practices happen, Government data and international literature are clear that the majority of conversion practices happen in religious settings, but the level in pseudo-healthcare settings was also quite high. According to the Government’s research, 51% of conversion practices happened in religious settings, 19% happened in healthcare settings and 16% happened in family settings. However, in Galop’s most recent report, “Still Not Illegal”—we are proud to say that it was used to show the testimonies and evidence of hundreds of cases of conversion practices that have come to our services in the past three years—we found that the majority of those contexts were actually in coercive and controlling behaviours, with parents being the most likely to be the perpetrators of that abuse. However, religious settings were still very prevalent, at 27% of the cases that we analysed, with 13% of them happening in pseudo-healthcare environments.

Professor Trispiotis: I will come at this question from a comparative perspective. We operate in a context where the United Nations and the Council of Europe have put forward specific recommendations for conversion practices to be banned in law, specifically in criminal law. A number of countries across the world have banned these practices. Many of those countries have followed recommendations from survivor-led studies published in peer-reviewed journals, which show beyond any doubt and in the most credible way that conversion practices affect a wide range of LGBT+ people. These practices take place in a wide range of contexts and are often clandestine and are under-reported. Many of these studies are cited in the UN’s SOGI report from 2020, as well as in the Council of Europe’s resolution. There are survivor-led studies from Canada, Victoria in Australia and, recently, Norway. They share many of their findings with the findings that Galop and Stonewall have put forward.

I want quickly to add something on your extraterritorial application question. We operate in the context that I just described. However, conversion practices are still not illegal in most countries across the world. There is a clear risk that conversion practices might be facilitated from within a country and take place elsewhere, so it is very positive that the draft Bill includes an extraterritorial provision. We can discuss this more technically if you have more questions about it.

Q3 Lord Issac: What do the conversion practices typically entail? I know that this is a sensitive area, but it is important for the Committee to understand what you believe the conversion practices typically to be.

Kai O’Doherty: Again, research suggests that those settings are the most consistent. In terms of what the practices themselves look like, in our 2022 report, “There was nothing to fix”, we looked at the range of behaviours occurring in the UK. They include things such as being pressured or forced to seek or participate in medical or psychotherapeutic practices or interventions; being manipulated, controlled or having their movement monitored; being threatened with homelessness or being made homeless; being threatened with or made to travel to another country; being verbally, emotionally and psychologically abused; or being sexually abused.

By way of an example, I want to bring the Committee recent cases that have to come to Galop. We spoke to a young lesbian woman, Jane, who had experienced conversion practices from her parents’ extended families and members of her church. Often, cases involve multiple perpetrators. Upon discovering her sexuality, Jane’s parents forced her to bathe in holy water and she was taken to another part of the country where the church publicly shamed her, forced her to the floor and dragged her along it to change her sexuality. When she refused to go to that church any longer, her parents physically abused her and said that she was better off dead. At this point, Jane fled from the house and was sofa-surfing for several weeks.

Importantly, social workers failed to understand this as conversion practices, advising that she be housed back with her extended family, who were part of that community. She was homeless for two months and it was only with support from Galop that we were able to find her alternative housing. This also demonstrates that most conversion practices happen as part of a pattern. They happen within different settings, and they can look like different incidents which, added up, create this clear abuse.

Q4 Baroness Brown of Silvertown: I am glad you talked about other jurisdictions. My understanding is that there have been no prosecutions in other jurisdictions. On that basis, what do the panel think the impact might be of not passing this legislation?

Professor Trispiotis: Thank you very much for the question. You are right that there have not been many prosecutions in other jurisdictions that have banned conversion practices, but we should put this in its relevant context. The bans that have been enacted in Victoria, Canada, Norway, France and Germany are very recent bans. Those jurisdictions have legislated over the past two to five years and there has not been time for the prosecutions to be put forward and for we academics and researchers to be able to analyse them. There might not have been many prosecutions, but it is difficult to find out specific numbers. As a law professor and an expert in this area, I have looked, and the data for some of those prosecutions is not readily available to the public. Also, many of those offences are very recent.

On the second part of your question, about what would be the effect of this legislation if it gets passed—

Baroness Brown of Silvertown: If it does not get passed.

Professor Trispiotis: If it does not get passed, LGBT+ people will still be at risk of conversion practices in our country. From the research that Galop, Stonewall and others have carried out, including the Government’s impact assessment of this legislation, we know that these practices are taking place in this country. We know that they fall between the gaps of existing criminal offences, so if the law does not get passed, unfortunately the protection will not be enough.

Stephen Kiely: If the law does not get passed, it will be hard for statutory authorities to identify conversion practices. By their nature, conversion practices are extremely hidden and complex, so it is very hard to capture data, much like with domestic abuse. Often, people do not know that they are going through it for a very long time. Consent is an issue. Passing a law will give clear guidance to statutory authorities, courts and professionals that conversion practices are happening, but we also need to think about the deterrent effect. They are happening, they are real and present, and anything we can do to pass robust legislation in this place is really important.

Kai O’Doherty: What we can learn from other jurisdictions is to make sure that this Bill is not weaker than those Bills. We already see the ways in which this Bill is weaker than international precedents, as well as the suggestions and recommendations of the United Nations. It is a great start. We are really happy to see what we have before us, but we urge you, throughout this process, to understand that, by only covering, for example, change and not suppression, and by having a distinction between abusive and non-abusive conversion practices, this Bill is setting a different precedent from international comparators. We need to make sure that it is not weaker, if we are already seeing that those Bills are not being used as they ought to be.

Q5 Olivia Bailey: I start by drawing everyone’s attention to my entry in the register of interests, in particular that I was the Minister responsible for drafting this legislation initially and, subsequent to being Minister, in my capacity as Chair of the Women and Equalities Committee, I have had a number of meetings with a number of today’s panellists. In my capacity as Minister, I met most of the panellists we are hearing from.

Thank you very much for your answers so far. To follow on from Baroness Brown’s question, as a Committee we have heard evidence from people arguing that existing criminal legislation should be sufficient to prosecute conversion practice abuse. Would you like to say anything about that? Why do you think there is a necessity for criminal legislation in this space?

Professor Trispiotis: Thank you for the question. Existing legislation is insufficient to protect people from conversion practices. Coercive control—the offence of controlling or coercive behaviour—is insufficient because it covers only people in former or current intimate relationships and family members, and we know from the evidence that we have that conversion practices do not take place just within these contexts.

Hate crime, and the regulation of hate crime, is also insufficient because it would require a basic offence that then is aggravated on the basis of hostility towards someone. Again, conversion practices very often do not involve an underlying offence. Very often, the motivation might be benevolent, so they would not be captured by hate crime. With regard to tort law, negligence and harassment would also be inadequate to protect people from conversion practices. In terms of tort law, private law and the law of negligence, it would also be insufficient to protect LGBT+ people from conversion practices because it would be very difficult to prove that, especially a pseudo-therapist, for example, had a duty of care towards someone that was breached, and that that breach led to the injury that gave rise to the negligence claim.

Tort law, at the same time, has very specific limitation periods. Again, we know from the evidence that we have that conversion practice survivors can take years to realise the harm that these practices have caused them. On a more fundamental level, when we are talking about tort law, negligence and private law, they frame the problem as a private dispute between two parties, whereas what we are describing here, conversion practice, is a public wrong. It is a form of discriminatory abuse that stigmatises and perpetuates the social subordination of a protected group of people, and it is a wrong sufficiently important for criminal law to intervene, as it does with other cases of discriminatory abuse.

Q6 Sir Jeremy Wright: Good afternoon. I should register the fact that I have spoken to all three of you before about this Bill. It is good to see you again. If I may, I will focus on the definition of conversion practice in clause 1 of the Bill. Others will come on to talk about abuse and the definition of serious harm, but I want to focus on that first element and ask you your view on the breadth or narrowness of that concept—and, specifically, one or two aspects of that.

First—this will be familiar to you because we have discussed this point—I want to make sure we are all on the same page and that it is your understanding that conversion practice does include seeking to persuade someone not to adopt either a particular sexual orientation or a particular transgender identity, as well as seeking to change a pre-existing sexual orientation or transgender identity.

My second question, which Kai has already adverted to, is on your view on whether or not—I suspect the answer is not—suppression is included in the current definition. If it is not included, should it be?

Professor Trispiotis: Thank you for the question. On whether the definition covers both change and trying to influence someone to adopt an identity they do not have—

Sir Jeremy Wright: Or not adopt.

Professor Trispiotis: Or not adopt an identity. A hypothetical case I am thinking of in response would involve children who might not have a formed identity, sexual orientation or gender identity. This is an area where we have specific protections for children’s rights, both from the children’s rights Act and internationally, which give children specific rights to development, to non-discrimination and to self-determination. These rights would have to be considered in this context. So the definition of conversion practices that this Bill adopts, and that any future Bill might adopt—they might be different or they might be the same—would have to be interpreted in the light of children’s rights protections that are very well established in the law in England and Wales.

Q7 Sir Jeremy Wright: That is understood, professor. I just want to understand whether, in addition to those perfectly fair points, you accept that the language in clause 1 at the moment would include both scenarios.

Professor Trispiotis: In my view, it includes both scenarios, yes: to believe or not to believe that you have or do not have. The current definition is sufficient to capture change efforts of conversion practices, and it is insufficient to cover suppression acts.

I will add a few things on suppression and then I will pass to my colleagues. Again, to take a comparative lens, this is the first definition of conversion practices that I have encountered—out of all the countries that have banned it, the United Nations report, the Council of Europe recommendations, and the research under way on an EU level—that does not include suppression. All the definitions of conversion practice that I am aware of—

Q8 Sir Jeremy Wright: It might perhaps be helpful to everyone to establish the definitions here. Can you explain to us what your definition of “suppression” would be in this context?

Professor Trispiotis: The definition of “suppression” in this context would be acts, or any other type of conduct, that aim to convince someone to, make someone or control someone so that they deny, for example, their sexual orientation or gender identity, conceal their sexual orientation or gender identity, or behave in ways that are incongruous with their sexual orientation or gender identity. All the examples of bans that exist at the moment have used examples of suppression, either in the text of the legislation itself, as New Zealand does, or in explanatory notes. I am not aware of a jurisdiction that has defined “suppression”, but most jurisdictions, including international recommendations, use illustrative examples of suppression that capture what I just said: concealing, denying, acting in ways that are incongruous, etc.

Kai O’Doherty: To give you real examples of what this means on the ground, conversion practices have evolved over time. They have become increasingly covert. Therefore, people will be wise enough, if they want to continue to abuse, to say not, “Do not be gay,” but just, “Do not show that you’re gay.” I say this as if it is historical, but you will hear from survivors in coming weeks about where that is not historical. Indeed, many religious examples that come to us involve people being told, “Just don’t show anyone you’re gay,” or, “Don’t present as transgender”—but they are not being told not to be those things explicitly. It is crucial that change and suppression are covered in the definition.

Q9 Sir Jeremy Wright: Just to be clear, your view would be that “Don’t be gay” is covered but “Don’t show that you’re gay” currently is not.

Kai O’Doherty: That would be a crude way of putting it, but I would agree. I will give some recent examples from our services. One is clients being blocked from seeing or interacting with friends and forced to end relationships or barred from participating in activities outside of the home, while also being subjected to surveillance of their movements and communication. We know well that, in domestic abuse, those would be versions of coercive control, but when that happens in a religious setting, as Ilias has explained, that is not covered.

A client being refused food until they deny that they are LGBT+—is that change or is that suppress? There is a reason why every other country has felt the need to include both, and it is because the change act is so overly specific that it does not account for the evolution of these practices. I fail to understand what is unique about Britain that means that we feel that it is immoral and abusive to change someone’s sexual orientation or gender identity but that it would be okay to be told not to express or to suppress it. I do not think that holds weight.

Stephen Kiely: This is an area that warrants a lot of discussion. We need to make sure we get this right because this is a critical part of the Bill. We find, in suppression, that it can be very insidious and corrosive when people are told that, if they ever acted on being gay or thought about being gay—if they ever went near it—religious leaders, parents and such would know that they were gay. That has a real corrosive, long-term and life-changing impact on your identity. That is very much a part of suppression, which needs to be included in this part of the Bill.

Q10 Sir Jeremy Wright: Just to make sure that we have covered the ground, is there anything any of you want to add on either the narrowness or the breadth of the current definition of conversion practice? As I say, we will come on to the other elements in a moment.

Professor Trispiotis: Can I make a quick point? At the moment, the definition of conversion practices does not include the discriminatory presumption. Again, to take a comparative lens, most definitions of conversion practices that exist, including the UN recommendations and the Council of Europe resolution, describe conversion practices as efforts to change or suppress sexual orientation or gender identity on the basis that this identity is considered inferior. Historically and sociologically, this is what conversion practices are. They target LGBT+ identities specifically because those identities are considered problematic. So conversion practices are inherently abusive, as the Government recognise, but they are not just abuse—

Q11 Sir Jeremy Wright: Forgive me, but the Government do not recognise that all conversion practices are abusive, because do they not make a distinction between those that are and those that are not in the framing of this legislation?

Professor Trispiotis: You are absolutely right, but I have a slight correction. The opening sentence of the Government’s equality impact assessment is: “The government’s position is that conversion practices are abuse.” So there is an inconsistency there between the Government’s own equality impact assessment and the distinction between abusive and non-abusive conversion practices that the text of the Bill currently draws.

Q12 Sir Jeremy Wright: Would it be your recommendation that there should be no such distinction? Does it follow from what you said a moment ago that you would seek to add a discriminatory motivation clause or some language that reflects that motivation in this process?

Professor Trispiotis: Yes—it is a discriminatory presumption. It would not be something for the prosecution to prove on top of the other elements of the conduct, but—

Q13 Sir Jeremy Wright: A rebuttable presumption or a non-rebuttable presumption?

Professor Trispiotis: I think the model that should be used is the Equality Act 2010 model for direct discrimination, given that we are talking about a case of discriminatory abuse. With regards to abusive conversion practices, the distinction between abusive and non-abusive conversion practices is very problematic. Again, it does not appear in any other legislation in this area in any other countries that have banned these practices, or in any international recommendations. There is a lot of academic literature and there are credible sources that show for specific legal reasons why conversion practices are always inherently abusive because of how they violate the human dignity of LGBT+ people. The distinction between abusive and non-abusive conversion practices suggests that some of those practices might be non-abusive and therefore might be legitimate and might continue. That is expressively harmful for LGBT+ people.

In terms of criminal law, as a lawyer, I think that it is important to recognise that, if this distinction is omitted by the Bill, it does not mean that the criminal threshold is going to get lower. Abusive conversion practices—the way in which “abusive” is described at the moment in the Bill and in the explanatory notes—includes a wide range of practices, including those that are threatening, intimidating, coercive and controlling. It is very hard—I found it impossible—to find an example of a conversion practice from the Galop report or from other survivor reports that would not meet that threshold of being abusive. All of them are frightening and controlling, and they all involve an unequal power relationship between an LGBT+ person and someone who presents themselves as an “enlightened converter”. That is the language of the UN report. All those practices would, by definition, reach that first threshold, so it does not even help us to restrict the scope of the offence. It is redundant and symbolically harmful, this first threshold, in my opinion.

Q14 Naz Shah: I declare my interest in that I met with Stephen this week, which was really helpful—thank you.

To follow on from the identity point that you have just made, professor—this may also apply to Stonewall—the draft Bill treats sexuality and transgender identity identically. Stonewall works on both those areas. Are there any relevant differences between them that the Committee should consider?

Stephen Kiely: The way the drafters have dealt with sexual orientation is to use the definition in the Equality Act, which is standard precedent in this area. Similarly, the drafters have used the definition of transgender identity from the Sentencing Act 2020, which derives from the Equality Act. We have seen from how the Bill is drafted that it can now include asexuality in the definition of sexual orientation, which is a very positive step from the Government’s own national LGBT survey—that people who are asexual experience conversion practices. Similarly, in the definition of transgender identity they have included non-binary identities as well. It is good to see that; it is important that legislation and law keeps pace with evolving identities. In the explanatory notes, the Government have explained why they can do that: it does not change the definition of gender reassignment in the Equality Act, so it makes sense why it is there.

Q15 Naz Shah: Just to build on that, the draft Bill defines “conversion practices” in a way that covers causing people to believe that they do not have a sexuality or gender identity but also that they do. Are you at all concerned that the services that support people to come out as gay or transgender might potentially be targeted in the scope of this Bill?

Stephen Kiely: That is a really good question. We are getting into the healthcare section of the Bill there, which is a really important part of it and one that we want to discuss a lot. It would be good if the Committee took evidence from regulators and clinical bodies. This is an area of concern for us; there is a healthcare clause in here that is quite broad, but it is doing a certain purpose. That is, first, to protect clinicians who offer legitimate, evidence-based care, so they are free to do that, from risk of prosecution. But we have an issue with the definition, which is very broad.

The definition of a healthcare professional is both regulated and unregulated; likewise, the services that can be provided are very broad. The reason we have an issue with that is that we are hearing a lot of evidence from survivors who have experienced conversion practices from organisations that have rebranded as therapists or pseudo-counsellors, etc., which are now going to be exempt from conversion practice, because of how it is drafted.

We have a simple solution—well, perhaps not simple—to this section, which it would be good to discuss. We would add a third section to the healthcare clause, which would mean that anyone who is engaging in this area would have to be regulated by a statutory professional regulatory body or listed on a register held by an organisation accredited by the Professional Standards Authority. So the first section would cover your psychiatrists and clinical psychologists, and the second section would cover your counsellors and psychotherapists, essentially. That would be a really important clause to add. First, it would mean that everyone who is regulated would now come under the exemption or the bar—but, equally, anyone who is outside of that and was practising could then be prosecuted.

Q16 Lord Hope of Craighead: Good afternoon. I should say that I have no interests to declare. Your point encourages me to ask you about the position in Northern Ireland. I do not know whether any of you are familiar with the draft Bill in Northern Ireland, but what it contains a provision saying that the Act “does not apply in respect of therapy provided or carried out…by an individual registered by, or subject to regulation by” various regulated bodies, and “in accordance with the standards and code of ethics of that regulatory body that apply at the time the therapy is carried out”. If we had something of that kind written into the Bill, would that satisfy you? Is it too narrow or too broad, or what?

Stephen Kiely: I think that would satisfy—although I am interested in the views of everyone else. But I think we are broadly in the same place. We need a clause that protects people who are regulated by statute and people who are regulated by accredited bodies done by the Professional Standards Authority. We need a kind of dual regulation there.

Q17 Lord Hope of Craighead: There are two things—the regulator itself and then the fact that you are applying the standards that the regulatory body expects of you.

Stephen Kiely: If you were a counsellor, you would be part of the British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy, and you would be governed by their codes of conduct or codes of ethics, which outlaw—not outlaw but are against—conversion practices.

Q18 Lord Hope of Craighead: Professor, is the Northern Ireland example familiar to you? Is it in accordance with what other countries are doing?

Professor Trispiotis: It is familiar to me and in accordance with what other countries have done. All countries have a provision that aims to reassure legitimate therapy providers—I shall come to that in a second, but I want to answer your question directly. Yes, something like that would be essential to be added to the Bill. The memorandum of understanding on conversion therapy in the UK is signed up to by a group of organisations and regulators who have come together; they include the NHS, the BACP and the Royal College of General Practitioners, some of the leading healthcare bodies of this country. Their preference would be for a provision that is inserted that would apply only to professionals who are regulated by statutory professional regulatory bodies or, as Stephen said, are listed on a register held by an organisation accredited by the PSA. So it is very similar to what you are saying.

Other countries have done this slightly differently: all countries that have banned conversion practices so far have a provision that aims to reassure healthcare professionals that they have the space they need to make their professional judgments in this area. For example, New Zealand, Canada and Victoria—I can think of many examples—include in their legislation a short indicative list of practices that are not conversion practices. That list includes support and encouragement in an open-ended way, and gender transition processes. These are indicative lists of practices that these jurisdictions have inserted in their legislation so that they can reassure healthcare providers that they are not captured by the ban.

Q19 Lord Hope of Craighead: Does that list apply to parents and teachers in schools or is it tied to healthcare providers in particular?

Professor Trispiotis: It depends. Some jurisdictions have framed this provision more broadly as a list of practices that are not and should not be considered conversion practices. Other countries, such as New Zealand, are more specific about healthcare providers.

Q20 Lord Hope of Craighead: From looking at how the Bill before us is worded, would that have a chilling effect across the healthcare sector? Is that right? We have to decide whether to change what is there and that is a factor that we need to consider.

Professor Trispiotis: In my opinion, legitimate healthcare is consistent with the Bill, which would not have a chilling effect on healthcare. As it stands, the Bill would provide insufficient protection for LGBT+ people from conversion practices. That is my position on this.

Q21 Lord Hope of Craighead: Really, the aim is specifically to avoid people taking advantage of this who should not be able to because they are not complying with proper practice and are not regulated.

Professor Trispiotis: Exactly, and there would also be no mechanisms of accountability if non-regulated professionals were included in the provision.

Kai O’Doherty: May I add something very briefly on some examples of actual practice that needs to be prevented, which we have come across? People have gone to doctors who have insisted that their asexuality is a medical condition that needs to be fixed. People have gone to counsellors who have said that they need to be turned straight. Parents have taken young people to psychologists to “fix” their gender identity. The definition of conversion practices would cover those cases.

Therefore, the exemption for healthcare does not need to be this broad. This idea that healthcare providers would be completely, carte-blanche immune from being accused of conversion practices has really and especially worried trans parts of our communities, because we know that they happen in pseudo-healthcare contexts. So I urge the Committee not to make that protection overly broad and to follow the experts in the memorandum of understanding.

Q23 Baroness Brown of Silvertown: Is prayer with a person who is struggling with identity a conversion practice? Where is the line drawn?

Most criminal offences require that you want to hurt someone or that you are reckless enough to hurt. This Bill does not say that.

If a mother says, “I want you to love your body,” she is intending the change, so that is ticked. The Bill then says that the behaviour has to be abusive. Helen Joyce: May I flesh out that example? Let us make it very concrete and use the example of an autistic child. Imagine an 11-year-old autistic child who identifies as non-binary. It is urgent for the mother to say—it is not just something that she might want to say but something that she must say to fulfil her parental duties—that this girl is still a girl. She is female. You sometimes do things that are really not ideal as a parent. Some of them were directly told to us by parents and professionals and some were examples that therapists passed on to us, which they heard in their practice from parents.

The important thing to remember is that the Bill makes no distinction whatever between adults and children. The words “adult”, “child”, “parent” and “family” do not appear in it. It is simply inappropriate to say that the way in which you act towards a child, which might be to enforce a family rule or to take them away from bad influences, can be compared with coercive control. If my husband takes my phone away from me because he does not want me contacting people, that is coercive control. If I do that to my child, that may be responsible parenting.

Dennis Kavanagh: This is a really difficult concern because in theory, under the Bill—because you do not require any sort of malicious intent, tortious intent or anything like that—you could, as a parent, be 100% motivated by welfare and still trigger the Bill if you satisfied “abusive” later on. There is a particular problem with the word “abusive” in the context of the gender debate, and it is this: that word means different things to different people. Some people say that, if you do not use the pronouns that someone asks for, that in and of itself is rude and abusive. It does not have a defence of reasonableness—that you thought that your actions were reasonable—or a defence that you were acting in the best interests of the other person. It does not have a defence that you were acting to prevent harm being done to the other person. This brings me to answering your question: it does not have a defence that your actions were to fulfil statutory obligations. All those things do appear in other criminal statutes.

There are some things that come up in the context of the Equality Act, and the European convention as well, that require people, or at least allow them, to recognise other people’s sex and to act on that. There are two examples: schools—where they have to safeguard and obey the keeping children safe in education law—and workplaces, where in certain situations employees have the right to single-sex spaces. It is not just a right but a duty in those situations to act upon somebody’s sex.

I know that is a rather detailed and specific example, but it is a place where this law butts hard up against other people’s rights: their rights to freedom of belief, to freedom of expression and, in some circumstances, to same-sex spaces—and, more broadly, to enjoy all their other rights, as a worker or at a school.

Dennis Kavanagh: Just on the convention, as quickly as I possibly can, for article 7, you have to be able to understand law. It has to be comprehensible.
